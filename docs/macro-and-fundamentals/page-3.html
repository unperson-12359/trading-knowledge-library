    Reverse stock split reviewed #

    A corporate action that consolidates shares - exchanging many old shares for one new share - to raise the per-share price proportionally while leaving market capitalization unchanged. It is most common among small or distressed companies trying to meet exchange minimum-price rules or attract institutional investors.
    Intuition: It is the same pie cut into fewer, bigger slices: economically neutral by itself, but usually a symptom that the share price fell low enough to become a problem.
    Mechanics: In a 1-for-N reverse split each holder receives one share per N owned and the price multiplies by N; fractional shares are typically cashed out. Exchanges enforce minimum bid prices, and FINRA processes OTC reverse splits as corporate actions after 10 days' advance notice, posting them to the OTC Daily List.
    Failure modes: Reverse splits often precede further decline because the underlying distress is untouched; they are strongly associated with low-priced, high-risk OTC names, and repeated reverse splits can trap holders in cascading dilution.
    Misconceptions: A higher post-split price does not make the company more valuable or safer - FINRA stresses that a split does nothing to change the value of a company - and institutional interest rarely follows if the fundamentals do not improve.
    Example: FINRA's illustration: 5,000 shares at $0.10 (a $500 position) become 25 shares at $20 in a 200-for-1 reverse split - the same $500 investment in fewer shares.
    Post-split price = Pre-split price x N; Shares after = Shares before / N (for a 1-for-N split).
    Aliases: Reverse split, Share consolidation

    Cash dividend reviewed #

    A distribution of a portion of a company's profits to shareholders in cash, typically declared by the board on a fixed schedule such as quarterly. Investor.gov defines a dividend as a portion of a company's profit paid to shareholders, with unscheduled payments known as special dividends.
    Intuition: It is the most direct way a company hands owners their share of the profits: cash in the account rather than a claim on future growth.
    Mechanics: The board declares an amount per share along with a record date and a payable date; entitlement is determined by the ex-dividend date, and on that date the stock's price typically drops by roughly the dividend amount because the cash leaving the company reduces its value.
    Failure modes: Dividends can be cut in downturns - a high stated yield may be weeks from disappearing - and funding dividends with debt or while free cash flow is negative is unsustainable.
    Misconceptions: A dividend is not free income: the share price mechanically adjusts down on the ex-date, so shareholder wealth is roughly unchanged before taxes, which often make dividends less efficient than buybacks.
    Example: Per Investor.gov's corporate-action illustration, if company ABC's board approves a $2 cash dividend, the stock's price on the ex-dividend date reflects a drop of about $2 from its previous close.
    Total dividend income = Dividend per share x Shares held; Dividend yield = Annual dividends per share / Price per share.
    Aliases: Dividend, Regular dividend, Special dividend

    Ex-dividend date reviewed #

    The first trading day on which a stock trades without the right to its declared dividend: buy on or after the ex-date and the seller, not you, receives the payment. It is set by stock-exchange rules, normally on the record date or one business day before if the record date is not a business day.
    Intuition: Because trades take a day to settle, the ex-date is the practical cutoff: it answers 'how late can I buy and still get the dividend?'
    Mechanics: Once a company sets the record date, the exchange fixes the ex-date so that only trades settling by the record date qualify, and the stock typically opens lower by about the dividend amount on the ex-date. For dividends of 25% or more of the stock's value, special rules defer the ex-date until one business day after payment.
    Failure modes: Dividend-capture strategies - buy before the ex-date, sell on it - fail in practice because the price drops by roughly the dividend and taxes on short-term trades erase any edge; large special dividends follow different ex-date rules that catch traders off guard.
    Misconceptions: You do not need to own the stock on the record date itself - owning it before the ex-date so the trade settles in time is what counts, and selling on the ex-date still entitles you to the dividend.
    Example: Investor.gov's example: declaration March 2, record date Monday March 16, ex-date March 16 - anyone buying on March 16 or later misses the March 17 payment.
    Aliases: Ex-date, Ex-dividend

    Record date reviewed #

    The date set by a company's board on which a shareholder must appear on the company's books to receive a declared dividend; shareholders of record are also the ones sent proxy statements, financial reports, and other corporate information.
    Intuition: It is the company's administrative snapshot: whoever the transfer records show as owner on this date gets the dividend.
    Mechanics: The board fixes the record date when it declares the dividend. Because trades settle one business day later (T+1), the exchange sets the ex-dividend date on the record date - or the prior business day if the record date falls on a weekend or holiday - making the ex-date, not the record date, the effective trading cutoff.
    Failure modes: Confusing the record date with the ex-date causes mistimed trades; with stock dividends and spin-offs the ex-date falls after payment, so selling too early obligates the seller to deliver the extra shares via a due bill.
    Misconceptions: Buying on the record date does not get you the dividend - settlement timing means you had to buy before the ex-dividend date, which is usually the same day as the record date or earlier.
    Example: Investor.gov's example: when the record date falls on Sunday March 15, the ex-date moves to Friday March 13, so anyone buying on or after that Friday misses the dividend.
    Aliases: Date of record

    Rights offering reviewed #

    An issue giving existing shareholders the right - but not the obligation - to buy additional shares pro rata to their holdings at a discount to the market price within a set window, usually 16 to 30 days. The rights are often transferable and tradable like ordinary shares until they expire.
    Intuition: It is a loyalty-preserving capital raise: current owners get first claim on new shares cheaply, and those who do not want in can sell the right itself.
    Mechanics: The company distributes rights in proportion to holdings; holders may exercise at the subscription price, sell the rights if renounceable, or let them lapse. In an insured or standby offering an investment bank backstops unexercised rights to guarantee the capital target; direct offerings have no backstop.
    Failure modes: Dilution falls on anyone who neither exercises nor sells the rights; offerings often signal distress because the firm could not raise money conventionally, and thin rights trading makes their price erratic.
    Misconceptions: The discount is not free money: the share price adjusts down when the stock goes ex-rights to reflect the dilution, so exercising is roughly economically neutral before weighing what the capital raise signals.
    Example: A holder of 100 shares receives rights to buy 10 new shares at $8 versus a $10 market price; exercising costs $80, and the theoretical ex-rights price drops to about $9.82.
    Theoretical ex-rights price = (Old shares x Price + New shares x Subscription price) / Total shares after issue.
    Aliases: Rights issue, Subscription rights

    Spin-off reviewed #

    A corporate action in which a company separates a division into an independent company by distributing new shares in it to existing shareholders, generally pro rata. Holders end up owning shares in both the parent and the spun-off entity.
    Intuition: A spin-off unbundles a conglomerate so each business can be managed - and valued - on its own merits.
    Mechanics: The parent distributes the subsidiary's shares to its holders as a stock dividend, often structured to be tax-free; the new shares may first be offered through a rights issue to existing shareholders before being offered to new investors. On distribution the parent's price drops by the value of what was separated.
    Failure modes: Spin-offs can load debt or liabilities onto the smaller entity, both stocks often sell off initially as index funds and disinterested holders exit, and separation costs can exceed the hoped-for sum-of-the-parts uplift.
    Misconceptions: Receiving spin-off shares is not a windfall: the parent's price falls by the value distributed, just as with a dividend, and spin-offs are not guaranteed to unlock value.
    Example: When eBay spun off PayPal in 2015, each eBay shareholder received one PayPal share per eBay share held; the two separately priced companies together approximated eBay's prior market value.
    Aliases: Spinoff, Demerger, Corporate spin-off

    Merger arbitrage reviewed #

    An event-driven strategy that seeks to profit from the spread between a takeover target's trading price and the announced deal price: typically buying the target's stock - and, in stock-for-stock deals, shorting the acquirer's - to capture the discount that reflects the risk the deal fails. Also called risk arbitrage.
    Intuition: After a $50-per-share cash bid the target might trade at $48; the arbitrageur buys the $2 gap, earning it if the deal closes and losing heavily if it breaks.
    Mechanics: In a cash merger the arbitrageur simply goes long the target; in a stock-for-stock merger they buy the target and short the acquirer in the exchange ratio, covering the short with the converted shares at closing. Returns annualize the spread over the expected time to completion, and positions are sized around deal-break risk from regulators, financing, or shareholder votes.
    Failure modes: The payoff is asymmetric - many small wins against occasional deal-break collapses of 20-40%; regulatory blocks and financing failures cluster in stress periods, so the strategy correlates with equities exactly when diversification is needed most.
    Misconceptions: It is not risk-free 'arbitrage' in the textbook sense: the spread is compensation for genuine completion risk, and a target trading below the offer price is normal, not a pricing error.
    Example: An acquirer bids $50 cash for a target that traded at $40 pre-deal; the stock jumps to $48. Buying at $48 yields a 4.2% gross spread if the deal closes in four months, but a broken deal can send the stock back toward $40.
    Gross spread (%) = (Offer price - Target price) / Target price; annualized return is approximately the spread x (365 / days to close).
    Aliases: Risk arbitrage, Risk arb, Deal arbitrage
