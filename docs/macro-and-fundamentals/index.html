    Gross domestic product reviewed #

    The total value of the final goods and services produced within a country during a given period. In the United States it is estimated quarterly by the Bureau of Economic Analysis as the signature measure of the National Income and Product Accounts.
    Intuition: GDP is the economy's scoreboard: it tells you how much the country actually produced in a quarter or year, and its growth rate is the standard gauge of whether the economy is expanding or contracting.
    Mechanics: BEA sums spending on final goods and services (consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports), counting only final output to avoid double-counting intermediates. Each quarter is estimated three times (advance, second, third) as more source data arrive, and figures are seasonally adjusted and reported at annual rates.
    Failure modes: GDP is revised repeatedly, so the first estimate traders react to can change materially; it is also backward-looking, ignores non-market activity and income distribution, and annualized quarterly rates can exaggerate one-off swings.
    Misconceptions: GDP does not measure well-being or total wealth, and a high GDP level does not imply strong growth; what markets trade on is the growth rate relative to expectations, not the level.
    Example: When BEA reported Q1 2020 GDP falling at a 5.0% annual rate, it confirmed the pandemic contraction; traders compared each release against consensus to reprice Fed policy expectations.
    GDP = C + I + G + (X - M), where C = personal consumption expenditures, I = gross private domestic investment, G = government consumption and investment, X = exports, M = imports (expenditure approach).
    Aliases: GDP

    Real GDP reviewed #

    Gross domestic product adjusted to remove the effects of price changes, so that output in different periods can be compared in constant purchasing-power terms. When media report 'GDP growth' they almost always mean the percent change in real GDP.
    Intuition: Real GDP answers: did the country actually produce more stuff, or did prices just go up? It strips out inflation so growth reflects volume, not price changes.
    Mechanics: BEA deflates each component of nominal spending using detailed price indexes (producing chained-dollar estimates), so real GDP changes reflect changes in quantities produced. Growth rates are computed from these chained series, seasonally adjusted at annual rates.
    Failure modes: Deflation choices and base-year/index updates can revise history; real GDP can rise while living standards for typical households stagnate if gains accrue narrowly or population grows faster.
    Misconceptions: Real GDP is not 'GDP with inflation subtracted at the CPI'; BEA uses its own chain-type price indexes, so the implied GDP deflator differs from CPI inflation.
    Example: If nominal GDP rises 5% but prices rise 3%, real GDP rises roughly 2% — the growth that economists and the Fed treat as actual output growth.
    Real GDP = Nominal GDP / GDP price index x 100 (conceptually); in practice BEA uses chained (Fisher) quantity indexes rather than a single deflator.
    Aliases: Chained-dollar GDP, Constant-dollar GDP
    Related:

    Nominal GDP reviewed #

    Gross domestic product measured at the market prices prevailing during the period being measured, without adjustment for inflation. It moves with both the quantity of output and the price level.
    Intuition: Nominal GDP is the raw dollar size of the economy; it grows even if the country produces the same amount, simply because everything costs more.
    Mechanics: BEA values current-period final output at current-period prices (current-dollar estimates). Comparing nominal GDP across periods mixes volume and price changes, so analysts deflate it to obtain real GDP.
    Failure modes: In high-inflation episodes nominal GDP can look strong while real output stagnates; cross-period or cross-country comparisons in nominal terms conflate price-level differences with size differences.
    Misconceptions: Nominal GDP is not 'wrong' or useless — it is the right denominator for debt-to-GDP and tax-revenue ratios, since debts and revenues are nominal; it is only misleading when used to infer real growth.
    Example: U.S. nominal GDP grew briskly in 2021-2022, but a large share of that increase was price inflation, which is why real GDP grew much more slowly over the same period.
    Nominal GDP = sum over goods i of (P_i,t x Q_i,t), where P_i,t and Q_i,t are current-period prices and quantities of final goods and services.
    Aliases: Current-dollar GDP
    Related:

    Inflation reviewed #

    A sustained increase in the general level of prices for goods and services over time, equivalently a decline in the purchasing power of money. In the U.S. it is most commonly measured by the 12-month percent change in the Consumer Price Index or the PCE price index.
    Intuition: Inflation means your dollar buys less this year than last year; central banks target a low, stable rate (the Fed aims for 2%) because both very high inflation and deflation damage the economy.
    Mechanics: Statistical agencies track a fixed basket of consumer goods and services and compute the average price change over time; the 12-month percent change of the index is the headline inflation rate. Persistent inflation is ultimately associated with money supply growing faster than output, though shocks (energy, supply chains) drive short-run swings.
    Failure modes: Headline rates are dominated by volatile food and energy swings; substitution, quality change, and housing measurement make any single index an approximation, and base effects can make year-over-year rates jump mechanically.
    Misconceptions: A fall in the inflation rate does not mean prices are falling (that is deflation) — it means prices are rising more slowly; also, one expensive item is not inflation, which refers to the general price level.
    Example: U.S. CPI inflation peaked at 9.1% year-over-year in June 2022, prompting the Federal Reserve's 2022-2023 rate-hiking cycle.
    Inflation rate (12-month) = (CPI_t / CPI_{t-12} - 1) x 100, where CPI_t is the consumer price index in month t.

    Consumer price index reviewed #

    A measure of the average change over time in the prices paid by urban consumers for a market basket of consumer goods and services, published monthly by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. The headline series is the CPI for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U).
    Intuition: The CPI is a giant shopping basket whose total cost is tracked every month; how fast that basket's cost rises is the most-watched U.S. inflation reading.
    Mechanics: BLS collects prices for thousands of items across urban areas, weights them by consumer spending patterns from the Consumer Expenditure Surveys, and aggregates them into an index (reference base 1982-84=100). Monthly releases report seasonally adjusted month-over-month and unadjusted 12-month changes, including the 'all items less food and energy' core series.
    Failure modes: CPI covers only urban consumers' out-of-pocket spending, uses owners' equivalent rent for housing, and embeds substitution and quality-adjustment assumptions, so it can diverge from any household's lived inflation and from the Fed's preferred PCE measure.
    Misconceptions: The CPI is not a cost-of-living index in the strict sense and does not measure price levels across cities; a higher index in one period means prices rose, not that every item did.
    Example: In June 2026, BLS reported CPI-U fell 0.4% month over month (seasonally adjusted) while rising 3.5% over 12 months — the 12-month figure is the headline inflation rate.
    Percent change = (Index_t / Index_{t-1} - 1) x 100; the index itself is a weighted average of item-level price relatives with expenditure weights.
    Aliases: CPI, CPI-U

    Producer price index reviewed #

    A family of indexes published monthly by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics measuring the average change over time in selling prices received by domestic producers of goods and services. It measures price change from the seller's perspective, in contrast to the CPI's purchaser perspective.
    Intuition: PPI tracks what producers charge at the factory gate or for their services; because it captures price pressure before the retail level, traders watch it as a possible early signal of consumer inflation.
    Mechanics: BLS surveys over 16,000 establishments for roughly 64,000 price quotes per month and publishes indexes by industry, by commodity, and by the Final Demand-Intermediate Demand (FD-ID) system, which groups goods, services, and construction by buyer type and processing stage. Weights come from value-of-shipments data in the economic censuses.
    Failure modes: PPI excludes most imports and part of the service sector, margins between producer and consumer prices vary, and pass-through to CPI is incomplete and lagged, so PPI spikes do not map one-for-one into consumer inflation.
    Misconceptions: PPI is not a direct predictor of next month's CPI; sellers' and purchasers' prices differ due to taxes, subsidies, and distribution costs, and many services in CPI have no PPI counterpart.
    Example: When the PPI for final demand jumped in early 2021-2022 amid supply bottlenecks, analysts read it as pipeline pressure that preceded the broader CPI surge.
    PPI percent change = (PPI_t / PPI_{t-1} - 1) x 100 for a given index; indexes are weighted averages of establishment price relatives.
    Aliases: PPI

    Core inflation reviewed #

    An inflation measure that excludes the volatile food and energy components, intended to reveal the underlying, persistent trend in prices. In the U.S. the standard readings are 'all items less food and energy' in the CPI and the core PCE price index.
    Intuition: Gasoline and grocery prices whipsaw month to month; stripping them out gives a cleaner read on whether inflation is really embedded in the economy, which is what central banks care about.
    Mechanics: Statistical agencies compute the same price index over the basket minus food and energy items and report its monthly and 12-month changes alongside headline figures. The concept of excluding food and energy originated in the 1970s when oil shocks made headline inflation noisy; the Fed's 2% target is defined on PCE, with core PCE watched as the trend gauge.
    Failure modes: Food and energy shocks can persist and pass through into core (second-round effects), so core can understate true inflation pressure for long stretches; shelter measurement lags also distort core readings.
    Misconceptions: Core inflation is not a claim that food and energy 'don't matter' to households — it is a signal-extraction device for policy, not a measure of the cost of living.
    Example: In June 2026, headline CPI rose 3.5% over 12 months while the index for all items less food and energy rose 2.6%, showing energy drove much of the headline overshoot.
    Core inflation rate = (CoreIndex_t / CoreIndex_{t-12} - 1) x 100, where the core index is the CPI (or PCE index) excluding food and energy components.
    Aliases: Core CPI, Core PCE, Inflation excluding food and energy

    Deflation reviewed #

    A sustained general decline in the prices of goods and services, equivalently an increase in the purchasing power of money. It is typically associated with contracting money and credit or collapsing aggregate demand and is considered more dangerous than moderate inflation because it raises the real burden of debt and encourages delayed spending.
    Intuition: When prices fall broadly, cash gains value just by sitting still, so consumers postpone purchases and borrowers must repay debts with more valuable money — a self-reinforcing drag on the economy.
    Mechanics: Deflation shows up as a negative 12-month change in a price index (e.g., CPI below zero). Causes include falls in aggregate demand, contractions in money and credit (as in the 1930s U.S. bank failures), or strong productivity gains; policymakers counter it with rate cuts, asset purchases, and fiscal stimulus.
    Failure modes: As a concept it can mislead when driven by productivity or technology (good deflation) rather than demand collapse; debt-deflation dynamics described by Fisher apply mainly when falling prices coincide with high leverage.
    Misconceptions: Cheaper prices are not always good: economy-wide deflation raises real debt burdens and real interest rates even at zero nominal rates, which is why central banks fight it as hard as inflation.
    Example: The U.S. last experienced significant deflation in the early 1930s after widespread bank failures shrank the money supply; Japan suffered prolonged deflation in the 1990s.
    Deflation occurs when (CPI_t / CPI_{t-12} - 1) < 0, i.e., a negative inflation rate.

    Disinflation reviewed #

    A decrease in the rate of inflation — prices are still rising, but at a slower pace. It is distinct from deflation, which is an actual decline in the price level.
    Intuition: Disinflation is easing off the accelerator, not going in reverse: inflation falling from 9% to 3% is disinflation even though everything still costs more than before.
    Mechanics: Measured as a decline in the 12-month percent change of a price index while it remains positive. It typically follows tighter monetary policy (higher interest rates) or the fading of supply shocks; the 1980s Volcker disinflation and the 2023 cooldown from the June 2022 CPI peak of 9.1% are canonical episodes.
    Failure modes: Disinflation driven by collapsing demand can precede recession or slide into deflation; base effects can also make the inflation rate fall mechanically even as monthly price gains stay firm.
    Misconceptions: Disinflation does not mean prices are coming down — confusing it with deflation leads to wrong expectations about purchasing power and policy.
    Example: After CPI inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022, the U.S. experienced disinflation through 2023 as the Fed's rate hikes slowed price increases without prices falling.
    Disinflation: inflation_t < inflation_{t-1} with inflation_t > 0, e.g., CPI 12-month change falling from 3% to 2%.

    Stagflation reviewed #

    The simultaneous occurrence of high or rising inflation with stagnant output and elevated unemployment — a combination that contradicts the simple Phillips-curve trade-off. The term is identified with the 1970s U.S. experience, when inflation and unemployment rose together after the oil shocks.
    Intuition: Stagflation is the worst of both worlds: the economy stops growing and jobs disappear while prices keep climbing, leaving policymakers with no painless option.
    Mechanics: It typically arises when adverse supply shocks (e.g., the 1973 and 1979 oil crises) raise costs and prices while reducing output, and monetary accommodation of those shocks entrenches inflation expectations. Fighting unemployment with stimulus worsens inflation; fighting inflation with tightening worsens unemployment — the 1970s dilemma that ended only with the Volcker tightening of 1979-82.
    Failure modes: The label is often misapplied to any period of above-target inflation with mediocre growth; true stagflation requires sustained inflation together with economic stagnation, and the 1970s lesson (unstable Phillips trade-off) may not generalize to anchored-expectation regimes.
    Misconceptions: Stagflation is not simply 'high inflation' or 'a recession'; its defining feature is the coexistence of both, which standard demand-management policy cannot fix from one side.
    Example: By summer 1980, U.S. inflation was near 14.5% while unemployment exceeded 7.5% — the textbook stagflation that followed a decade of oil shocks and accommodative policy.

    Unemployment rate reviewed #

    The percentage of the civilian labor force that is jobless, actively seeking work, and available to take a job. In the United States it is estimated monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics from the Current Population Survey of about 60,000 households and published in The Employment Situation report.
    Intuition: It is the best-known single gauge of labor-market slack: a low rate means most people who want work have it, while a rising rate signals a weakening economy.
    Mechanics: BLS divides the number of unemployed - people with no job who actively searched in the prior four weeks and are available for work, plus those awaiting recall from layoff - by the labor force (employed plus unemployed). Data come from live Census Bureau interviews covering the week containing the 12th of the month, are weighted to population estimates, and are seasonally adjusted.
    Failure modes: It excludes discouraged workers who stopped searching, so slack is understated when participation falls; the headline U-3 rate is also revised and can diverge from broader underutilization measures like U-6 during stress.
    Misconceptions: It is not derived from unemployment-insurance claims - BLS notes UI records cover only about one-third of the unemployed - and it counts as unemployed only people actively searching or awaiting recall, not everyone without a job.
    Example: In April 2020 the U.S. unemployment rate jumped from 4.4% in March to 14.7%, the highest in the series' history, as pandemic lockdowns idled over 20 million workers.
    Unemployment rate = (Number unemployed / Civilian labor force) x 100, where the labor force = employed + unemployed.
    Aliases: U-3 unemployment rate, U3

    Nonfarm payrolls reviewed #

    The monthly change in the number of workers on U.S. nonfarm business and government payrolls, estimated by the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Current Employment Statistics (CES) survey of establishments. Released with the unemployment rate in The Employment Situation, it is the first major hard-data read on the U.S. economy each month.
    Intuition: It answers 'how many jobs did employers actually add last month' from the business side, making it the market's favorite single labor-market number.
    Mechanics: CES surveys about 119,000 businesses and government agencies covering roughly 622,000 worksites, using payroll records for the pay period including the 12th of the month. Estimates are seasonally adjusted, revised in each of the next two releases, and benchmarked annually to near-universe counts from unemployment-insurance tax records, with a birth-death model capturing new firms.
    Failure modes: First prints are revised twice and re-benchmarked annually (the 2025 benchmark cut about 862,000 jobs from 2025 estimates), and strikes or severe weather can distort a single month by hundreds of thousands.
    Misconceptions: It counts jobs, not people - a worker with two payroll jobs appears twice - and it excludes farm workers, the self-employed, and unpaid family workers, so it can diverge from the household-survey employment count.
    Example: In April 2020 nonfarm payrolls fell by roughly 20.5 million, the largest one-month drop on record; the June 2020 rebound of about 4.8 million was the largest gain.
    Net payroll change = Total nonfarm employment(t) - Total nonfarm employment(t-1), in thousands of jobs.
    Aliases: NFP, Non-farm payrolls, Payroll employment, Establishment survey employment

    Labor-force participation rate reviewed #

    The share of the U.S. civilian noninstitutional population aged 16 and over that is either working or actively seeking work. It is computed monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics from the Current Population Survey.
    Intuition: It measures how much of the working-age population is actually engaged in the labor market, separating 'no job but looking' from 'out of the labor force entirely.'
    Mechanics: BLS divides the civilian labor force (employed plus unemployed) by the civilian noninstitutional population 16 and older, a base that excludes active-duty military and people in institutions such as prisons and nursing homes.
    Failure modes: Demographics - aging baby boomers retiring - drive slow structural declines easily mistaken for cyclical weakness, and the unemployment rate can fall for the 'wrong' reason when participation drops.
    Misconceptions: A falling participation rate does not necessarily mean people 'gave up looking' - retirement, schooling, and caregiving account for most non-participation, with discouraged workers only a small subset of those out of the labor force.
    Example: U.S. participation peaked near 67.3% in early 2000, drifted down to about 62.4% by late 2015, and collapsed to 60.2% in April 2020 during pandemic lockdowns.
    LFPR = (Civilian labor force / Civilian noninstitutional population 16+) x 100.
    Aliases: LFPR, Participation rate
    Related:
    • Unemployment rate
    • Nonfarm payrolls
    • Consumer confidence
    Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, How the Government Measures Unemployment — Section: What are the unemployment rate, labor force participation rate, and employment-population ratio?

    Purchasing managers index reviewed #

    A monthly diffusion index compiled from surveys of purchasing and supply executives that summarizes whether business conditions in manufacturing or services are expanding or contracting relative to the prior month. The best-known U.S. versions are ISM's Manufacturing PMI and Services PMI (formerly the Report On Business).
    Intuition: Because purchasing managers feel order flow before it shows up in output statistics, the PMI is one of the earliest monthly reads on the economy's direction.
    Mechanics: Respondents report whether activity in areas such as new orders, production, employment, supplier deliveries, and inventories is better, unchanged, or worse than the prior month; ISM's Manufacturing PMI is a composite of five equally weighted diffusion indexes, seasonally adjusted, with a panel structured to mirror each industry's contribution to GDP. Readings above 50 indicate expansion and below 50 contraction.
    Failure modes: It measures the breadth of change, not its magnitude, so a move from 52 to 53 says little about the size of growth; survey sentiment can decouple from hard output data for months.
    Misconceptions: A reading below 50 does not mean the whole economy is in recession - it means the surveyed sector contracted versus the prior month, and ISM notes overall GDP can keep growing with manufacturing PMI readings in the low-to-mid 40s.
    Example: ISM releases the Manufacturing PMI at 10:00 a.m. ET on the first business day of each month and the Services PMI on the third; a 49.0 manufacturing print signals a slight monthly contraction in that sector.
    Diffusion index = % reporting improvement + 0.5 x % reporting no change; the Manufacturing PMI is the equal-weighted average of five such indexes (new orders, production, employment, supplier deliveries, inventories).
    Aliases: PMI, ISM Manufacturing Index, ISM, Purchasing Managers Index
    Related:
    • Industrial production
    • Capacity utilization
    • Gross domestic product
    • Economic surprise index
    Source: Institute for Supply Management, ISM PMI Reports — ISM PMI Reports overview

    Retail sales reviewed #

    The monthly estimate of receipts at U.S. retail and food-services establishments, published by the Census Bureau through the Advance Monthly Retail Trade Survey (MARTS) and the Monthly Retail Trade Survey (MRTS). It is the earliest broad indicator of consumer spending on goods.
    Intuition: Because consumer spending is the largest component of GDP, the retail-sales report is a fast proxy for the health of household demand.
    Mechanics: The Census Bureau samples retail firms from its business register and collects sales for the reference month; an advance estimate from early respondents is released about two weeks after month-end and revised in the full MRTS, with all headline figures seasonally adjusted and reported in nominal dollars.
    Failure modes: Figures are nominal, so inflation inflates them; they cover goods and food services but miss most services spending, and the advance estimate is revised heavily once full MRTS data arrive.
    Misconceptions: Retail sales are not total consumer spending - services such as housing, healthcare, and travel are largely excluded - so the series understates and skews the true consumption picture.
    Example: U.S. retail and food-services sales fell 8.7% in March 2020 as lockdowns began, then a record drop, and rebounded more than 18% in May 2020, the largest monthly gain on record.
    Aliases: Advance retail sales, Retail trade, MARTS
    Related:
    • Gross domestic product
    • Consumer confidence
    • Consumer price index
    Source: U.S. Census Bureau, Monthly Retail Trade — MARTS and MRTS overview

    Industrial production reviewed #

    A monthly index of the real output of U.S. manufacturing, mining, and electric and gas utilities, published by the Federal Reserve in its G.17 release. It measures the volume of industrial output relative to a base year, currently expressed as a percentage of its 2017 average.
    Intuition: It tracks the factory-and-resource side of the economy, which swings much harder than services over the business cycle.
    Mechanics: The Federal Reserve builds industry output indexes from physical-product data where available (vehicles assembled, electricity generated) and from production-worker hours where not, aggregating them with value-added weights in a Fisher index formula. The series is benchmarked to 2017=100 and revised monthly and annually.
    Failure modes: It covers only the industrial sector and excludes services and construction, so it overstates cyclical swings in overall GDP; weather-driven utility output and strikes add noise, and early estimates are revised.
    Misconceptions: Industrial production is not all economic production - services dominate U.S. output but are excluded entirely, so IP can fall while the broader economy grows.
    Example: In the G.17 release of March 16, 2026, total industrial production for February 2026 stood at 102.6% of its 2017 average, up 1.4% from a year earlier.
    IP index = 100 x (current-period real output / output in the 2017 base year), aggregated across industries with value-added weights.
    Aliases: IP, Industrial production index, G.17
    Related:

    • Purchasing managers index
    • Nonfarm payrolls
    • Gross domestic product
    Source: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, Industrial Production and Capacity Utilization (G.17) — Current Release: Summary

    Capacity utilization reviewed #

    The percentage of an industry's productive capacity actually being used, published monthly by the Federal Reserve alongside industrial production for manufacturing, mining, and utilities. It is computed as actual output divided by an estimate of sustainable maximum capacity.
    Intuition: It shows how much slack the industrial economy has: high utilization foreshadows bottlenecks and price pressure, while low utilization signals idle plants and weak investment.
    Mechanics: The Fed divides the industrial-production index for each industry by a related capacity index built from survey and physical-capacity data, reporting the operating rate as a percentage and comparing it with long-run (1972-present) averages for total industry and each sector.
    Failure modes: Capacity is unobservable and estimated, so utilization rates are revised materially; readings above the long-run average do not reliably trigger inflation when supply chains are global.
    Misconceptions: 100% is not the practical ceiling in these data - 'capacity' is defined as a sustainable maximum level of output, so utilization reflects engineering and economic constraints rather than a literal physical wall.
    Example: In February 2026 total-industry capacity utilization was 76.3%, about 3.1 percentage points below its 1972-2025 average of roughly 79.4%.
    Capacity utilization = (Industrial production index / Capacity index) x 100.
    Aliases: Operating rate, Capacity utilization rate
    Related:
    • Industrial production
    • Purchasing managers index
    • Inflation
    • Producer price index
    Source: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, Industrial Production and Capacity Utilization (G.17) — Current Release: Capacity Utilization tables

    Consumer confidence reviewed #

    A monthly survey-based index of U.S. household attitudes about current business and labor-market conditions and expectations for income, business activity, and jobs, published by The Conference Board with 1985 as the base year (1985=100). It comprises the Present Situation Index and the Expectations Index.
    Intuition: Confident consumers tend to spend more freely, so the index is watched as a leading indicator of consumption, the largest part of GDP.
    Mechanics: The Conference Board surveys households online each month (preliminary cutoff around the 22nd), asking five questions on present conditions and six-month expectations; responses are scored, seasonally adjusted, and indexed to the 1985 average of 100, with release at 10 a.m. ET on the last Tuesday of the month.
    Failure modes: Sentiment and actual spending can diverge for long stretches - consumers report gloom yet keep spending - and answers are sensitive to headlines such as gasoline prices, stock-market moves, and geopolitical conflict.
    Misconceptions: It is a survey of attitudes, not a measure of spending itself, and it differs from the University of Michigan's sentiment index, which weights personal finances and inflation expectations differently.
    Example: In July 2026 the Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index fell 1.4 points to 90.8, with the Present Situation Index at 114.9 and the Expectations Index at 74.7.
    Consumer Confidence Index = composite of the Present Situation and Expectations indexes, normalized so the 1985 average equals 100.

    Yield-curve inversion reviewed #

    A condition in which short-term Treasury yields exceed long-term yields, inverting the normally upward-sloping term structure of interest rates. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York measures the term spread as the 10-year minus 3-month Treasury rate and uses its slope to estimate the probability of a recession twelve months ahead.
    Intuition: Inversion signals that markets expect the central bank to cut rates because of coming weakness, so a negative spread has preceded nearly every modern U.S. recession with few false alarms.
    Mechanics: Policy tightening pushes short rates above long rates, turning the term spread negative. The New York Fed feeds the monthly 10-year/3-month spread into a probit model that outputs the probability of recession within twelve months, publishing updates in the first two weeks of each month.
    Failure modes: Lead times are long and variable - historically many months to around two years - and structural forces such as QE, compressed term premia, and foreign demand for long bonds can invert the curve without a near-term recession.
    Misconceptions: Inversion is a probabilistic signal, not a mechanical cause of recession; the signal is considered more reliable when the curve stays inverted, and the New York Fed stresses its estimates are not official Federal Reserve forecasts.
    Example: The 10-year/3-month spread turned negative in 2019 and again from late 2022 through 2024, reaching roughly -1.8 percentage points in mid-2023, the deepest inversion since the early 1980s.
    Term spread = Yield(10-year Treasury) - Yield(3-month Treasury); the curve is inverted when the spread is below zero.
    Aliases: Inverted yield curve, Negative term spread

    Monetary policy reviewed #

    The actions and communications of a central bank to influence the availability and cost of money and credit in pursuit of macroeconomic goals. In the United States, the Federal Reserve conducts monetary policy under its congressional mandate of maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates.
    Intuition: It is the economy's thermostat: the central bank tightens to cool demand and inflation, and eases to support growth and employment.
    Mechanics: The FOMC meets eight times a year to set a target range for the federal funds rate, implemented through open market operations, interest on reserve balances, the discount rate, and reserve requirements. Changes in the funds rate transmit to other short-term rates, foreign-exchange rates, long-term rates, money and credit, and ultimately employment, output, and prices.
    Failure modes: Transmission works with long and variable lags, so policy can overshoot; at the zero lower bound conventional rate policy loses traction, forcing reliance on balance-sheet tools and guidance.
    Misconceptions: The Fed does not set mortgage rates or the money supply directly, and it is legally independent: Congress sets the goals, but the FOMC chooses the instruments and timing.
    Example: Between March 2022 and July 2023 the FOMC raised the federal funds target range from 0-0.25% to 5.25-5.5%, the fastest tightening cycle since the early 1980s, after CPI inflation peaked at 9.1%.
    Aliases: Central bank policy, Fed policy

    Fiscal policy reviewed #

    The use of government taxation and spending to influence the economy, set in the United States by Congress and the President through the federal budget. Its outcomes are tracked as revenue, outlays, and the deficit - the annual gap between spending and revenue, financed by issuing Treasury debt.
    Intuition: Where monetary policy moves the price of money, fiscal policy moves money itself: cutting taxes or boosting spending injects demand, while raising taxes or cutting spending withdraws it.
    Mechanics: The federal government collects revenue mainly from individual income, payroll, and corporate taxes and spends on programs, transfers, defense, and interest on outstanding debt. When spending exceeds revenue the resulting deficit is covered by borrowing, adding to the national debt; automatic stabilizers such as unemployment insurance and progressive taxes widen the deficit in recessions without new legislation.
    Failure modes: Legislative lags make discretionary stimulus slow to arrive; large structural deficits raise debt-service costs that crowd out other priorities and constrain future policy room.
    Misconceptions: Fiscal policy is made by the elected government, not the Federal Reserve - the Fed's balance-sheet operations are monetary, not fiscal - and the deficit is a flow per year while the debt is the accumulated stock.
    Example: In fiscal year 2020 the U.S. deficit reached about $3.1 trillion, roughly 15% of GDP, on pandemic relief - the largest shortfall since World War II.
    Budget deficit = Federal outlays - Federal revenues; the national debt accumulates past deficits (adjusted for other means of financing).
    Aliases: Government budget policy, Budget policy

    Quantitative easing reviewed #

    Large-scale purchases of longer-term securities - Treasuries, agency debt, and mortgage-backed securities - by a central bank to ease financial conditions when the policy rate is near zero. The Federal Reserve ran several such large-scale asset purchase programs from late 2008 to 2014 and again in 2020-21.
    Intuition: When the overnight rate cannot go lower, the central bank instead pushes down longer-term rates directly by becoming a huge buyer of bonds.
    Mechanics: The New York Fed's Trading Desk buys securities outright in the open market for the System Open Market Account, crediting sellers' banks with new reserve balances. The purchases expand the Fed's balance sheet and put downward pressure on longer-term yields, making credit cheaper and financial conditions more accommodative.
    Failure modes: Effects fade without fiscal support and can inflate asset prices more than real activity; the enlarged balance sheet is politically fraught and hard to unwind without tightening financial conditions.
    Misconceptions: QE is not 'printing money' that goes directly to households - it swaps bank reserves for securities within the financial system - and it is distinct from the fiscal spending that puts cash in consumers' hands.
    Example: From January 2009 to August 2010 the Fed purchased $1.25 trillion of agency MBS, $175 billion of agency debt, and $300 billion of longer-term Treasuries; the 2008-2014 programs took total assets from under $1 trillion to about $4.5 trillion.
    Aliases: QE, Large-scale asset purchases, LSAP

    Quantitative tightening reviewed #

    The reduction of a central bank's securities holdings - by letting maturing bonds roll off without full reinvestment or, more rarely, selling them - which drains reserve balances and tightens financial conditions. It is the reverse of quantitative easing.
    Intuition: If QE steps on the gas by adding reserves and holding down long rates, QT gently lifts off, letting the balance sheet shrink so bond markets stand on their own.
    Mechanics: The FOMC caps reinvestment of principal payments from its System Open Market Account portfolio, allowing Treasury and MBS holdings to decline passively up to monthly runoff caps. The Fed began balance-sheet normalization this way in October 2017 and ran a second, larger runoff starting in June 2022.
    Failure modes: Runoff interacts unpredictably with banks' demand for reserves - the September 2019 repo-market spike forced the Fed to stop runoff and add reserves - and passive QT is slow to reverse if easing is suddenly needed.
    Misconceptions: QT is not the same as raising rates: it operates on the balance sheet rather than the policy rate, and it usually proceeds passively through runoff caps, not outright bond sales.
    Example: In October 2017 the Fed began capping reinvestment at $6 billion of Treasuries and $4 billion of MBS per month, stepping up quarterly to $30 billion and $20 billion; the 2022 runoff began at $47.5 billion per month and doubled to $95 billion after three months.
    Aliases: QT, Balance sheet normalization, Balance sheet runoff

    Forward guidance reviewed #

    Communication by a central bank about the likely future course of monetary policy, used to shape public expectations and thereby influence financial conditions today. The FOMC has used forward guidance in its postmeeting statements since the early 2000s, most intensively when the funds rate was pinned near zero.
    Intuition: Telling markets where rates are heading does some of the tightening or easing for you: long-term rates move on expectations, not just on today's rate.
    Mechanics: The FOMC embeds guidance in its postmeeting statements, minutes, and press conferences; guidance can be calendar-based ('for some time'), threshold-based (tied to unemployment or inflation levels), or data-dependent, conditioning the future path of the policy rate on how economic conditions evolve.
    Failure modes: Guidance loses potency if markets doubt the central bank will follow through, and data-dependent commitments can constrain the bank when conditions change abruptly - a credibility trap.
    Misconceptions: Forward guidance is not a binding promise - the Fed describes it as communication about the likely path of policy, and it evolves as the data change.
    Example: In December 2008, with the funds rate cut to near zero, the FOMC said weak conditions were 'likely to warrant exceptionally low levels of the federal funds rate for some time,' holding long rates down with no room left to cut.
    Aliases: Policy guidance, Central bank communication

    Rate hike reviewed #

    An increase in a central bank's policy interest rate - in the United States, an upward move in the FOMC's target range for the federal funds rate - intended to restrain demand and inflation. Hikes are typically delivered in 25-basis-point steps, with larger moves when inflation is urgent.
    Intuition: Raising the overnight rate makes borrowing costlier across the economy, cooling spending and price pressure at the cost of slower growth.
    Mechanics: The FOMC votes at its meetings to raise the target range, and the change transmits through money markets to other short-term interest rates, foreign-exchange rates, long-term interest rates, and the amount of money and credit, ultimately affecting employment, output, and prices with a lag.
    Failure modes: Lags mean the full braking effect can arrive after inflation has already slowed, risking overshoot; aggressive cycles also stress rate-sensitive sectors and leveraged borrowers abruptly.
    Misconceptions: The Fed does not raise 'all interest rates' directly - it moves one overnight interbank rate and relies on transmission - and a hike is a change in the rate, not a description of its level.
    Example: In June 2022 the FOMC raised the target range by 75 basis points, the largest single increase since 1994, and delivered three more 75-basis-point hikes that year as CPI inflation ran above 8%.
    Aliases: Interest rate increase, Policy tightening, Tightening
