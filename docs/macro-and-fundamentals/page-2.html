    Rate cut #

    A decrease in a central bank's policy interest rate - in the United States, a downward move in the FOMC's target range for the federal funds rate - intended to stimulate borrowing, spending, and employment. Cuts may be gradual 'insurance' moves or emergency responses to crises.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Cheaper overnight money cascades into cheaper loans, encouraging firms and households to spend and invest when the economy needs support.
    Mechanics: The FOMC lowers the target range and the change transmits through the same chain as a hike in reverse: other short-term rates, the exchange rate, long-term yields, and credit conditions ease, supporting demand, output, and employment with a lag.
    Failure modes: Cuts cannot push rates meaningfully below the zero lower bound, limiting ammunition in deep recessions; easing into a supply shock can stoke inflation without fixing output.
    Misconceptions: A cut does not guarantee markets rally - if it signals the Fed sees serious deterioration, equities can fall on the news - and rate futures, not the Fed, often lead the timing of expectations.
    Example: In March 2020 the FOMC cut 150 basis points in two emergency moves - 50 basis points on March 3 and 100 on March 15 - taking the target range to 0-0.25% as the pandemic struck.
    Aliases: Interest rate reduction, Easing, Policy easing
    Related:
    • Rate hike
    • Monetary policy
    • Dovish policy stance
    • Quantitative easing
    • Forward guidance
    Source: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, Federal Open Market Committee — About the FOMC

    Hawkish policy stance #

    A monetary-policy stance, or a description of a policymaker, that prioritizes controlling inflation - favoring higher interest rates and restraint even at some cost to growth and employment. The opposite stance is dovish.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A hawk would rather risk a cool economy than let inflation expectations escape; the hawk's nightmare is 1970s-style entrenched inflation.
    Mechanics: Hawkishness shows up in votes and language: supporting rate hikes or higher-for-longer rates, favoring balance-sheet runoff, and emphasizing inflation risks in FOMC statements and speeches. Researchers and markets track the committee's hawk-dove composition because it helps predict policy outcomes.
    Failure modes: Persistent hawkishness can over-tighten into a slowing economy, turning a soft landing into recession, and rates are a blunt tool against supply-driven inflation.
    Misconceptions: Hawkish does not mean 'always wants higher rates' - stances shift with conditions, and figures such as Alan Greenspan were rated hawkish in one era and dovish in another.
    Example: After CPI inflation hit 9.1% in June 2022, the FOMC turned decisively hawkish, delivering four consecutive 75-basis-point hikes; by 2024, with inflation much lower, the same committee pivoted and began cutting.
    Aliases: Hawkish, Inflation hawk, Hawk
    Related:
    • Dovish policy stance
    • Rate hike
    • Monetary policy
    • Inflation
    • Quantitative tightening
    Source: Investopedia, What Is an Inflation Hawk? — Definition and Key Takeaways

    Dovish policy stance #

    A monetary-policy stance, or a description of a policymaker, that prioritizes supporting growth and employment - favoring low interest rates, easing, and tolerance of inflation risk. The opposite stance is hawkish.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A dove worries more about the jobless than about a few tenths of extra inflation; the dove's nightmare is a weak economy left to stagnate.
    Mechanics: Dovishness appears as support for rate cuts, asset purchases such as quantitative easing, accommodative forward guidance, and statement language emphasizing employment shortfalls rather than inflation overshoots.
    Failure modes: Unchecked dovishness can overheat the economy and unanchor inflation expectations, forcing a more painful tightening later; critics link the 2021-22 inflation surge partly to policy that stayed dovish too long.
    Misconceptions: Dovish is not 'soft on inflation forever' - doves judge the side effects of low rates as manageable, and most policymakers move between stances as conditions change.
    Example: Ben Bernanke and Janet Yellen were widely regarded as doves for their commitment to low rates and QE after 2008; the Fed kept the funds rate below 1% until December 2016 despite years of expansion.
    Aliases: Dovish, Dove
    Related:
    • Hawkish policy stance
    • Rate cut
    • Quantitative easing
    • Monetary policy
    Source: Investopedia, What Is a Dove? — Definition and Key Takeaways

    Economic surprise index #

    An index that aggregates how economic data releases compare with consensus forecasts, turning 'better or worse than expected' into a single daily time series. The best known is the Citigroup Economic Surprise Index, a rolling three-month, weighted measure of data surprises versus Bloomberg survey medians.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Markets move on surprises, not levels - the index tracks whether the flow of data is beating or disappointing what economists had already priced in.
    Mechanics: Each release's surprise is the actual value minus the survey median, normalized by the historical standard deviation of surprises; the index sums these standardized surprises over a rolling three-month window, weighting indicators by their historical impact on high-frequency FX moves and applying a time decay so recent surprises count more.
    Failure modes: It is mean-reverting by construction - a long run of beats pulls consensus up until misses become arithmetically likely - and it measures deviation from forecast, not whether the economy is actually strong or weak.
    Misconceptions: A positive reading does not mean the economy is good, only better than expected; a deeply negative index can coincide with solid growth if consensus was simply too optimistic.
    Example: The Citi U.S. Economic Surprise Index fell deeply negative in mid-2022 as data kept surprising hawkish forecasts, then climbed back to around its 90th historical percentile by early 2026 as releases repeatedly beat consensus.
    ESI = Sum over releases of w_i x (Actual_i - Consensus_i) / sigma_i, computed daily over a trailing three-month window with FX-impact weights and time decay.
    Aliases: Citi Economic Surprise Index, CESI, Economic Surprise Index
    Related:
    • Nonfarm payrolls
    • Consumer price index
    • Purchasing managers index
    • Earnings surprise
    Source: iCapital, Market Pulse: Macro Playbook (index definitions) — Index definitions: Citi Economic Surprise Index

    Earnings per share #

    A public company's net profit divided by its number of common shares outstanding, reported as basic EPS on weighted-average shares and as diluted EPS including the potential shares from options, warrants, and convertibles. It is the standard per-share measure of profitability shown on the income statement.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: EPS turns total profit into an amount 'per share of ownership,' making companies of different sizes comparable and forming the denominator of the P/E ratio.
    Mechanics: Basic EPS divides net income minus preferred dividends by the weighted-average common shares outstanding during the period; diluted EPS assumes conversion of all dilutive securities, adding them to the share count and adjusting income. U.S. companies report both on the face of the income statement.
    Failure modes: Buybacks can grow EPS even when total profit stagnates; one-time gains and non-GAAP 'adjusted' figures undermine comparability, and dilution from stock compensation is easy to undercount.
    Misconceptions: Higher EPS is not automatically value creation - borrowing to repurchase shares raises EPS while raising risk - and EPS ignores how much capital was invested to generate the earnings.
    Example: A company earning $1 billion with 500 million weighted-average shares reports basic EPS of $2.00; if options add 25 million dilutive shares, diluted EPS is about $1.90.
    EPS = (Net income - Preferred dividends) / Weighted-average common shares outstanding.
    Aliases: EPS, Earnings per share
    Related:
    • Price-earnings ratio
    • Net profit margin
    • Revenue
    • Share buyback
    • Earnings surprise
    Source: SEC Investor.gov, Investing Basics Glossary: Earnings Per Share — Earnings Per Share entry
    Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements — Earnings Per Share or EPS

    Revenue #

    The total amount of money a company brings in from selling its products or services during a period, before any expenses are deducted - the 'top line' of the income statement. It is typically reported as net revenue after subtracting returns, allowances, and discounts.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Revenue is the raw scale of a business's sales engine; every margin and profit figure ultimately starts from it.
    Mechanics: Companies recognize revenue when performance obligations are satisfied - goods delivered or services rendered - under GAAP or IFRS rules, not necessarily when cash arrives. The income statement then deducts costs stepwise from revenue down to the bottom line.
    Failure modes: Recognition rules leave room for timing games such as channel stuffing, and revenue growth can mask deteriorating economics when margins are collapsing underneath.
    Misconceptions: Revenue is neither income nor cash: a firm can book large revenue while losing money or burning cash, because expenses and working-capital needs are accounted for elsewhere.
    Example: Apple reported about $394 billion of net sales in fiscal 2022 against roughly $100 billion of net income; the $294 billion gap is cost of sales, operating expenses, interest, and taxes.
    Net revenue = Gross revenue (sales) - Returns, allowances, and discounts.
    Aliases: Sales, Top line, Turnover
    Related:
    • Gross margin
    • Operating margin
    • Net profit margin
    • Price-to-sales ratio
    • Earnings per share
    Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements — Income Statements

    Gross margin #

    Revenue remaining after subtracting the direct costs of producing the goods or services sold, expressed as a percentage of revenue. The SEC's investors' guide describes gross profit - sometimes called gross margin - as net revenues minus the cost of sales.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It measures how much markup the core product carries before overhead: the first test of whether a business model works.
    Mechanics: From the income statement, subtract cost of goods sold (materials, direct labor, production overhead) from net revenue to obtain gross profit, then divide by net revenue for the margin percentage.
    Failure modes: Cross-industry comparisons are meaningless - software firms run near 80% while grocers run near 25% - and shifts in product mix or inventory accounting methods distort the trend.
    Misconceptions: A high gross margin does not imply profitability: heavy R&D or marketing spending below the gross line can still leave the company loss-making.
    Example: If a company sells goods for $10 million that cost $6 million to produce, gross profit is $4 million and the gross margin is 40%.
    Gross margin = (Net revenue - Cost of goods sold) / Net revenue x 100.
    Aliases: Gross profit margin
    Related:
    • Operating margin
    • Net profit margin
    • Revenue
    Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements — Income Statements: gross profit / gross margin

    Operating margin #

    Income from operations as a percentage of net revenues - the share of each sales dollar left after production costs and operating expenses, but before interest and taxes. The SEC's investors' guide defines it exactly as income from operations divided by net revenues.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It captures the profitability of the core business itself, independent of how the company is financed or taxed.
    Mechanics: From the income statement, take operating income (gross profit minus selling, general and administrative costs, R&D, and depreciation and amortization) and divide by net revenue; because it excludes interest and taxes it is often called the EBIT margin.
    Failure modes: 'Operating' definitions vary - some firms exclude restructuring or stock-based compensation - so adjusted margins across vendors are not always comparable, and the metric hides the interest burden of leveraged firms.
    Misconceptions: A healthy operating margin does not guarantee survival: debt service and capital expenditure live below the operating line, and a margin percentage says nothing about absolute profit dollars.
    Example: With $50 million of operating income on $200 million of net revenue, operating margin is 25% - each sales dollar yields 25 cents of profit before interest and taxes.
    Operating margin = Income from operations / Net revenues x 100.
    Aliases: EBIT margin, Return on sales
    Related:
    • Gross margin
    • Net profit margin
    • Revenue
    • Enterprise value
    • EV-to-EBITDA
    Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements — Financial Statement Ratios and Calculations: Operating Margin

    Net profit margin #

    Net income as a percentage of revenue - the final 'bottom-line' share of each sales dollar remaining after all costs, interest, and taxes have been deducted.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is the most complete single-margin summary: what ultimately falls to shareholders out of each dollar of sales.
    Mechanics: Divide the income statement's bottom line - net income after cost of sales, operating expenses, interest, and income taxes - by net revenue for the same period, usually on a trailing-twelve-month basis for valuation work.
    Failure modes: One-off items such as asset sales, tax credits, or write-downs can dominate a period's bottom line, so a single quarter's net margin is noisy; the margin also embeds leverage and tax structure, not just operating skill.
    Misconceptions: A low net margin is not a sign of a bad business: a 2-3% margin grocer can earn excellent returns on capital through rapid turnover, while a 30% margin firm may sit on vast invested capital.
    Example: Walmart's net margin runs about 2-3% while Microsoft's exceeds 35% - both highly profitable in absolute dollars, through opposite volume-versus-margin models.
    Net profit margin = Net income / Net revenue x 100.
    Aliases: Net margin, Profit margin, Bottom-line margin
    Related:
    • Gross margin
    • Operating margin
    • Earnings per share
    • Price-earnings ratio
    • Revenue
    Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements — Income Statements: net profit / bottom line

    Free cash flow #

    The cash a business generates from operations after the capital expenditures needed to maintain and grow its asset base - the cash genuinely available to debt and equity holders. In Damodaran's framework, valuation discounts cash flows to equity (after interest, principal, and reinvestment) or to the firm (after taxes and reinvestment but before debt payments).
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Accounting profit is an opinion; free cash flow is what is actually left to pay dividends, buy back stock, or pay down debt.
    Mechanics: A common derivation starts from the cash flow statement: FCF equals cash flow from operating activities minus capital expenditures. Damodaran's equity variant (FCFE) further nets out debt payments and the debt-financed share of reinvestment, while the firm variant (FCFF) uses after-tax operating income minus total reinvestment.
    Failure modes: Capex timing makes single-year FCF lumpy; growth companies can show negative FCF precisely because they are investing well, and 'adjusted FCF' definitions vary widely across data providers.
    Misconceptions: Positive net income does not imply positive free cash flow - rising receivables, inventory build, or heavy capex can leave a profitable company cash-negative, and the reverse also holds.
    Example: A company with $800 million of operating cash flow and $300 million of capex generates $500 million of FCF; against a $10 billion market cap that is a 5% free-cash-flow yield.
    FCF = Cash flow from operations - Capital expenditures (FCFE = Net income + Depreciation - Capex - Change in working capital + Net borrowing).
    Aliases: FCF, Free cash flow to the firm, FCFF, FCFE
    Related:
    • Discounted cash flow
    • Earnings per share
    • Share buyback
    • Dividend yield
    • Revenue
    • Terminal value
    Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements — Cash Flow Statements
    Source: Aswath Damodaran (NYU Stern), Discounted Cash Flow Valuation lecture notes — Equity Valuation versus Firm Valuation

    Price-earnings ratio #

    A company's share price divided by its earnings per share - how many dollars investors pay for each dollar of annual earnings. The SEC's investors' guide and Damodaran both define it as market price per share over EPS, with variants depending on which earnings period and share count are used.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: P/E is the market's price tag on earnings: high ratios imply investors expect growth or see low risk, while low ratios imply the opposite - or neglect.
    Mechanics: Divide the current share price by EPS, most often trailing-twelve-month EPS. Damodaran stresses definitional consistency - trailing versus forward, diluted versus basic, before or after extraordinary items - and shows that embedded in every multiple are the same growth, risk, and cash-flow drivers as a DCF.
    Failure modes: It is undefined or meaningless for loss-makers, deceptively low at cyclical earnings peaks, and incomparable across firms with different leverage, growth, or accounting rules.
    Misconceptions: A low P/E is not automatically 'cheap' - it may price declining earnings - and comparing raw P/Es across industries ignores the growth and risk differences that justify different multiples.
    Example: A stock at $20 with EPS of $2 trades at a P/E of 10 - the SEC guide uses exactly this case, calling it a stock 'selling at 10 times its earnings.'
    P/E = Price per share / Earnings per share; for a stable-growth firm, P/E = Payout ratio x (1 + g) / (r - g).
    Aliases: P/E ratio, PE, Price-to-earnings ratio, Earnings multiple
    Related:
    • Forward price-earnings ratio
    • Earnings yield
    • Earnings per share
    • Price-to-book ratio
    • Price-to-sales ratio
    • Dividend yield
    Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, Beginners' Guide to Financial Statements — Financial Statement Ratios and Calculations: P/E Ratio
    Source: SEC Investor.gov, Investing Basics Glossary: Price-earnings (P/E) Ratio — Price-earnings (P/E) Ratio entry
    Source: Aswath Damodaran (NYU Stern), Relative Valuation lecture notes (Packet 2) — Price Earnings Ratio: Definition

    Forward price-earnings ratio #

    The price-earnings ratio computed using expected earnings per share - for the next fiscal year or the next four quarters - rather than trailing earnings. Damodaran classifies both of those forward-EPS variants as forward P/E ratios.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Since investing is about the future, forward P/E prices what a company is expected to earn, giving the market's growth-adjusted valuation snapshot.
    Mechanics: Divide the current price by consensus analyst EPS estimates for the coming year. Because the denominator is a forecast, the multiple moves when analysts revise estimates even if the price is unchanged: it rises on downgrades and falls on upgrades.
    Failure modes: It rests on analyst estimates that are systematically optimistic and herd toward consensus; at turning points forward EPS collapses after the price does, so the apparently cheap multiple was never real.
    Misconceptions: A forward P/E below the trailing P/E does not prove cheapness - it may simply bake in aggressive growth forecasts that are later cut.
    Example: A stock at $100 with trailing EPS of $4 (trailing P/E of 25) and consensus forward EPS of $5 trades at a forward P/E of 20.
    Forward P/E = Current price / Expected EPS (next 12 months or next fiscal year); for a stable-growth firm, forward P/E = Payout ratio / (r - g).
    Aliases: Forward P/E, Forward earnings multiple
    Related:
    • Price-earnings ratio
    • Earnings per share
    • Guidance
    • Earnings surprise
    • Earnings yield
    Source: Aswath Damodaran (NYU Stern), Relative Valuation lecture notes (Packet 2) — Price Earnings Ratio: Definition (EPS time variants)

    Price-to-book ratio #

    Market capitalization - or price per share - divided by the book value of shareholders' equity: what investors pay per dollar of net asset value on the balance sheet. Damodaran's consistency rule for multiples requires equity value in the numerator to be paired with equity book value in the denominator.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: P/B asks whether the market values the business above or below the accumulated accounting value of what it owns minus what it owes.
    Mechanics: Divide price per share by book value per share (shareholders' equity divided by shares outstanding). The ratio links fundamentally to return on equity: firms earning ROE above their cost of equity deserve to trade above book value.
    Failure modes: Book value omits intangibles such as brands, software, and R&D, so P/B misleads for asset-light firms; heavy buybacks or losses can shrink equity to the point of absurd ratios.
    Misconceptions: A P/B below 1 is not automatically a bargain - it can mean the market expects the assets to earn below their cost of capital or to be written down.
    Example: A bank trading at $12 per share with a book value of $10 per share has a P/B of 1.2; a software firm at the same multiple would be unusually cheap because its real assets are mostly off-balance-sheet.
    P/B = Price per share / Book value per share; for a stable-growth firm, P/B = ROE x Payout ratio / (r - g).
    Aliases: P/B ratio, P/BV, Price-to-book
    Related:
    • Price-earnings ratio
    • Price-to-sales ratio
    • Enterprise value
    • Economic moat
    Source: Aswath Damodaran (NYU Stern), Relative Valuation lecture notes (Packet 2) — Definitional Tests: equity value over equity book value

    Price-to-sales ratio #

    Market capitalization divided by trailing revenue - equivalently price per share over revenue per share - the amount investors pay per dollar of a company's sales. It remains usable when earnings are negative, which the P/E ratio is not.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Sales are the hardest income-statement line to fake and exist even for loss-making firms, so P/S is the multiple of last resort for young or distressed companies.
    Mechanics: Divide market capitalization by trailing-twelve-month revenue. Because the ratio ignores costs entirely, it must be read alongside margins: a firm at 1x sales with 20% net margins is effectively on a 5x P/E.
    Failure modes: It treats a dollar of grocery revenue like a dollar of software revenue, so low-margin, high-revenue firms look deceptively cheap, and it ignores leverage - EV/Sales is the better tool for indebted firms.
    Misconceptions: A low P/S does not imply undervaluation - thin-margin businesses rationally trade near zero - and the ratio says nothing about whether sales will ever convert to profit.
    Example: A retailer with a $50 billion market cap and $100 billion of sales trades at 0.5x sales; with a 2% net margin that equates to a P/E of 25 - not obviously a bargain.
    P/S = Market capitalization / Trailing 12-month revenue = Price per share / Revenue per share.
    Aliases: P/S ratio, Price-to-sales
    Related:
    • Price-earnings ratio
    • Revenue
    • Net profit margin
    • Enterprise value
    Source: Aswath Damodaran (NYU Stern), Relative Valuation lecture notes (Packet 2) — Multiples are standardized estimates of price

    Enterprise value #

    The total economic value of a business - what an acquirer would effectively pay: market value of equity plus total debt (and other claims such as preferred stock and minority interest) minus cash and cash equivalents. It measures the value of the operating business independent of how it is financed.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Buying a company means assuming its debts and receiving its cash, so the true price tag is the equity value plus net debt - enterprise value is that price tag.
    Mechanics: EV equals market capitalization plus total debt minus cash and cash equivalents, adding preferred stock and noncontrolling interest where present. As the numerator of firm-level multiples such as EV/EBITDA it must be paired with pre-financing metrics - Damodaran's consistency rule: firm value over firm earnings, equity value over equity earnings.
    Failure modes: Debt and cash are book figures that can be stale, off-balance-sheet obligations such as leases and pensions are often missed, and for banks 'debt' is raw material rather than financing, which makes EV meaningless for financials.
    Misconceptions: EV is not what shareholders receive in a takeover - equity holders get the equity value (with any premium), while EV prices the whole enterprise including the debt an acquirer assumes.
    Example: Using Investopedia's Dollar General illustration: a $56.2 billion market cap plus $14.25 billion of debt minus $345 million of cash gives an enterprise value of about $70.1 billion.
    EV = Market capitalization + Total debt - Cash and cash equivalents (+ Preferred stock + Minority interest).
    Aliases: EV, Firm value, Aggregate value
    Related:
    • EV-to-EBITDA
    • Price-earnings ratio
    • Discounted cash flow
    • Merger arbitrage
    • Price-to-sales ratio
    Source: Investopedia, What Is the Enterprise Multiple (EV/EBITDA)? — Understanding the Enterprise Multiple Formula and Calculation
    Source: Aswath Damodaran (NYU Stern), Relative Valuation lecture notes (Packet 2) — Definitional Tests: firm value over firm earnings

    EV to EBITDA #

    Enterprise value divided by earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization - a capital-structure-neutral multiple showing how many years of pre-financing operating earnings the whole business costs. It is widely used to compare takeover values across firms with different debt levels and tax regimes.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Because both the numerator and the denominator are pre-financing, EV/EBITDA puts a leveraged firm and a debt-free firm on equal footing.
    Mechanics: Compute EV as market capitalization plus total debt minus cash, and EBITDA as operating earnings before depreciation and amortization, then divide. Lower multiples relative to peers or history suggest relative cheapness; higher multiples imply growth expectations or overvaluation.
    Failure modes: EBITDA ignores capex and working-capital needs, so capital-intensive firms look cheaper than they are; the multiple is misleading for financials, and 'value traps' show low multiples because projected EBITDA is unrealistically high.
    Misconceptions: EV/EBITDA is not a cash-flow multiple - EBITDA overstates cash generation by ignoring reinvestment - and a low multiple alone does not signal undervaluation without checking forward EBITDA.
    Example: In Investopedia's worked example, Dollar General's EV of about $70.1 billion divided by $3.86 billion of trailing EBITDA gave an enterprise multiple of 18.2x.
    EV/EBITDA = (Market cap + Total debt - Cash and equivalents) / EBITDA.
    Aliases: EV/EBITDA, Enterprise multiple, EV to EBITDA
    Related:
    • Enterprise value
    • Price-earnings ratio
    • Operating margin
    • Merger arbitrage
    • Discounted cash flow
    • Terminal value
    Source: Investopedia, What Is the Enterprise Multiple (EV/EBITDA)? — Understanding the Enterprise Multiple Formula and Calculation

    Dividend yield #

    Annual dividends per share divided by the current share price - the dividend-only rate of return on a stock at today's price. Holding the dividend constant, the yield rises when the price falls and falls when the price rises.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is the stock-market analog of a bond's yield: the cash income you collect per dollar invested, ignoring price changes.
    Mechanics: Sum the last four quarters of dividends - or annualize the latest quarterly rate - and divide by the current price. Because the denominator moves every day, a falling stock's yield spikes even before any dividend cut is announced.
    Failure modes: A very high yield often signals distress - the market pricing in a cut, the classic 'yield trap'; trailing yields lag announced cuts, and pass-through structures such as REITs, MLPs, and BDCs yield high by legal design, not by strength.
    Misconceptions: Dividend yield is not your total return: the price typically drops by about the dividend amount on the ex-date, and taxes further reduce the net income from the stated yield.
    Example: A $20 stock paying $1 per year yields 5%; an otherwise identical $40 stock paying $1 yields only 2.5%.
    Dividend yield = Annual dividends per share / Price per share x 100.
    Aliases: Yield, Dividend return
    Related:
    • Cash dividend
    • Ex-dividend date
    • Earnings yield
    • Price-earnings ratio
    • Share buyback
    Source: Investopedia, What Is the Dividend Yield? — Calculating the Dividend Yield

    Earnings yield #

    Earnings per share divided by the share price - the reciprocal of the P/E ratio - expressing how much annual earnings the company generates per dollar invested. It allows direct comparison of equity valuations with bond yields.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: If P/E is the price of a dollar of earnings, the earnings yield is the 'interest rate' a stock's current earnings represent on its price.
    Mechanics: Compute E/P using trailing or forward EPS; practitioners compare it with Treasury yields (the so-called Fed model) or rank stocks by it. A 5% earnings yield corresponds to a P/E of 20.
    Failure modes: It inherits every P/E flaw - meaningless for loss-makers, distorted by cyclicality and accounting noise - and comparing it with bond yields ignores that earnings can grow while bond coupons are fixed.
    Misconceptions: A high earnings yield relative to Treasuries does not guarantee equities are cheap: it can simply reflect higher equity risk, and E/P is not a promised payment like a coupon.
    Example: A stock at $50 with EPS of $2.50 has an earnings yield of 5% (a P/E of 20); against a 4.3% 10-year Treasury yield, the equity 'spread' is 0.7 percentage points.
    Earnings yield = Earnings per share / Price per share = 1 / (P/E ratio).
    Aliases: E/P ratio, Earnings-to-price ratio
    Related:
    • Price-earnings ratio
    • Forward price-earnings ratio
    • Dividend yield
    • Yield curve inversion
    Source: Aswath Damodaran (NYU Stern), Relative Valuation lecture notes (Packet 2) — Price Earnings Ratio: Definition

    Discounted cash flow #

    An intrinsic valuation method that estimates an asset's worth as the present value of its expected future cash flows, discounted at a rate reflecting their risk. Damodaran states the principle directly: assets that generate cash flows have value equal to the sum of those expected cash flows discounted back to today.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A dollar next year is worth less than a dollar today; DCF simply adds up all future dollars after shrinking each one for time and risk.
    Mechanics: Forecast free cash flows over an explicit horizon and discount them at the cost of equity (equity valuation) or the weighted average cost of capital (firm valuation), then add a discounted terminal value for all cash flows beyond the horizon. Damodaran's first rule is never to mix and match cash flows and discount rates.
    Failure modes: Outputs are hypersensitive to the discount rate and growth assumptions - small input changes can swing value by multiples - and the terminal value commonly supplies most of the total, concentrating fragility at the least forecastable point.
    Misconceptions: A DCF does not produce 'the' value of a company - it prices a specific set of assumptions - and, as Damodaran notes, many sell-side DCFs are really relative valuations reverse-engineered to a target multiple.
    Example: Damodaran's lecture example discounts five years of equity cash flows ($50 to $83.5 million) plus a $1,603 million terminal value at a 13.625% cost of equity to reach an equity value near $1 billion.
    Value = Sum over t=1..n of CF_t / (1+r)^t + TV / (1+r)^n, where CF_t = expected cash flow in year t, r = discount rate, TV = terminal value.
    Aliases: DCF, Intrinsic valuation, DCF valuation

    Terminal value #

    The estimated value of all cash flows beyond the explicit forecast horizon in a DCF model, computed either as a growing perpetuity or as an exit multiple. It typically dominates the valuation, often contributing well over half of total present value.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Since a business presumably outlives any five- or ten-year forecast, terminal value is the compressed answer to 'and then what?' - and where most of the value hides.
    Mechanics: The Gordon-growth version sets TV = CF(n+1) / (r - g), with g capped at or below long-run nominal economic growth; the exit-multiple version applies an observed multiple such as EV/EBITDA to the final forecast year, which smuggles relative valuation into the DCF.
    Failure modes: The result explodes as g approaches r - a one-point change in either can double the value - and assuming g above long-run GDP growth implies the firm eventually grows larger than the economy itself.
    Misconceptions: A large terminal-value share is not automatically a red flag - it is mathematically inevitable for growing firms - but it demands that growth and margin assumptions remain defensible far beyond the forecast window.
    Example: In Damodaran's worked example, a firm whose five explicit years of equity cash flows total about $337 million carries a $1,603 million terminal value - roughly 80% of the present value of equity comes from beyond year five.
    Terminal value = CF_(n+1) / (r - g); its present value is TV / (1+r)^n.
    Aliases: TV, Continuing value, Horizon value

    Economic moat #

    A structural competitive advantage that lets a company defend its profits against rivals for an extended period - the concept popularized by Warren Buffett and operationalized by Morningstar as its Economic Moat Rating of wide, narrow, or none. Morningstar grants a wide moat when advantages are expected to last more than 20 years and a narrow moat for about 10.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A moat is what stops competition from grinding a great business's returns down to average: the difference between a temporary profit spike and a durable cash machine.
    Mechanics: Morningstar identifies five moat sources: network effects, intangible assets (patents, brands, regulatory licenses), cost advantage, switching costs, and efficient scale. Analysts verify that returns on invested capital have exceeded the cost of capital for years and then judge whether the source of those excess profits can persist.
    Failure modes: Moats erode: technology shifts, regulation, and disruption can drain them faster than expected, and as Morningstar cautions, past excess returns are a highly imperfect predictor of future ones.
    Misconceptions: A moat is not a guarantee of a good investment - overpaying for even a wide-moat stock can produce poor returns - and high current profitability alone does not prove that a moat exists.
    Example: Morningstar cites Visa's network effect (more cardholders attract more merchants, which attract more cardholders) and Autodesk's switching costs (careers built on its software) as classic wide-moat sources.
    Aliases: Moat, Competitive advantage, Wide moat

    Earnings surprise #

    The difference between a company's reported earnings and the consensus analyst estimate - positive when profits beat expectations, negative when they miss. Since prices embed consensus beforehand, the surprise rather than the earnings level is what moves the stock.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Markets price in what everyone expects; only the unexpected part of an earnings report is genuinely new information.
    Mechanics: Analysts model EPS using company guidance, industry data, and SEC filings; data vendors aggregate these into a consensus. The surprise equals actual EPS minus consensus, often expressed as a percentage, and research finds positive surprises produce both an immediate price jump and a gradual drift over the following weeks.
    Failure modes: Companies manage the benchmark by guiding low to manufacture beats, so serial 'beats' can be engineered; surprises driven by tax rates or one-offs rather than operations fade fastest.
    Misconceptions: A good report can still sink the stock: if a company beats EPS but issues weak guidance, the forward-looking disappointment dominates the backward-looking beat.
    Example: A company expected to earn $1.00 that reports $1.10 posts a +10% surprise; studies show such beats are typically rewarded with both an immediate pop and a multi-week drift higher.
    Earnings surprise (%) = (Actual EPS - Consensus EPS) / |Consensus EPS| x 100.
    Aliases: Earnings beat, Earnings miss, EPS surprise

    Guidance #

    A public company's own forecast of its future performance - typically revenue and EPS for the coming quarter or year - issued voluntarily alongside earnings reports and discussed on analyst calls. It shapes the consensus against which actual results are later judged.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Guidance is management's answer to 'what should we expect?' - and it often matters more to the stock than the quarter just reported.
    Mechanics: Companies typically release guidance with quarterly results and may update it mid-quarter via pre-announcements. Under Regulation FD it must be shared with all investors simultaneously, and the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act's safe harbor shields good-faith forward-looking statements that turn out wrong.
    Failure modes: Management incentives skew it - conservative 'sandbagging' to manufacture beats or promotional optimism - and companies are under no obligation to update guidance even when events make it stale.
    Misconceptions: Guidance is not required disclosure and not a promise: it is an estimate protected by safe-harbor provisions, and critics such as Warren Buffett argue quarterly guidance pushes managers toward short-termism.
    Example: A company guiding to $4.00-4.20 of EPS that reports $4.05 is treated as 'in line'; the identical $4.05 against $4.50 guidance counts as a miss despite equal profits.
    Aliases: Earnings guidance, Company guidance, Forward outlook

    Share buyback #

    A company's repurchase of its own shares on the open market or via tender offer, reducing shares outstanding. Repurchased shares are retired or held as treasury stock, raising each remaining share's claim on earnings.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is the flexible cousin of the dividend: another way to return cash to shareholders, but one that also shrinks the share count and lifts per-share metrics.
    Mechanics: The board authorizes a repurchase program and the company buys in the open market over time or via a fixed-price or Dutch-auction tender offer, funded from cash, operating cash flow, or new debt. Mechanically EPS rises as the share count falls, and since 2023 large U.S. buybacks incur a 1% excise tax.
    Failure modes: Buybacks at elevated prices destroy value - companies tend to buy the most near market peaks - and repurchases can starve investment or merely mask dilution from executive stock compensation rather than genuinely shrinking the count.
    Misconceptions: A buyback does not automatically create value: it raises EPS arithmetically, but value is added only if shares are bought below intrinsic value; nor is it a dividend, since non-selling holders receive no cash directly.
    Example: Investopedia's illustration: with $1 million of earnings and 1 million shares, EPS is $1 and P/E is 20 at $20; repurchasing 100,000 shares lifts EPS to $1.11, and holding the P/E constant implies an 11% price rise to $22.22.
    Post-buyback EPS = Net income / (Shares outstanding - Shares repurchased).

    Stock split #

    A corporate action that increases the number of outstanding shares by issuing new shares to existing holders in a set ratio, cutting the per-share price proportionally while leaving market capitalization unchanged. The most common forms are 2-for-1, 3-for-2, and 3-for-1 splits.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A split is slicing the same pie into more pieces: your ownership stake and the company's value do not change, only the size of each slice.
    Mechanics: The board approves the split, and on the effective date each holder receives additional shares per existing share while the exchange adjusts the price down by the ratio. FINRA processes such corporate actions for OTC companies, which must submit notice 10 days before the record/effective date.
    Failure modes: Splits change nothing fundamental, so any post-split 'momentum' can reverse; the lower nominal price can invite speculative froth, and the mechanics create temporary confusion for options and historical price series.
    Misconceptions: A split does not make the stock 'cheaper' in valuation terms - market cap and multiples are unchanged - and a high nominal share price is not, by itself, a reason a stock cannot keep rising.
    Example: FINRA's illustration: 10 shares at $100 become 20 shares at $50 in a 2-for-1 split; Netflix's 7-for-1 split in July 2015 adjusted its close from $702.60 to $100.37 without changing its market value.
    Post-split price = Pre-split price / Split ratio; Shares after = Shares before x Split ratio.
    Aliases: Forward stock split, Share split
