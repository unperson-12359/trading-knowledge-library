    Locate requirement reviewed #

    The Regulation SHO requirement (Rule 203(b)(1)-(2)) that, before effecting a short sale in any equity security, a broker-dealer must have reasonable grounds to believe the security can be borrowed so that it can be delivered on the date delivery is due, and must document that 'locate' before executing the short sale.
    Intuition: Before you sell shares you don't own, your broker must point to a real, borrowable source of those shares — no selling into thin air.
    Mechanics: The broker-dealer identifies a source of borrowable shares (its own inventory, a stock-loan desk, or an 'easy to borrow' list) and records the locate prior to the short sale; reliance on blanket assurances without reasonable grounds is prohibited. Bona fide market making is excepted from the locate requirement, though not from close-out and pre-borrow obligations.
    Failure modes: Locates are typically arranged before settlement, so located stock can be recalled or used elsewhere by settlement day, producing failures to deliver despite compliance; 'easy to borrow' lists can be stale, and sham locates (e.g., repeatedly relying on the same exhausted source) have been the subject of SEC enforcement.
    Misconceptions: A locate is not the same as an actual borrow or a guarantee of delivery — it is a reasonable-grounds belief documented in advance; and failing to deliver later is not per se proof the locate was improper, since legitimate delays occur.
    Example: A trader wants to short 10,000 shares of a small-cap; her broker's loan desk confirms 50,000 shares are borrowable from a lender, records the locate with source and quantity, and only then routes the short-sale order.
    Aliases: Rule 203(b)(1) locate

    Reg SHO reviewed #

    The SEC's short-sale regulation (Rules 200-204 under the Exchange Act), adopted in 2004 with compliance beginning January 3, 2005, to update short-sale regulation and address abusive naked short selling and persistent failures to deliver. Its four pillars are order marking (Rule 200), the short-sale price test circuit breaker (Rule 201), the locate requirement (Rule 203(b)), and close-out requirements for fails to deliver (Rules 204 and 203(b)(3)).
    Intuition: It's the rulebook that keeps short selling honest: mark your orders correctly, don't pile onto crashing stocks, line up shares before you sell, and actually deliver what you sold.
    Mechanics: Rule 200 requires sale orders to be marked 'long,' 'short,' or 'short exempt.' Rule 201 imposes a bid-based price test for a stock down 10% intraday. Rule 203(b) requires a documented locate before shorting and a 13-day close-out for threshold securities; Rule 204 requires clearing-agency participants to close out fails to deliver by the start of the next settlement day (short sales) or third settlement day (long sales/bona fide market making), with pre-borrow penalties otherwise.
    Failure modes: Fails to deliver persist for legitimate reasons (processing delays, hard-to-borrow names), so threshold-list inclusion does not prove abuse; the rules also cannot fully prevent manipulation executed through options, offshore venues, or deceptive conduct addressed instead by antifraud Rule 10b-21.
    Misconceptions: Naked short selling is not categorically illegal under Reg SHO — market makers in bona fide market making may short without a locate; nor does appearing on a threshold securities list brand a company or its stock as manipulated.
    Example: A broker-dealer that fails to deliver shares from a short sale must buy or borrow like-kind securities to close out the fail by the opening of trading on the next settlement day, or it and its introducing brokers are barred from further short sales in that stock without a pre-borrow.
    Aliases: Regulation SHO

    Pattern day trader rule reviewed #

    The FINRA margin rule regime under which a customer who executes four or more day trades within five business days in a margin account (a day trade being the purchase and sale, or sale and purchase, of the same security on the same day) is designated a 'pattern day trader' and becomes subject to special margin requirements, historically a $25,000 minimum equity requirement.
    Intuition: If you day trade frequently with borrowed money, regulators make you keep a serious cash cushion in the account so a bad day doesn't sink the broker too.
    Mechanics: Day trades are counted per margin account across a rolling five-business-day window; once designated, the account historically had to maintain at least $25,000 in equity and was limited in day-trading buying power, with violations triggering restrictions to cash-only trading. FINRA has since adopted new intraday margin requirements replacing the former day-trading margin requirements, so the precise margin mechanics are in transition.
    Failure modes: The five-day counting window and per-account application confuse retail traders, who inadvertently trigger the designation; the rule has also been criticized for pushing small accounts toward options, offshore brokers, or cash-account settlement workarounds (good faith violations) rather than reducing risk.
    Misconceptions: The rule is a margin requirement, not a ban on day trading, and it does not apply to cash accounts (which face separate free-riding/settlement constraints); holding a position overnight and selling the next day is not a day trade.
    Example: A trader buys and sells AAPL three times on Monday and does one more round trip on Thursday in her margin account; with four day trades in five business days she is designated a pattern day trader and must meet the applicable minimum equity rules to keep day trading.
    Designation trigger: day trades >= 4 within 5 business days in a margin account; classic requirement: minimum account equity >= $25,000.
    Aliases: PDT rule

    Wash-sale rule reviewed #

    The U.S. tax rule (IRC section 1091, detailed in IRS Publication 550) that disallows a loss deduction when stock or securities are sold at a loss and substantially identical stock or securities are bought (or an option or contract to buy them is acquired) within 30 days before or after the sale; the disallowed loss is added to the basis of the replacement position.
    Intuition: You can't sell a stock for a tax loss on Friday and buy it right back on Monday — the IRS treats that as never having really sold, so the loss waits until you truly exit.
    Mechanics: The 61-day window runs from 30 days before to 30 days after the loss sale. If triggered, the disallowed loss is added to the cost basis of the new shares, and the holding period of the old shares tacks onto the new, deferring (not destroying) the loss. Acquisitions by a spouse, a controlled corporation, or into an IRA also trigger the rule — with the IRA variant permanently forfeiting the loss.
    Failure modes: The rule is often triggered accidentally by dividend reinvestment plans, rebalancing across taxable and retirement accounts, or algorithmic tax-loss harvesting that re-buys too soon; 'substantially identical' is undefined for many instruments (e.g., different share classes, options), creating gray areas.
    Misconceptions: The rule does not make the loss vanish in a taxable account — it defers the loss via basis adjustment (the IRA exception is the permanent trap); and it applies only to losses — gains realized and immediately re-established are simply taxed, not deferred.
    Example: You buy 100 shares of X for $1,000, sell them for $750, and repurchase 100 shares two weeks later for $800; the $250 loss is disallowed and your basis in the new shares becomes $1,050.
    Adjusted basis of replacement shares = purchase price of replacement shares + disallowed wash-sale loss; window = sale date ± 30 days.
    Aliases: wash sale (tax), IRC section 1091

    Capital gain reviewed #

    The profit realized when a capital asset — such as stocks or bonds held as investments — is sold for more than its adjusted basis; per the IRS, a capital gain is the excess of the amount realized on sale over the asset's adjusted basis. Gains are classified as short-term (asset held one year or less) or long-term (held more than one year), with long-term net gains generally taxed at preferential rates.
    Intuition: Buy low, sell high — the positive difference between what you paid (your basis) and what you sold for is your capital gain, and the tax rate depends on how long you held it.
    Mechanics: Gain is computed per disposition as amount realized minus adjusted basis (generally cost, with adjustments). Short-term gains net against short-term losses and are taxed as ordinary income; long-term gains net against long-term losses and most net long-term gain is taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on taxable income, with special rates for collectibles and certain real-estate gain. Sales are reported on Form 8949 and summarized on Schedule D.
    Failure modes: Basis errors (neglecting reinvested dividends, wash-sale adjustments, corporate actions) produce misreported gains; the one-year holding boundary and trade-date settlement conventions cause classification mistakes, and unrealized gains create no tax event until disposition.
    Misconceptions: A capital gain is not income received while holding the asset — dividends and interest are separate; nor are all investment gains 'capital' — dealer property, inventory, and certain hedging transactions produce ordinary income regardless of price appreciation.
    Example: You buy 100 shares at $30 ($3,000 basis) and sell them 14 months later at $45 ($4,500 realized): the $1,500 long-term capital gain is taxed at the preferential long-term rate for your income bracket.
    Capital gain = amount realized - adjusted basis (when amount realized > adjusted basis); long-term if holding period > 1 year, short-term if <= 1 year.

    Capital loss reviewed #

    The loss realized when a capital asset is sold for less than its adjusted basis; per the IRS, you have a capital loss if you sell the asset for less than your adjusted basis. Capital losses offset capital gains, and if losses exceed gains, up to $3,000 ($1,500 if married filing separately) of the excess is deductible against ordinary income per year, with the remainder carried forward.
    Intuition: Sell for less than you paid and you have a capital loss — painful, but it can shrink your tax bill by canceling out gains and a limited slice of ordinary income each year.
    Mechanics: Losses are classified short-term or long-term by the same one-year holding rule as gains, then netted within and across categories on Schedule D. Net losses above the annual $3,000/$1,500 limit carry forward to later years indefinitely; losses on personal-use property (home, car) are not deductible, and losses on investment property repurchased within the 30-day window are deferred by the wash-sale rule.
    Failure modes: Taxpayers lose deductions by triggering wash sales, misclassifying holding periods, or failing to track carryovers across years; losses between related parties can be disallowed entirely, and the $3,000 annual cap means large realized losses may take many years to absorb.
    Misconceptions: Capital losses cannot generally offset ordinary income beyond $3,000 per year — a $100,000 net loss does not wipe out $100,000 of salary; and losses on personal-use assets like your car or main home are never deductible.
    Example: You sell stock bought for $10,000 for $6,000, realizing a $4,000 capital loss; with no gains that year you deduct $3,000 against ordinary income and carry $1,000 forward to the next year.
    Capital loss = adjusted basis - amount realized (when amount realized < adjusted basis); annual ordinary-income offset limit = lesser of $3,000 ($1,500 MFS) or total net loss.
