    Base currency reviewed #

    The first currency in a currency-pair quotation, i.e. the currency being priced. The quoted exchange rate expresses how many units of the quote (counter) currency are needed to buy one unit of the base currency, as in EUR/USD 1.2500 meaning one euro exchanges for 1.25 US dollars, with EUR as the base.
    Intuition: The base currency is the 'thing being bought or sold' in an FX quote; the number tells you its price in the other currency.
    Mechanics: Pairs are written by concatenating the ISO 4217 codes of the base and quote currencies (e.g. EUR/USD, sometimes EURUSD or EUR-USD). Market convention fixes a priority ranking of major currencies (EUR > GBP > AUD > USD > CAD > CHF > JPY) that determines which currency appears as the base; buying the pair means buying the base and selling the quote.
    Failure modes: The term is ambiguous: it can also denote a firm's or bank's functional (accounting) currency, so some desks use the unambiguous labels CCY1/CCY2 instead. Misreading which currency is the base inverts profit/loss and risk calculations.
    Misconceptions: A common error is assuming the domestic currency is always the base; the base is set by market quotation convention, not by the trader's location.
    Example: In GBP/USD 1.2700, GBP is the base currency: buying 100,000 GBP/USD means buying 100,000 pounds and paying 127,000 US dollars.
    Pair quote Q = units of quote currency per 1 unit of base currency; base amount = quote amount / Q.
    Aliases: transaction currency, currency 1 (CCY1)
    Related:
    • Quote currency
    • Cross rate
    • Pip
    • Direct quotation
    Source: Wikipedia — Currency pair — lead and 'Syntax and quotation'
    Note: Cited source is a high-quality secondary reference; no official rulebook defines retail FX quotation conventions. Definition follows standard market practice (ISO 4217 pair notation).

    Quote currency reviewed #

    The second currency in a currency-pair quotation, i.e. the currency in which the price of one unit of the base currency is expressed. In EUR/USD 1.2500, USD is the quote (counter) currency and the rate states that 1 euro is exchanged for 1.25 US dollars.
    Intuition: The quote currency is the 'money you pay with' in the pair — the quoted number is an amount of it.
    Mechanics: The pair price Q is measured in quote-currency units per base-currency unit. Profit and loss on an FX position accrues in the quote currency and is then converted to the account currency; the quote currency's interest rate enters forward pricing through covered interest parity.
    Failure modes: Confusing base and quote currencies inverts the meaning of an appreciation and of position P&L; the CCY1/CCY2 convention exists partly to remove this ambiguity.
    Misconceptions: It is wrong to think the quote currency is 'always the dollar'; the dollar is the quote currency in EUR/USD but the base currency in USD/JPY.
    Example: If EUR/USD rises from 1.2500 to 1.2600, it takes more of the quote currency (USD) to buy one euro, so the euro (base) has appreciated against the dollar (quote).
    Quote-currency amount = base-currency amount x Q, where Q is the quoted pair rate.
    Aliases: counter currency, terms currency, currency 2 (CCY2)
    Related:
    • Base currency
    • Cross rate
    • Interest rate parity
    Source: Wikipedia — Currency pair — lead and 'Syntax and quotation'
    Note: Cited source is a high-quality secondary reference; no official rulebook defines retail FX quotation conventions.

    Direct quotation reviewed #

    An exchange rate quotation expressed from the home country's perspective as the number of units of home currency per one unit of foreign currency. For example, from the euro area's perspective, EUR 0.8989 = USD 1.00 is a direct quotation; it is the convention used in most countries.
    Intuition: Direct quotation treats the foreign currency as the 'good' and states its price in your own money.
    Mechanics: Under direct quotation the home currency is the price (quote) currency and the foreign currency is the unit (base). If the home currency appreciates, the quoted number falls (fewer home units per foreign unit); if the home currency depreciates, the number rises.
    Failure modes: Whether a quote is 'direct' depends entirely on the observer's home currency, so the label is meaningless in cross-border communication unless the perspective is stated; mixing conventions flips the interpretation of rate moves.
    Misconceptions: A rising number under direct quotation is often misread as the home currency strengthening; it actually means the home currency is weakening (more home units are needed per foreign unit).
    Example: For a US-based observer, USD 1.11 = EUR 1.00 would be an indirect quote, whereas EUR 0.90 = USD 1.00 is the direct quote used from a euro-area perspective.
    Direct rate = home-currency units per 1 foreign-currency unit = 1 / (indirect rate).
    Aliases: price quotation
    Related:
    • Indirect quotation
    • Base currency
    • Quote currency
    Source: Wikipedia — Exchange rate — Quotations
    Note: Cited source is a high-quality secondary reference; quotation-convention terminology is standard in international finance textbooks but not codified by any regulator.

    Indirect quotation reviewed #

    An exchange rate quotation expressed from the home country's perspective as the number of units of foreign currency per one unit of home currency. For example, from the euro area's perspective, USD 1.11 = EUR 1.00 is an indirect quotation; the convention is common in the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand and the euro area.
    Intuition: Indirect quotation states how much foreign money one unit of your own currency buys.
    Mechanics: Under indirect quotation the home currency is the unit (base) currency and the foreign currency is the price (quote) currency. It is the reciprocal of the direct quotation, so a rising number under indirect quotation means the home currency is appreciating.
    Failure modes: Because the same numerical rate can be 'direct' for one party and 'indirect' for the other, reports that omit the perspective invite sign errors in appreciation/depreciation statements.
    Misconceptions: Traders sometimes believe one convention is the global standard; in practice both coexist, and only market pair conventions (e.g. EUR/USD, USD/JPY) are standardized, not the direct/indirect label.
    Example: British newspapers quote cable as USD per GBP (e.g. GBP 1 = USD 1.27); for a UK resident that is an indirect quotation, and a rise to 1.30 means sterling has strengthened.
    Indirect rate = foreign-currency units per 1 home-currency unit = 1 / (direct rate).
    Aliases: quantity quotation, reciprocal quotation
    Related:
    • Direct quotation
    • Base currency
    • Quote currency

    Pip reviewed #

    The conventional smallest whole-unit increment of an FX exchange rate quote. For most major currency pairs (priced to four decimal places) one pip is one unit of the fourth decimal place (0.0001); for yen pairs one pip is one unit of the second decimal place (0.01).
    Intuition: A pip is the FX market's standard 'cent' for talking about exchange-rate moves and spreads.
    Mechanics: Price changes, bid-ask spreads and forward points are quoted in pips. The monetary value of a pip in quote-currency terms equals the pip size multiplied by the position size in base-currency units; a pip is distinct from the tick size, which in a given market may be a fraction (e.g. 1/2 pip) or multiple of a pip.
    Failure modes: The four-decimal convention breaks down for pairs quoted with more or fewer decimals (e.g. JPY pairs at 0.01, or pairs priced to five decimals), so 'pip' must be re-anchored per pair; confusing pips with basis points or ticks misstates spread and P&L figures.
    Misconceptions: A pip is not a universal 0.01% move in the exchange rate — its percentage value varies with the level of the rate and the pair's decimal convention.
    Example: If EUR/USD moves from 1.3000 to 1.3010, the pair has risen 10 pips; on a 500,000 EUR position that move is worth 500,000 x 0.0010 = USD 500.
    Pip value (quote currency) = pip size x position size (base units); pip size = 0.0001 for most pairs, 0.01 for JPY pairs.
    Aliases: percentage in point, price interest point
    Related:
    • Pipette
    • Forward points
    • Base currency
    • Quote currency
    Source: Wikipedia — Percentage in point — lead and 'Example'
    Note: Pip is a market convention term, not a regulatory concept; primary sources (BIS, exchanges) do not define it, so a secondary reference is cited.

    Pipette reviewed #

    One tenth of a pip, i.e. the additional decimal place (the fifth decimal for most pairs, the third for yen pairs) that electronic FX platforms added to quotes. Pipettes allow finer pricing than the traditional four-decimal convention.
    Intuition: A pipette is the extra digit beyond the pip that lets platforms quote spreads in tenths of a pip.
    Mechanics: Electronic trading platforms extended quote precision by one decimal place, displaying rates in 1/10-pip increments. In a five-decimal EUR/USD quote such as 1.30105, the last digit (5) counts pipettes; tighter spreads from electronic price competition made this finer granularity necessary.
    Failure modes: Platforms differ in how many decimals they display, so 'points' on one platform may be pips on another; comparing spreads across venues without checking decimal conventions misstates trading costs.
    Misconceptions: A pipette is not a new minimum tick imposed by any authority — it emerged from platform pricing practice and is not uniformly adopted.
    Example: If EUR/USD is quoted 1.30105/1.30115, the spread is 1 pipette (0.1 pip), one tenth of the traditional 1-pip minimum increment.
    1 pipette = 0.1 pip = 0.00001 for most pairs (0.001 for JPY pairs).
    Aliases: fractional pip, point (tenth of a pip)
    Related:
    • Pip
    • FX spot
    Source: Wikipedia — Percentage in point — Fractional pips
    Note: Pipette is a platform-convention term with no official definition; cited secondary source documents the 1/10-pip fractional-pip convention.

    FX spot reviewed #

    A transaction for the exchange of two currencies at a rate agreed on the trade date for settlement on the standard spot value date, normally two business days later (T+2). Spot is one of the core OTC FX instrument categories in the BIS Triennial Survey and accounted for 28% of the $7.5 trillion average daily global FX turnover in April 2022.
    Intuition: A spot FX deal is 'buy currency now at today's rate', with the actual exchange of funds settling a couple of business days later.
    Mechanics: Counterparties agree a rate and amounts on the trade date; the two currency legs are delivered on the spot value date (T+2 for most major pairs, T+1 for some such as USD/CAD). Spot transactions settle delivery-versus-payment through systems like CLS for eligible currencies and form the price base from which forwards and swaps are derived.
    Failure modes: Spot quotes embed the T+2 settlement convention, so around holidays the actual value date shifts; settlement (Herstatt) risk exists for non-CLS-settled currencies, and spot prices can gap over weekends when the OTC market is closed.
    Misconceptions: 'Spot' does not mean immediate same-day settlement — the standard is T+2 — and spot FX is overwhelmingly an OTC interbank/dealer market, not an exchange-traded one.
    Example: On Monday a corporate treasurer buys EUR 1 million against USD at 1.0850; the euros and dollars are exchanged on Wednesday, the T+2 spot value date.
    Aliases: spot foreign exchange, spot FX transaction
    Related:

    FX forward reviewed #

    An over-the-counter contract to exchange one currency for another at a rate fixed today for settlement on a specific future date beyond the spot value date. Outright forwards are used mainly to hedge future currency payments and receipts, and accounted for 15% of global OTC FX turnover in April 2022.
    Intuition: An FX forward locks in today the exchange rate for a currency trade you will make weeks or months from now.
    Mechanics: The forward rate equals the spot rate adjusted by forward points reflecting the interest rate differential between the two currencies over the contract tenor (covered interest parity). A single outright forward involves one exchange of principals at maturity; non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) settle only the net value difference in a convertible currency. Unlike futures, forwards are bilateral OTC contracts and carry counterparty risk.
    Failure modes: Forward rates are a no-arbitrage pricing output, not forecasts of future spot rates; since 2008 persistent cross-currency basis deviations mean forward pricing also embeds balance-sheet costs. Bilateral forwards carry counterparty credit risk absent in centrally cleared instruments.
    Misconceptions: A forward rate is not the market's expected future exchange rate; it is mechanically derived from the spot rate and money-market rates.
    Example: A US importer owing EUR 10 million in six months buys a 6-month EUR/USD forward at 1.0920; whatever the spot rate is at maturity, the euros cost exactly USD 10.92 million.
    F = S x (1 + r_q x T) / (1 + r_b x T), where F = forward rate, S = spot rate (quote per base), r_q = quote-currency interest rate, r_b = base-currency interest rate, T = tenor in years under the applicable day-count convention.
    Aliases: outright forward, forward outright

    FX swap reviewed #

    A contract combining a spot purchase (sale) of one currency against another with a simultaneous offsetting forward sale (purchase) of the same amounts. In the spot leg one party lends one currency and borrows the other at the spot rate; in the forward leg the exchange is reversed at a pre-agreed forward rate, making the instrument economically similar to collateralized borrowing and lending. FX swaps were the most traded FX instrument, at $3.8 trillion per day (51% of global turnover) in April 2022.
    Intuition: An FX swap is like a repo with a currency instead of a security: you swap currencies today and swap them back later at a pre-set rate.
    Mechanics: Because the two legs offset, an FX swap creates no net open currency position; its price is the difference between the forward and spot legs, quoted in forward (swap) points that reflect the interest rate differential plus any cross-currency basis. Typical maturities are very short — instruments maturing within a week accounted for about 70% of FX swap turnover in April 2022 — and the instrument is used for funding liquidity management, hedging and rolling forward positions.
    Failure modes: FX swap markets are vulnerable to funding squeezes — during the 2008 crisis and March 2020 dollar borrowers faced sharply higher rates when lenders stepped back. Persistent deviations from covered interest parity (the cross-currency basis) mean swap-implied funding costs can differ materially from cash-market rates.
    Misconceptions: An FX swap is not a bet on the exchange rate (the legs offset, so there is no open FX position), and it differs from a cross-currency swap, a longer-term instrument that also exchanges periodic interest payments.
    Example: A Dutch pension fund borrows dollars and lends euros in the spot leg of a 3-month FX swap, then repays the dollars and receives back euros in the forward leg at the pre-agreed rate, hedging its dollar assets without an open currency position.
    Swap cost is quoted as forward points = F - S, with F = S x (1 + r_q x T) / (1 + r_b x T) under covered interest parity; S = spot, F = forward rate, r_q and r_b = quote- and base-currency interest rates, T = tenor.
    Aliases: forex swap

    Currency carry trade reviewed #

    A strategy of borrowing (funding) in a low-interest-rate currency and investing in a high-interest-rate currency without hedging the exchange-rate exposure, aiming to earn the interest rate differential. Its profitability violates uncovered interest parity, and research documents that carry-trade returns are negatively skewed — subject to sudden crash risk when funding liquidity falls and positions unwind en masse.
    Intuition: Collect the interest-rate gap between two currencies and hope the exchange rate doesn't move against you before you exit.
    Mechanics: The trader shorts the funding currency (e.g. a low-yielder) and goes long the investment currency, earning the rate differential as 'carry' plus or minus any exchange-rate change. Because UIP predicts the high-rate currency should depreciate by the differential, persistent carry profits are a pricing anomaly; unwind episodes cluster when risk appetite and funding liquidity decline, producing negatively skewed, crash-prone returns and excess co-movement among currencies with similar interest rates.
    Failure modes: Carry trades crash during liquidity squeezes and risk-off episodes: sudden unwinding produces large depreciations of high-yield currencies (negative skewness), e.g. 2008. Losses can exceed years of accumulated carry, and funding-liquidity constraints force deleveraging exactly when prices gap.
    Misconceptions: The interest differential is not a free lunch: it is compensation for crash risk, not a market inefficiency that can be harvested risklessly; carry strategies are not reliably profitable on a risk-adjusted basis.
    Example: Borrowing Japanese yen at a near-zero rate to buy Australian dollars yielding several percent more earns the differential while AUD/JPY is stable, but a sharp yen rally during a global risk-off episode can wipe out the position.
    Approximate carry-trade return = (r_invest - r_funding) + percentage change in the exchange rate (investment currency vs funding currency) over the holding period.
    Aliases: carry trade, FX carry
    Related:
    • Uncovered interest parity
    • Interest rate parity
    • FX spot
    Source: NBER Working Paper 14473 — Carry Trades and Currency Crashes (Brunnermeier, Nagel, Pedersen) — abstract

    Forward points reviewed #

    The difference between the forward exchange rate and the spot exchange rate for a given tenor, i.e. the adjustment added to or subtracted from the spot rate to obtain the outright forward rate. FX swaps are typically quoted in forward points rather than outright rates.
    Intuition: Forward points are the 'time value' of a currency pair — how much the forward rate differs from spot because of the interest-rate gap.
    Mechanics: Under covered interest parity, forward points are approximately proportional to the interest rate differential between the two currencies times the tenor; when the quote-currency interest rate exceeds the base-currency rate, points are positive (forward above spot), and vice versa. Deviations from the parity-implied level appear as the cross-currency basis. Points are expressed in pips of the exchange rate.
    Failure modes: Forward points reflect relative funding costs, not expected currency moves; since the 2008 crisis, balance-sheet constraints and hedging demand have driven persistent non-zero bases, so realized points can diverge from the simple interest-differential formula.
    Misconceptions: Positive forward points do not mean the market expects the base currency to appreciate; they mainly encode the interest differential between the two currencies.
    Example: With NZD/USD spot at 0.8325 and 1-year forward points quoted at -270 pips, the outright 1-year forward rate is 0.8055 (= 0.8325 - 0.0270).
    Forward points = F - S = S x [(1 + r_q x T)/(1 + r_b x T) - 1] ~= S x (r_q - r_b) x T for small r_b x T; S = spot, F = forward, r_q and r_b = quote- and base-currency rates, T = tenor in years.
    Aliases: swap points, FX points
    Related:

    Forward premium reviewed #

    The situation in which a currency's forward exchange rate is above its spot rate, i.e. the currency trades at a premium for future delivery (equivalently, positive forward points). Under covered interest parity, a currency trades at a forward premium when its interest rate is below that of the other currency in the pair.
    Intuition: A currency at a forward premium costs more in the forward market than in the spot market — the mirror image of its lower interest rate.
    Mechanics: With the exchange rate quoted as quote currency per base currency, positive forward points (F > S) mean the base currency is at a forward premium and the quote currency at a forward discount. The size of the premium, annualized as (F - S)/S, is tied by covered interest parity to the interest differential, plus any cross-currency basis.
    Failure modes: The premium is an arithmetic consequence of interest differentials and carries no information about expected appreciation; treating it as a bullish signal is an error. Basis deviations can also make the observed premium differ from the parity-implied value.
    Misconceptions: A forward premium does not indicate that the market expects the currency to strengthen — under CIP it simply compensates for the currency's lower interest rate.
    Example: If the yen's interest rate is below the dollar's and USD/JPY spot is 150.00 with a 1-year forward of 146.50, the dollar is at a forward discount and the yen at a forward premium of roughly 2.3% annualized.
    Annualized forward premium = (F - S) / S x (360 / days to maturity); F = forward rate, S = spot rate.

    Forward discount reviewed #

    The situation in which a currency's forward exchange rate is below its spot rate, i.e. the currency trades at a discount for future delivery (equivalently, negative forward points for that currency). Under covered interest parity, a currency trades at a forward discount when its interest rate is above that of the other currency in the pair.
    Intuition: A currency at a forward discount costs less forward than spot — the arithmetic counterpart of its higher interest rate.
    Mechanics: With the rate quoted as quote currency per base currency, negative forward points (F < S) mean the base currency is at a forward discount and the quote currency at a forward premium. The annualized discount (F - S)/S is tied by covered interest parity to the interest differential, plus any cross-currency basis. High-yielding (carry target) currencies normally trade at a forward discount.
    Failure modes: The discount is not a forecast of depreciation; uncovered interest parity — which would turn the discount into an expected spot change — fails empirically, and high-rate currencies often appreciate instead, which is the basis of the carry trade.
    Misconceptions: Selling a currency 'because it is at a forward discount' double-counts the interest differential: hedging a high-yielder via forwards surrenders exactly the carry that the discount reflects.
    Example: If USD/MXN spot is 18.00 and Mexican rates exceed US rates, the 1-year forward might be 19.10: the peso is at a forward discount (it buys fewer dollars forward than spot).
    Annualized forward discount = (F - S) / S x (360 / days to maturity) < 0; F = forward rate, S = spot rate.
    Related:

    Interest rate parity reviewed #

    The family of equilibrium conditions linking spot and forward exchange rates to interest rate differentials between two currencies. It comprises covered interest parity — a no-arbitrage condition tying the forward premium to the interest differential — and uncovered interest parity, which relates the interest differential to the expected change in the spot rate.
    Intuition: Money should earn the same total return wherever it is parked once currency effects are accounted for; IRP is the statement of that idea, hedged (covered) or unhedged (uncovered).
    Mechanics: In its covered form, borrowing in one currency, converting at spot, investing in the other, and locking in the conversion back with a forward must yield no riskless profit, forcing F/S to equal the ratio of gross interest returns. The uncovered form replaces the forward rate with the expected future spot rate and is a hypothesis about expectations rather than a pure arbitrage condition. Spot/forward pricing of FX swaps is read off these parity relations.
    Failure modes: CIP has been systematically violated since 2008 (persistent cross-currency basis) because of hedging demand and limits to arbitrage from bank balance-sheet constraints. UIP fails empirically as well: high-interest-rate currencies tend to appreciate rather than depreciate, the 'forward premium puzzle'.
    Misconceptions: IRP is not a law that markets obey at all times; only the covered version is arbitrage-enforced, and even it has broken down persistently since the financial crisis.
    Example: If 1-year rates are 5% in USD and 1% in JPY, CIP requires the 1-year USD/JPY forward to trade roughly 4% below spot; a materially different forward would open a covered arbitrage.
    Covered: F/S = (1 + r_q x T)/(1 + r_b x T). Uncovered: E[S_T]/S = (1 + r_q x T)/(1 + r_b x T), with S and F in quote currency per base currency, r_q and r_b the two interest rates, T the tenor.
    Aliases: IRP
    Related:
    • Covered interest parity
    • Uncovered interest parity
    • Forward points
    • FX swap

    Covered interest parity reviewed #

    The no-arbitrage condition that interest rates on two otherwise identical assets in different currencies must be equal once the foreign currency risk is hedged in the forward market: the interest rate differential should equal the differential between the forward and spot exchange rates. Often described as the closest thing to a physical law in international finance, it has been systematically violated since the 2008 financial crisis, visible in a persistent cross-currency basis.
    Intuition: If you fully hedge the currency risk, borrowing dollars to invest in yen (or vice versa) should earn nothing extra — CIP is what makes that true.
    Mechanics: If CIP fails, an arbitrageur can borrow the cheaper currency in the cash market, swap it via an FX swap (sell spot, repurchase forward), and lock in a riskless profit; such trades should close the gap. Post-crisis, CIP deviations persist because FX hedging demand (from banks, institutional investors and bond issuers) meets limits to arbitrage: arbitrageurs' balance-sheet space is costly, so the basis is not competed away. The deviation is measured by the cross-currency basis — the amount by which swap-implied funding costs differ from cash-market rates.
    Failure modes: CIP breaks down when arbitrage is balance-sheet-constrained: during the GFC, the euro-area crisis, and persistently since 2014, the cross-currency basis has stayed non-zero, with quarter-end spikes reflecting regulatory reporting constraints. Deviations mean synthetic dollar funding via FX swaps can be costlier (or cheaper) than direct funding.
    Misconceptions: CIP is not enforced by nature — it requires arbitrageurs with free balance-sheet capacity, so 'riskless' CIP arbitrage in practice consumes capital, carries counterparty and liquidity risk, and may not be executable at scale.
    Example: If borrowing dollars via a USD/JPY FX swap costs 25 bp more than borrowing dollars directly in the cash market, the dollar is said to command a premium in swaps — a CIP violation measured by the negative cross-currency basis.
    (1 + r) = (F/S) x (1 + r*), where S = spot and F = forward in US dollars per foreign currency, r = USD interest rate, r* = foreign interest rate (BIS Box A convention).
    Aliases: CIP, covered interest rate parity

    Uncovered interest parity reviewed #

    The hypothesis that the interest rate differential between two currencies equals the expected change in their exchange rate, so that the higher-yielding currency is expected to depreciate just enough to offset its yield advantage for an unhedged investor. Empirically UIP fails: high-interest-rate currencies tend to appreciate rather than depreciate, a puzzle that underlies carry-trade profitability.
    Intuition: UIP says there's no free lunch from chasing higher foreign interest rates — the exchange rate should move to cancel the extra yield. In practice it often doesn't.
    Mechanics: Unlike covered parity, UIP cannot be enforced by arbitrage because the future spot rate is unknown; it is a joint hypothesis about expectations and risk neutrality. Research links its failure to funding-liquidity constraints on carry traders: exchange-rate moves between high- and low-rate currencies are negatively skewed, and controlling for liquidity helps explain the uncovered interest-rate puzzle.
    Failure modes: UIP fails systematically (the forward premium puzzle): since the 1990s high-rate currencies characteristically appreciated, so unhedged carry positions earned excess returns punctuated by crash episodes when funding liquidity dried up.
    Misconceptions: UIP is not an arbitrage condition that 'must' hold — it is a falsifiable expectations hypothesis, and treating the forward rate as an unbiased forecast of the future spot rate is empirically wrong.
    Example: If Australian rates exceed Japanese rates by 4%, UIP predicts the AUD should depreciate about 4% against the JPY over the year; historically AUD/JPY instead appreciated for long stretches, rewarding carry traders until sharp reversals.
    E[S_T] / S = (1 + r_q x T) / (1 + r_b x T), equivalently the expected percentage change in the spot rate ~= r_b - r_q over the tenor; S = spot in quote per base currency.
    Aliases: UIP, uncovered interest rate parity, UIRP

    Cross rate reviewed #

    An exchange rate between two currencies that does not directly involve the home reference currency — in market practice, a pair not involving the US dollar (e.g. EUR/GBP, GBP/JPY). Because most currencies are quoted primarily against the dollar, cross rates are typically derived by multiplying or dividing the two currencies' dollar rates.
    Intuition: A cross rate is the exchange rate between two 'non-anchor' currencies, usually computed through their rates against the dollar.
    Mechanics: The derived (implicit) cross rate is obtained as S(a/b) = S(a/$) x S($/b) using the quoted dollar pairs. Dealers quote liquid crosses directly, but the bid-ask of the implicit cross disciplines market makers: if a directly quoted cross diverges from the implicit value, triangular arbitrage becomes possible. The dollar's vehicle-currency role (on one side of 88% of all FX trades in 2022) is why crosses route through it.
    Failure modes: Derived crosses inherit the spreads and stale prices of both dollar legs, so in fast or illiquid markets the implicit cross can be wrong; thin crosses have wide spreads and limited depth compared with dollar pairs.
    Misconceptions: Cross rates are not independent prices: arbitrage keeps them aligned with the constituent dollar pairs, so a 'mispriced cross' is usually just a fleeting inconsistency, not a distinct market view.
    Example: With EUR/USD at 1.0850 and USD/JPY at 150.00, the implicit EUR/JPY cross is 1.0850 x 150.00 = 162.75.
    S(a/b) = S(a/$) x S($/b); for quotes sharing the same base, divide: EUR/GBP = EUR/USD / GBP/USD.
    Aliases: cross rate, currency cross, cross

    FX triangular arbitrage reviewed #

    The practice of exploiting a pricing discrepancy among three currencies by executing three trades — exchanging an initial currency for a second, the second for a third, and the third back into the initial — when the quoted cross exchange rate diverges from the implicit cross rate derived from the other two pairs. High-frequency studies find such opportunities are rare, small (often ~1 basis point) and fleeting (mostly lasting seconds or less).
    Intuition: If EUR/USD and USD/JPY imply one EUR/JPY rate but a bank quotes another, you can cycle through the three pairs and end up with more than you started.
    Mechanics: The arbitrageur computes the implicit cross rate S(a/b) = S(a/$) x S($/b) from quoted rates and compares it with the market cross quote, accounting for bid and ask on each leg. A profitable cycle exists only if the discrepancy exceeds transaction costs; electronic trading systems detect and exhaust such gaps in milliseconds, which is why opportunity incidence fell as algorithmic trading spread.
    Failure modes: Execution risk dominates: even millisecond delays between identifying the gap and filling all three legs mean one leg can re-price, leaving the arbitrageur with an unwanted position and a close-out cost equal to the price move that killed the gap. Fees, latency and competition from faster systems erode the thin margins.
    Misconceptions: Triangular arbitrage is not an easy retail strategy: executable opportunities at sizes above a basis point or two are essentially competed away by professional low-latency systems before slower participants can act.
    Example: If dealers quote EUR/USD 0.8171 (euros per dollar) and GBP/USD 1.4650, the implicit EUR/GBP is 1.1971; a bank offering pounds at EUR 1.1910 lets an arbitrageur cycle $5m through the three pairs for about $25,400 profit before costs.
    Implicit cross rate S(a/b) = S(a/$) x S($/b); a no-arbitrage condition requires the quoted cross to equal the implicit cross (within bid-ask bounds).
    Aliases: triangular arbitrage, cross currency arbitrage, three-point arbitrage

    Dollar index reviewed #

    A proprietary index, designed and published by ICE Data Indices, measuring the value of the US dollar as a weighted geometric mean against a fixed basket of six currencies: euro (57.6%), yen (13.6%), pound sterling (11.9%), Canadian dollar (9.1%), Swedish krona (4.2%) and Swiss franc (3.6%). Set at 100.000 at its March 1973 start, it rises when the dollar strengthens against the basket; futures on the index trade on ICE.
    Intuition: DXY is the standard single number summarizing how strong the dollar is overall against other major currencies.
    Mechanics: The index is a geometric average with fixed weights applied to the six bilateral rates, with EUR and GBP entering with negative exponents (they are quoted as dollars per unit of currency) and the others with positive exponents. The basket composition has been revised only once, when euro-area currencies were subsumed by the euro in 1999; the Federal Reserve's broader trade-weighted dollar index is a separate measure.
    Failure modes: The 1970s-era basket omits major current US trade partners (China, Mexico, Korea, Brazil) while retaining Sweden and Switzerland, and the euro dominates with a 57.6% weight, so DXY moves often reflect EUR/USD more than broad dollar strength; it is not a trade-representative effective exchange rate.
    Misconceptions: DXY is not an official or trade-weighted measure of the dollar — it is a commercial futures-underlying index — and a rising DXY says little about the dollar against emerging-market currencies.
    Example: A sharp EUR/USD sell-off from 1.10 to 1.05 lifts DXY mechanically because of the euro's 57.6% weight, even if the dollar is flat against most Asian and Latin American currencies.

    Real effective exchange rate reviewed #

    A trade-weighted index of a currency's value against a basket of partner currencies, adjusted for relative price levels — in the BIS methodology, derived by adjusting the nominal effective exchange rate by relative consumer prices. An increase in the REER indicates a real appreciation of the currency, interpreted as a loss of price competitiveness.
    Intuition: The REER tells you whether your currency is really getting more expensive abroad, after accounting for inflation differences — not just what the nominal rate says.
    Mechanics: The BIS computes NEER as a geometric trade-weighted average of bilateral rates and then adjusts by relative consumer price indices to obtain the REER. Weights derive from manufacturing trade flows, double-weighted to capture both direct bilateral trade and third-market competition, and are time-varying on a three-year basis; BIS broad indices cover 64 economies. REER changes thus reflect both nominal exchange-rate moves and the inflation differential against trading partners.
    Failure modes: REER indices are sensitive to the choice of price deflator (CPI vs unit labour costs vs GDP deflator), weighting scheme and base period, so different providers' REERs can give different competitiveness readings; they are indices relative to a base year and carry no absolute 'fair value'.
    Misconceptions: A rising REER does not by itself prove overvaluation or predict depreciation — it is a competitiveness statistic relative to a chosen base period, not an equilibrium benchmark.
    Example: If a country's nominal effective rate is flat but its inflation runs well above its trading partners', its BIS REER appreciates, signalling eroding export price competitiveness.
    REER = NEER adjusted by relative consumer prices: REER_t ~ Product over i of [S_i,t x (P_t / P_i,t*)]^(w_i), where S_i are bilateral rates, w_i trade weights (sum to 1), P home and P_i* partner consumer price indices.
    Aliases: REER

    Nominal effective exchange rate reviewed #

    A trade-weighted index of a currency's nominal value against a basket of trading-partner currencies — in the BIS methodology, calculated as a geometric trade-weighted average of bilateral exchange rates. An increase in the NEER indicates a nominal appreciation of the currency; it is the building block from which the real effective exchange rate is derived by adjusting for relative prices.
    Intuition: The NEER collapses a currency's many bilateral exchange rates into one number weighted by how much you trade with each partner.
    Mechanics: Bilateral rates are combined as a weighted geometric mean using weights derived from manufacturing trade flows, double-weighted to capture direct bilateral trade and third-market competition; weights are time-varying on a three-year basis. BIS broad NEER indices cover 64 economies (narrow indices 26) and use published US dollar exchange rates as inputs where possible.
    Failure modes: NEER ignores inflation differentials, so a country with high inflation can show a stable NEER while losing real competitiveness; results also depend on the weighting scheme and base period, limiting cross-provider comparability.
    Misconceptions: The NEER is not a forecast or an equilibrium measure — it is a descriptive weighted average, and its level is meaningful only relative to the index base year.
    Example: If the euro appreciates against the dollar and yen but weakens against sterling, the BIS euro NEER moves by the trade-weighted net of those changes rather than by any single pair.
    NEER_t = Product over i of S_i,t^(w_i), where S_i,t is the bilateral exchange-rate index against partner i and w_i are trade weights summing to 1 (geometric trade-weighted average).

    Value date reviewed #

    The date on which the counterparties to a foreign exchange trade actually exchange the funds, i.e. when the traded amounts become effective (delivered). For a spot FX transaction the value date is normally two business days after the trade is agreed (T+2); for forwards and swaps it is the agreed future delivery date of each leg.
    Intuition: The trade date is when you agree the deal; the value date is when the money actually changes hands.
    Mechanics: The value date determines when interest accrues and when currency deliveries occur; it rolls forward over weekends and currency holidays under modified-following conventions. Interest rate differentials between the two currencies are earned or paid for each day a position's value date is shifted, which is what tom-next and other short-dated swaps price. Forward rates are derived by adjusting the spot rate for the interest carry between the spot value date and the forward value date.
    Failure modes: Holidays in either currency's center push the value date and change the number of interest days, so quotes around month-ends, year-ends and holidays embed extra carry; assuming a fixed 'T+2 = 2 days of interest' misprices rollovers (e.g. Wednesday spot rolls three days).
    Misconceptions: The value date is not the trade date and not necessarily two calendar days later — it is counted in good business days for both currency centers.
    Example: A EUR/USD spot trade agreed on Thursday 3 August has a T+2 value date of Monday 7 August (skipping the weekend), so the interest carry covers three calendar days.
    Aliases: settlement date (FX context)

    Tom-next swap reviewed #

    A short-dated FX swap used to roll a currency position forward by one day: the holder buys (or sells) a foreign currency amount settling tomorrow and simultaneously does the opposite transaction settling the day after (the spot value date). Institutions use tom-next swaps at the end of each day to close out and re-institute foreign balances, thereby collecting or paying the overnight interest differential (cost of carry) without holding an open FX position.
    Intuition: Tom-next is the daily 'roll' that keeps a spot FX position from ever settling, in exchange for paying or receiving one day of the interest-rate gap.
    Mechanics: The near leg settles on the tomorrow date (T+1) and the far leg on the spot date (T+2), shifting the position's value date by one day. The price, in swap points, reflects the one-day interest rate differential between the two currencies (with extra days priced over weekends and holidays); retail platforms implement the same economics as the daily rollover or swap charge on open positions.
    Failure modes: Tom-next pricing turns on overnight rates and day counts, so costs jump over weekends (three days) and quarter/ year-ends when funding rates spike; traders ignoring the roll can see carry economics dominate returns on long-held leveraged positions.
    Misconceptions: A tom-next roll is not a new directional trade and not a fee set arbitrarily by the broker — it is the market-priced interest differential for deferring settlement by one day.
    Example: A desk long EUR/USD over value date Tuesday executes a tom-next swap: it sells EUR settling Wednesday and buys it back settling Thursday, paying or earning one day of the EUR-USD rate differential.
    Tom-next swap points ~ S x (r_q - r_b) x (days / 360 or 365 by convention), where S = spot rate and r_q, r_b = quote- and base-currency overnight rates.
    Aliases: tom-next, tomorrow-next, T/N swap

    Fixing reviewed #

    A reference exchange rate determined at a set time each trading day from market transactions and orders around a fixing window, used to value portfolios, settle contracts and execute customer 'fix orders' at a common published price. The most prominent is the London WM/Reuters 4 p.m. fixing in the interbank forex market.
    Intuition: A fixing is the market's agreed 'official' exchange rate for a given moment each day, so that funds and contracts everywhere can be valued on the same number.
    Mechanics: A benchmark administrator computes the rate from trades and order-book data captured during a short window around the fixing time (the WM/Reuters 4 p.m. window was widened from one to five minutes in the 2015 reform). Banks accept customer orders guaranteed to execute at the fix price, which concentrates volume into the window; investigations found banks had colluded around the window, leading to penalties and the 2015 reform of the London fixing.
    Failure modes: Fixings create manipulation incentives: dealers holding guaranteed fix orders profit by moving the window price, and the 2013-15 FX scandal showed collusive behavior around the London fix. Post-reform, price anomalies around the window and passive execution strategies still generate predictable price patterns.
    Misconceptions: The fix is not a transaction price anyone can always trade at — it is a computed reference from a short window, and large fix orders are typically executed by dealers over time rather than all at the fix print.
    Example: An index fund rebalancing at month-end instructs its bank to execute a large currency conversion 'at the 4 p.m. fix'; the bank's execution and hedging flows concentrate into the fixing window.
    Aliases: FX fixing, fix, benchmark fixing

    London 4 p.m. fix reviewed #

    The daily reference exchange rate computed from market activity in a window around 4:00 p.m. London time, historically administered as the WM/Reuters benchmark. It is the most widely used FX fixing for valuing funds, settling derivatives and executing customer orders, and was the focus of the 2013-15 benchmark-manipulation investigations and the subsequent 2015 reform that widened the fixing window.
    Intuition: The 4 p.m. London fix is the FX market's global 'closing price' — the single rate the world's asset managers use to value and convert currencies.
    Mechanics: The rate is computed from actual trades and order data over a window centred on 4 p.m. London time; the window was lengthened from one minute to five minutes in the 2015 reform to reduce manipulability. Banks accepting client fix orders guarantee execution at the fix price and hedge the resulting exposure, which produces volume spikes and characteristic price dynamics in the window — patterns that persisted in altered form after the reform, as banks shifted to passive execution strategies.
    Failure modes: The pre-2015 one-minute window was manipulable: traders at major banks colluded to push the fix, resulting in multi-billion-dollar fines. Post-reform, the fix still exhibits anomalous price dynamics and heavy month-end volume, so execution quality at the fix can differ systematically from prevailing market prices.
    Misconceptions: The fix is not set in London by a committee of quotes (like old LIBOR) — it is computed from actual market transaction data in the window — and trading 'at the fix' does not guarantee the mid-market price.
    Example: A global equity fund's NAV converts its foreign holdings to dollars each day using the WM/Reuters 4 p.m. rates, so its reported performance is tied to those fix prints.
    Aliases: WM/Reuters 4pm fix, WMR 4pm fix, London fix
