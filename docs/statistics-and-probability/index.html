    Correlation #

    A statistical measure of the strength and direction of association between two variables, expressed as a dimensionless coefficient bounded in [-1, +1]. The most common form is the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient, which measures linear association; rank-based variants (Spearman, Kendall) measure monotonic association.
    Intuition: It tells you whether two series tend to move together (+1), in opposite directions (-1), or without any linear pattern (0). In markets it describes how strongly two assets' returns co-move, which is the statistical basis of diversification.
    Mechanics: For the Pearson form, the covariance of the two variables is normalized by the product of their standard deviations, producing a unit-free coefficient in [-1, 1]. Rank correlations convert the observations to ranks before applying the same computation.
    Failure modes: The Pearson coefficient only captures linear dependence, so r near 0 can coexist with a strong nonlinear relationship, and a single extreme observation can dominate the estimate. Financial return correlations are also unstable over time and tend to jump toward 1 during crises, exactly when diversification is most needed.
    Misconceptions: Correlation does not imply causation: a high correlation between two assets or between an indicator and returns does not mean one drives the other. Nor does a high historical correlation guarantee that the relationship will persist out of sample.
    Example: The daily returns of two large bank stocks over one year have r = 0.82, indicating strong co-movement; combining them in a portfolio delivers far less diversification than pairing a bank with a utility stock at r = 0.15.
    rho(X,Y) = Cov(X,Y) / (sigma_X * sigma_Y); sample estimate r = SUM[(x_i - x_bar)(y_i - y_bar)] / sqrt( SUM(x_i - x_bar)^2 * SUM(y_i - y_bar)^2 ), where x_bar and y_bar are the sample means.
    Aliases: correlation coefficient

    Variance #

    The average of the squared deviations of a variable from its mean; the canonical measure of dispersion around the mean. Population variance divides by the population size N, while the unbiased sample variance divides by n - 1 (Bessel's correction).
    Intuition: It measures how spread out values are around their average, in squared units. Squaring means points far from the mean contribute disproportionately, so variance is dominated by extremes.
    Mechanics: Compute the mean, subtract it from each observation, square the deviations, and average them. Squaring removes sign and amplifies tail observations; the square root of variance (standard deviation) restores the original units.
    Failure modes: Because deviations are squared, variance is highly sensitive to extreme values and tail behavior, and it does not exist for heavy-tailed distributions such as the Cauchy distribution. Its squared units also make raw variance hard to interpret economically.
    Misconceptions: Variance is not identical to 'risk' in general: it treats upside and downside deviations symmetrically and says nothing about the shape or direction of losses. The n - 1 divisor in the sample formula is not arbitrary; it is the correction that makes the sample variance an unbiased estimator of the population variance.
    Example: Daily stock returns of 0.02, -0.01, and 0.03 have mean 0.0133 and sample variance s^2 = [(0.0067)^2 + (-0.0233)^2 + (0.0167)^2] / 2 = 0.000433, so the sample standard deviation is about 0.0208 (2.1% per day).
    Population: sigma^2 = SUM(x_i - mu)^2 / N. Sample: s^2 = SUM(x_i - x_bar)^2 / (n - 1), where mu is the population mean, x_bar the sample mean, N the population size, and n the sample size.
    Aliases: second central moment

    Standard deviation #

    The square root of the variance, restoring the measure of spread to the original units of the data. It is the most widely used single-number summary of variability and the standard proxy for volatility in finance.
    Intuition: It is the typical distance of observations from their mean, expressed in the same units as the data. For a normal distribution, about 68% of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean and 95% within two.
    Mechanics: Compute the variance as the average squared deviation from the mean (dividing by N for a population or n - 1 for a sample), then take the square root. In trading, the standard deviation of periodic returns is quoted as volatility and is usually annualized by multiplying by the square root of the number of periods per year.
    Failure modes: Like variance, it is pulled strongly by tail outliers and is undefined for distributions without a finite second moment (e.g., Cauchy). It is a poor summary for skewed distributions, where the two sides of the mean have different spreads.
    Misconceptions: The 68-95-99.7 rule only applies to bell-shaped, symmetric (approximately normal) data; applying it to skewed or fat-tailed return series misstates tail probabilities. A low historical standard deviation does not imply low forward risk when regimes change.
    Example: If a fund's monthly returns have a standard deviation of 3%, its annualized volatility is commonly quoted as 3% * sqrt(12) = 10.4%.
    s = sqrt( SUM(x_i - x_bar)^2 / (n - 1) ) for a sample; sigma = sqrt( SUM(x_i - mu)^2 / N ) for a population.
    Aliases: SD, sigma

    Expected value #

    The probability-weighted average of all possible values of a random variable; the long-run mean outcome if the experiment is repeated indefinitely. It is the first moment of a probability distribution.
    Intuition: It is the average you would converge to after many repetitions, not necessarily a value that can actually occur. A bet has positive expected value if the probability-weighted payouts exceed the stake over the long run.
    Mechanics: For a discrete random variable, multiply each possible value by its probability and sum the products; for a continuous variable, integrate x times the density. Linearity of expectation (E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y]) holds even when variables are dependent.
    Failure modes: The expected value need not exist for heavy-tailed distributions (the Cauchy distribution has no mean), and it can be a misleading summary of a single risky decision when the distribution is skewed or the stakes are ruinous. Expectation ignores variance and path, so equal-mean bets can have wildly different risk.
    Misconceptions: The expected value is not the most likely outcome (that is the mode) and need not be a possible value at all - a die's expected roll is 3.5. A positive-expectancy strategy can still lose over any finite horizon.
    Example: A trade wins 10% with probability 0.55 and loses 8% with probability 0.45; E[return] = 0.55*0.10 + 0.45*(-0.08) = 0.019, or 1.9% per trade on average.
    Discrete: E[X] = SUM x_i * P(x_i). Continuous: E[X] = integral of x * f(x) dx over the support, where f is the probability density function.
    Aliases: expectation, mean of a random variable, E[X]

    Simple return #

    The fractional change in value of an investment over a single period: the difference between final and initial value divided by the initial value, including any income such as dividends in the final value. It is the standard per-period return measure in empirical finance.
    Intuition: It answers 'by what percentage did my money grow this period?' A 5% simple return means $100 became $105.
    Mechanics: Divide the change in value (final plus distributions minus initial) by the initial value. Simple returns aggregate across assets by value-weighted averaging (portfolio return is the weighted sum of asset returns) but compound, not add, across time.
    Failure modes: Simple returns do not add across time - summing them over multiple periods overstates compounded growth when returns are volatile. They are also bounded below by -100%, which complicates symmetric statistical models.
    Misconceptions: A +50% gain followed by a -50% loss does not net to zero: (1.5)(0.5) = 0.75, a 25% cumulative loss. Arithmetic averaging of simple returns is not the rate at which capital actually compounds.
    Example: A stock bought at $100 that pays a $2 dividend and ends the period at $105 has simple return R = (105 + 2 - 100)/100 = 7%.
    R_t = (V_t + D_t - V_{t-1}) / V_{t-1}, where V_t is the end-of-period value, V_{t-1} the initial value, and D_t any distributions (dividends, interest) received during the period.
    Aliases: arithmetic return, discrete return, holding period return, net return (single period)

    Log return #

    The natural logarithm of the gross return (price ratio) over a period, equivalent to the continuously compounded rate of return. Its key property is time-additivity: multi-period log returns are the sum of single-period log returns.
    Intuition: Log returns convert compounding into addition. Gains and losses become symmetric: +10% and -10% log returns cancel exactly to zero, unlike simple returns.
    Mechanics: Take the natural log of the ratio of final to initial value. Because log returns add across periods, they are preferred for statistical modeling, aggregation over time, and continuous-time finance; they approximate simple returns closely when the period change is small.
    Failure modes: Log returns are undefined for a final value of zero or negative and diverge to minus infinity as value approaches zero, so they behave badly for instruments that can hit zero. Portfolio-level log returns are not the weighted sum of asset log returns (that property holds only for simple returns), so they must be converted back before portfolio aggregation.
    Misconceptions: Log returns are not identical to simple returns except approximately for small changes: +50% simple corresponds to 40.55% log, and -50% simple to -69.31% log. Treating a mean log return as an arithmetic mean return understates or overstates expected growth if conversion is skipped.
    Example: A stock closes at $3.570 one day and $3.575 the next; the daily log return is ln(3.575/3.570) = 0.0014, or 0.14%.
    r_t = ln(V_t / V_{t-1}) = ln(1 + R_t), where V_t and V_{t-1} are the final and initial values and R_t is the simple return. Multi-period: r_{1..T} = SUM r_t = ln(V_T / V_0).
    Aliases: logarithmic return, continuously compounded return, force of interest

    Gross return #

    Two usages coexist: (1) in asset-pricing mathematics, the gross return is the growth factor of an investment over a period, i.e., final value divided by initial value, equal to one plus the simple return; (2) in performance reporting, gross return is the return before deduction of fees, commissions, and taxes (gross of costs), as opposed to net return.
    Intuition: In sense (1), it is 'what $1 becomes' - a multiplier like 1.07 for a 7% gain. In sense (2), it is what the investment earned before anyone took their cut.
    Mechanics: As a growth factor, gross returns multiply across periods, which is why cumulative performance is computed as their product. As a reporting concept, gross-of-fees return subtracts no management fees or trading costs, while net return is gross return minus those costs.
    Failure modes: The term is ambiguous across literatures: confusing the growth-factor sense (1.07) with the percentage sense (7%) corrupts compounding calculations, and gross-of-fees performance systematically overstates what an investor actually receives.
    Misconceptions: Gross return is not 'the return in gross dollar terms' nor simply the simple return restated; in reporting contexts it differs from net return precisely by fees and taxes, which can be several percentage points per year for active funds.
    Example: An index fund reports a 9.0% gross annual return but a 0.8% expense ratio and transaction costs reduce the investor's net return to about 8.2%; meanwhile the year's gross return as a growth factor is 1.09.
    G_t = V_t / V_{t-1} = 1 + R_t, where V_t and V_{t-1} are final and initial values and R_t is the simple (net) return; log return r_t = ln(G_t).
    Aliases: growth factor, gross-of-fees return

    Cumulative return #

    The total return of an investment over a multi-period horizon, obtained by compounding (geometrically linking) the single-period returns, assuming gains are reinvested. It measures the full change in value from the start to the end of the holding period.
    Intuition: It is the answer to 'if I invested $1 at the start and reinvested everything, what fraction do I have now, minus one?' It compounds, so it is not the sum of the periodic returns.
    Mechanics: Multiply the gross returns (growth factors) of each sub-period together and subtract one. Equivalently, sum the log returns over the horizon and exponentiate minus one.
    Failure modes: Cumulative returns are path-dependent with respect to cash flows: with deposits and withdrawals, the simple compounded figure misstates performance and time-weighted or money-weighted methods are needed. Long cumulative figures also conceal interim drawdowns.
    Misconceptions: Cumulative return is not the sum or arithmetic average of periodic returns: four annual returns of +50%, -20%, +30%, -40% sum to +20% but compound to a cumulative return of 1.5*0.8*1.3*0.6 - 1 = -6.4%.
    Example: Monthly returns of +2%, -1%, and +3% give a cumulative return of 1.02 * 0.99 * 1.03 - 1 = 4.03% over the quarter.
    R_cum = PRODUCT_{t=1..T} (1 + R_t) - 1, where R_t are the per-period simple returns; equivalently R_cum = exp(SUM r_t) - 1 using log returns r_t.
    Aliases: total holding period return, overall return

    Annualized return #

    The constant per-year rate of return that, compounded over the holding period, would produce the observed cumulative return. It converts returns measured over periods longer or shorter than a year to a comparable yearly basis.
    Intuition: It answers 'what steady yearly growth rate would have gotten me to the same final value?' It lets you compare a 9-month return with a 5-year return on one scale.
    Mechanics: Take the cumulative gross return, raise it to the power 1/T where T is the holding period in years, and subtract one. For volatility-style annualization of average per-period returns, the compounded convention (1+R_period)^k - 1 is used, not simple multiplication by k.
    Failure modes: Annualizing short or volatile track records extrapolates a possibly unrepresentative period to a full year; CFA Institute GIPS prohibit annualizing returns for periods shorter than one year because such figures project an unverified rate onto the remainder of the year. Annualized returns also smooth over the path and hide drawdowns.
    Misconceptions: Annualized return is not the arithmetic average of yearly returns: +10% then -10% averages to 0% arithmetically but annualizes to (0.99)^(1/2) - 1 = -0.5% per year. The gap between arithmetic and annualized (geometric) return grows with volatility.
    Example: A cumulative return of 33.1% over 3 months annualizes to (1.331)^(12/3) - 1 = 214% if naively compounded, which is why sub-year annualization is discouraged; a 21% return over 2 years annualizes to (1.21)^(1/2) - 1 = 10% per year.
    R_ann = (1 + R_cum)^(1/T) - 1 = (PRODUCT (1 + R_t))^(1/T) - 1, where R_cum is the cumulative return and T is the horizon in years.
    Aliases: annualized rate of return, compound annual growth rate, CAGR

    Arithmetic mean #

    The sum of a set of values divided by their count; the most common measure of central tendency (location). It is the value that minimizes the sum of squared deviations from the data.
    Intuition: It is the 'fair share' value: if every observation were replaced by the mean, the total would stay the same. It balances the data like a center of gravity.
    Mechanics: Add all observations and divide by the number of observations. Every value, including extremes, contributes fully, which makes the mean efficient for well-behaved data but sensitive to outliers.
    Failure modes: Extreme tail values can pull the mean far from the bulk of the data (NIST illustrates this with Cauchy data, where the mean is useless as a location estimate). For multiplicative processes such as compounded returns, the arithmetic mean overstates the actual growth rate.
    Misconceptions: The arithmetic mean is not always the 'typical' value: for skewed distributions like income or returns it sits above the median. Averaging periodic returns arithmetically does not give the compounded growth rate - that requires the geometric mean.
    Example: Monthly returns of +5%, -3%, and +7% have arithmetic mean (5 - 3 + 7)/3 = 3% per month; the compounded monthly growth is slightly lower at the geometric mean of about 2.9%.
    x_bar = (1/N) * SUM_{i=1..N} x_i, where x_i are the observations and N is their count.
    Aliases: average, sample average, mean

    Geometric mean #

    The nth root of the product of n positive numbers; the central-tendency measure appropriate when quantities combine multiplicatively rather than additively. In finance, the geometric mean of period growth factors gives the constant per-period growth rate that reproduces the compounded result.
    Intuition: It is the average growth rate that actually compounds to the observed total. Where the arithmetic mean answers 'average change', the geometric mean answers 'average multiplier'.
    Mechanics: Multiply the n values and take the nth root; equivalently average their natural logarithms and exponentiate. It is always less than or equal to the arithmetic mean, with equality only when all values are identical (AM-GM inequality), so the gap widens with dispersion.
    Failure modes: It is defined only for positive values, so it cannot be applied to raw returns that can be negative - one must average gross returns (1 + R) instead. A single zero value makes the whole product zero, and highly volatile return sequences push it far below the arithmetic mean (volatility drag).
    Misconceptions: The geometric mean of returns is not interchangeable with the arithmetic mean: for +50% and -50% the arithmetic mean is 0% but the geometric mean of 1.5 and 0.5 is about -13.4% per period, which is what the capital actually did. It is also wrong to apply it to negative raw returns directly.
    Example: An investment with annual returns of +10%, -12%, +90%, -30%, +25% ends at 1.609 times its start; the geometric mean growth factor is 1.0998, i.e., about 9.98% per year, versus a 16.6% arithmetic mean that does not reproduce the final value.
    GM = (PRODUCT_{i=1..n} a_i)^(1/n) = exp( (1/n) * SUM ln(a_i) ), for a_i > 0. For returns, apply it to gross returns: g = (PRODUCT (1 + R_t))^(1/n) - 1.
    Aliases: mean proportional, compound average

    Median #

    The middle value of an ordered data set: the point with half the observations below it and half above. For an even number of observations it is conventionally the average of the two central values.
    Intuition: It is the 'typical middle' value that ignores how extreme the tails are. Half the data lies on each side, by construction.
    Mechanics: Sort the data ascending; if N is odd, take the ((N+1)/2)th value; if N is even, average the (N/2)th and (N/2 + 1)th values. Because it depends only on ranks, it is robust to outliers and skew.
    Failure modes: By discarding magnitude information, the median can hide important tail behavior - two return series with the same median can have very different crash risk. It is also less statistically efficient than the mean when data truly are normal.
    Misconceptions: The median is not necessarily a member of the data set, and it equals the mean only for symmetric distributions; for skewed data they differ systematically, with the mean pulled toward the long tail.
    Example: For trade P&Ls of -50, -20, 10, 30, 400, the median is 10 while the mean is 74; the single large win dominates the mean but leaves the median unchanged.
    For ordered data Y_(1) <= ... <= Y_(N): median = Y_((N+1)/2) if N is odd; median = (Y_(N/2) + Y_(N/2 + 1))/2 if N is even.
    Aliases: 50th percentile, second quartile, Q2

    Mode #

    The value that occurs with the greatest frequency in a data set, or the location of the peak of a probability distribution. A distribution may have one mode (unimodal), several modes (multimodal), or no well-defined mode.
    Intuition: It is the most common outcome - the value you would bet on if you had to guess exactly one number.
    Mechanics: For discrete data, count frequencies and select the most frequent value; for continuous data, values essentially never repeat, so the mode is taken as the peak of a histogram or density estimate (e.g., the midpoint of the tallest histogram bin).
    Failure modes: For continuous data the sample mode is unstable and depends on histogram binning, and multimodal data make 'the mode' ambiguous. The mode ignores all non-peak values, so it carries no information about spread or tails.
    Misconceptions: The mode is not the same as the mean or median and is not necessarily near the center of the data - in a right-skewed distribution it sits below both. A data set can legitimately have more than one mode or none.
    Example: For a normal sample the mean, median, and mode nearly coincide, while for a lognormal sample NIST reports mode 0.68, median 0.99, mean 1.68 - the mode sits at the peak of the right-skewed density.
    Aliases: modal value, most frequent value

    Covariance #

    A measure of the joint variability of two random variables: the expected product of their deviations from their respective means. Its sign indicates the direction of the linear relationship (positive: variables tend to move together; negative: oppositely), and its magnitude depends on the units of the variables.
    Intuition: It is the raw, unstandardized version of correlation: it tells you whether two variables move together and by how much in squared unit terms, but you cannot compare covariances across different unit scales.
    Mechanics: For each paired observation, multiply the deviation of x from its mean by the deviation of y from its mean, then average these products. Normalizing covariance by the product of the standard deviations yields the Pearson correlation coefficient; in portfolio theory, asset covariances determine portfolio variance.
    Failure modes: Because covariance carries units of x times y, its magnitude is not comparable across variable pairs or scales - only its sign and the normalized correlation are interpretable. Zero covariance rules out linear dependence but not nonlinear dependence (e.g., X uniform on [-1,1] and Y = X^2 have covariance zero yet are perfectly dependent).
    Misconceptions: Uncorrelated does not mean independent, except under joint normality. Also, a large absolute covariance does not by itself imply a strong relationship - it may just reflect large units or variances.
    Example: If two stocks have daily returns with variances 0.0004 and 0.0009 and covariance 0.0003, their correlation is 0.0003 / sqrt(0.0004*0.0009) = 0.5; the covariance value alone says nothing without that normalization.
    cov(X,Y) = E[(X - E[X])(Y - E[Y])] = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]; sample estimate: q = SUM_{i=1..N}(x_i - x_bar)(y_i - y_bar) / (N - 1).
    Aliases: sigma_XY, cov(X,Y)

    Pearson correlation #

    The standardized measure of linear association between two variables, equal to their covariance divided by the product of their standard deviations, ranging from -1 (perfect negative linear) to +1 (perfect positive linear). Developed by Karl Pearson around the early 1900s.
    Intuition: It measures how well a straight line describes the relationship: +1 or -1 means all points lie exactly on a line, 0 means no linear pattern at all.
    Mechanics: Standardize each variable (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation), multiply the paired standardized values, and average. The sign matches the slope of the least-squares regression line, and r^2 is the share of variance in y explained linearly by x.
    Failure modes: It detects only linear structure: U-shaped, cyclical, or threshold relationships can yield r near 0 despite perfect dependence. It is also non-robust - one outlier can inflate or destroy r - and it presumes the relationship is stable over the sample.
    Misconceptions: r = 0 does not mean 'no relationship', only no linear one. Squaring r gives variance explained, so r = 0.5 means only 25% shared variance, not '50% association'; and strong correlation still does not imply causation.
    Example: For 11 students' third-exam and final-exam scores, OpenStax computes r = 0.6631, so r^2 = 0.44: about 44% of the variation in final scores is linearly explained by third-exam scores.
    r = [n*SUM(xy) - (SUM x)(SUM y)] / sqrt( [n*SUM x^2 - (SUM x)^2] * [n*SUM y^2 - (SUM y)^2] ), where n is the number of paired observations.
    Aliases: Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient, Pearson's r, r

    Spearman rank correlation #

    A nonparametric measure of monotonic association between two variables, defined as the Pearson correlation coefficient computed on the ranks of the observations rather than their raw values. It ranges from -1 to +1 and detects any consistent monotonic relationship, linear or not.
    Intuition: It asks 'do bigger x values reliably come with bigger y values?' regardless of how big the steps are. Only the ordering matters, so outliers can do no more damage than their rank allows.
    Mechanics: Rank each variable separately (averaging ranks for ties), then compute the Pearson correlation of the rank pairs. With no ties, a simplified formula in the squared rank differences d_i applies; with ties, the general Pearson-on-ranks form must be used.
    Failure modes: The simplified d^2 formula gives wrong values when ties are present. Like all correlation measures it does not imply causation, and it quantifies monotone association only - a non-monotonic (e.g., inverted-U) dependence can score near zero. With small samples, significance is hard to establish.
    Misconceptions: A perfect Spearman coefficient does not require a linear relationship - any strictly monotonic one gives +/-1, so Spearman = 1 is weaker evidence than Pearson = 1. Nor is Spearman simply 'Pearson with outliers removed'; it answers a different question (monotonic vs. linear).
    Example: Ranking 10 people by IQ and by weekly TV hours yields SUM d_i^2 = 58 and n = 10, giving rho = 1 - 6*58/(10*99) = -0.176, a weak negative monotonic association.
    rs = 1 - 6*SUM(d_i^2) / (n*(n^2 - 1)), where d_i is the difference between the two ranks of observation i and n is the sample size; valid only when all ranks are distinct (no ties).
    Aliases: Spearman's rho, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient, grade correlation

    Kendall rank correlation #

    A nonparametric measure of ordinal association between two variables, computed from the difference between the number of concordant and discordant observation pairs, normalized by the total number of pairs. It ranges from -1 (perfectly reversed rankings) to +1 (identical rankings) and was introduced by Maurice Kendall in 1938.
    Intuition: Pick any two observations; the pair is 'concordant' if both variables rank them in the same order. Tau is essentially the probability of concordance minus the probability of discordance.
    Mechanics: For all n(n-1)/2 pairs, count pairs where the x-order and y-order agree (concordant) minus those where they disagree (discordant), and divide by the number of pairs. Variants tau-b and tau-c adjust the denominator for ties; under the null of independence, tau has mean zero and an approximately normal sampling distribution for large n.
    Failure modes: With many tied values, unadjusted tau-a cannot reach +/-1 and the tie-corrected tau-b or tau-c must be used, changing the value. Direct computation is O(n^2), and tau values are systematically smaller in magnitude than Spearman's rho for the same data, so the two are not numerically interchangeable.
    Misconceptions: Tau is not 'a worse Spearman': it has a direct probabilistic interpretation (concordance minus discordance) that rho lacks. And like any rank correlation it measures ordinal association only, never causation.
    Example: For n = 30 points with 395 concordant and 40 discordant pairs out of 435 total, tau = (395 - 40)/435 = 0.816, indicating strong agreement between the two rankings.
    tau = (n_C - n_D) / (n(n-1)/2) = 1 - 2*n_D / C(n,2), where n_C and n_D are the numbers of concordant and discordant pairs and C(n,2) is the binomial coefficient (number of pairs).
    Aliases: Kendall's tau, Kendall tau coefficient

    Probability distribution #

    A mathematical function that assigns probabilities to the possible outcomes of a random variable. For discrete variables it is a probability mass function giving P(X = x) with all masses non-negative and summing to 1; for continuous variables it is a probability density function whose integral over the whole line is 1, with probabilities defined over intervals.
    Intuition: It is the complete map of what values a random quantity can take and how likely each is - the 'shape of uncertainty' for a variable such as tomorrow's return.
    Mechanics: A discrete PMF p(x) satisfies p(x) >= 0 and SUM p(x) = 1, with P(X = x) = p(x) at each value. A continuous PDF f(x) satisfies f(x) >= 0 and integrates to 1; the probability of any single point is zero and interval probabilities are areas under the curve. The cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X <= x) gives left-tail probabilities in both cases.
    Failure modes: Real financial return series rarely match any simple parametric distribution exactly - they show skewness, excess kurtosis, volatility clustering, and regime changes - so a fitted distribution understates tail risk if taken literally. For continuous models, treating density height as a probability is a category error.
    Misconceptions: A probability density is not a probability: f(x) can exceed 1, and only areas under it are probabilities. Also, knowing a distribution's mean and variance does not determine the distribution - many shapes share the same two moments.
    Example: Modeling the number of daily limit-up moves in a year with a binomial distribution and tomorrow's percentage price change with a normal distribution are both choices of a probability distribution; each implies different tail odds.
    Discrete: SUM_j p_j = 1, p_j >= 0. Continuous: integral_{-inf}^{+inf} f(x) dx = 1, f(x) >= 0; P(a <= X <= b) = integral_a^b f(x) dx.
    Aliases: probability function, distribution

    Normal distribution #

    A continuous, symmetric, single-peaked probability distribution fully determined by its location parameter mu (mean = median = mode) and scale parameter sigma (standard deviation). It is the default modeling distribution in statistics, theoretically justified by the central limit theorem for sums and averages.
    Intuition: It is the classic bell curve: most values cluster near the mean and extremes become rapidly rarer, with about 68% of probability within one sigma and 99.7% within three.
    Mechanics: The density is fully specified by mu and sigma; the standard normal (mu = 0, sigma = 1) is obtained by z-scoring. Sums and means of many independent contributions converge to it (CLT), which is why many test statistics are normal-based; its CDF has no closed form and is computed numerically.
    Failure modes: Financial returns exhibit heavier tails and skewness than the normal model, so normality assumptions understate the probability of extreme moves (crashes). Tests and intervals built on normality can mislead when applied to fat-tailed or asymmetric data without robustness checks.
    Misconceptions: 'Everything averages out to a bell curve' is false for heavy-tailed data - the CLT requires finite variance, and Cauchy-type data never normalize. Also, normality of returns does not follow from price levels being lognormal; they are distinct modeling choices.
    Example: Under a normal model with daily mean 0.05% and sigma 1%, a -3% day is a -3.05-sigma event with probability near 0.1% - yet such days occur far more often in real equity returns, illustrating the thin-tail failure.
    f(x) = exp( -(x - mu)^2 / (2*sigma^2) ) / (sigma * sqrt(2*pi)), where mu is the mean and sigma the standard deviation; skewness = 0, kurtosis = 3.
    Aliases: Gaussian distribution, bell curve, N(mu, sigma^2)

    Lognormal distribution #

    A continuous probability distribution of a positive random variable X whose natural logarithm is normally distributed. It is right-skewed with support on (0, infinity) and is the standard model for asset prices under geometric Brownian motion.
    Intuition: If returns compound multiplicatively, prices end up lognormal: the product of many positive random growth factors has a log that is a sum, hence normal by the CLT. Prices cannot go below zero, which the lognormal respects.
    Mechanics: If Y = ln(X) ~ N(mu, sigma^2) then X is lognormal; the scale parameter m = exp(mu) is the median of X, and sigma is the standard deviation of log X. The mean exp(mu + sigma^2/2) exceeds the median, reflecting the right skew.
    Failure modes: Real asset prices deviate from lognormality: returns show fat tails, skew, and jumps, so option-pricing and risk models built on strict lognormality (e.g., basic Black-Scholes) misprice tail events. Parameter estimates are also fragile when the data contain outliers.
    Misconceptions: Lognormal is not 'the log of a normal variable' (that can be negative) but a variable whose log is normal. And a lognormal price process implies normal log returns - the two statements are equivalent, not competing assumptions.
    Example: If a stock's annual log return is N(0.08, 0.2^2), its one-year price ratio is lognormal with median exp(0.08) = 1.083 and mean exp(0.08 + 0.02) = 1.105 - the mean outcome is pulled above the median by upside compounding.
    f(x) = exp( -(ln x - mu)^2 / (2*sigma^2) ) / (x * sigma * sqrt(2*pi)) for x > 0, where mu and sigma are the mean and standard deviation of ln(X); median = exp(mu), mean = exp(mu + sigma^2/2).
    Aliases: log-normal distribution, Galton distribution

    Student's t-distribution #

    A symmetric, bell-shaped probability distribution with heavier tails than the normal, parameterized by the degrees of freedom nu. It is the exact sampling distribution of the standardized sample mean when the population standard deviation is estimated from a small sample, and it converges to the normal distribution as nu grows.
    Intuition: It is the 'cautious normal': when you estimate spread from limited data, the extra uncertainty fattens the tails, so extreme values are more likely than the bell curve suggests.
    Mechanics: With shape parameter nu (a positive integer in testing contexts), the t density resembles the normal but with tail weight decreasing as nu increases; nu = 1 gives the Cauchy distribution and nu > 30 is practically normal. Its variance nu/(nu-2) exists only for nu > 2, and kurtosis only for nu > 4.
    Failure modes: Low degrees of freedom imply very heavy tails - the mean is undefined at nu = 1 and the variance at nu <= 2 - so low-nu t models can break variance-based risk metrics. As a model for returns it captures symmetric fat tails but not skewness or volatility clustering.
    Misconceptions: The t distribution is not just a small-sample curiosity: in finance it is used deliberately as a fat-tailed return model. Nor does 't-distributed test statistic' require the raw data to be t-distributed - it arises from normal data with estimated variance.
    Example: A 95% confidence interval for a mean from n = 20 observations uses the t critical value with 19 degrees of freedom (about 2.09) instead of the normal 1.96, widening the interval to account for the estimated standard deviation.
    f(x) = (1 + x^2/nu)^(-(nu+1)/2) / (B(0.5, 0.5*nu) * sqrt(nu)), where nu is the degrees-of-freedom parameter and B is the beta function; variance = nu/(nu-2) for nu > 2.
    Aliases: t distribution, Student's t

    Bernoulli distribution #

    The probability distribution of a single trial with exactly two outcomes, conventionally 'success' (X = 1) with probability p and 'failure' (X = 0) with probability 1 - p. It is the building block of the binomial distribution, which counts successes across n independent Bernoulli trials.
    Intuition: It models any one-shot yes/no event: will this trade close in profit, will this coin land heads? Only one number - p - fully describes it.
    Mechanics: The random variable takes value 1 with probability p and 0 with probability q = 1 - p. Its mean is p and variance is p(1 - p); summing n independent Bernoulli variables with the same p gives a Binomial(n, p) variable.
    Failure modes: It only fits single binary trials with a fixed success probability; applying it to sequences requires independence and constant p, both of which often fail in trading (regime shifts, path dependence). It carries no information about magnitude - a 'win' of 1% and 50% are the same event.
    Misconceptions: A Bernoulli trial is not any two-outcome situation: the probabilities must be fixed and, for sequences, the trials independent - drawing without replacement violates the setup. Also, p = 0.5 is not required; 'binary' does not mean 'fair'.
    Example: Modeling one trade as a Bernoulli variable with win probability p = 0.55 gives expected value 0.55 and variance 0.2475; a 20-trade win count then follows a Binomial(20, 0.55) distribution.
    P(X = 1) = p, P(X = 0) = 1 - p; E[X] = p; Var(X) = p(1 - p), where p is the success probability.
    Aliases: Bernoulli trial, binary distribution

    Binomial distribution #

    The discrete probability distribution of the number of successes in n independent trials, each with exactly two outcomes and the same success probability p. It is the sum of n independent Bernoulli variables and is probably the most commonly used discrete distribution.
    Intuition: It answers: 'If each trade wins with probability p, what are the chances of exactly x wins in n trades?' The win count, not the order of wins, is what matters.
    Mechanics: The probability of x successes equals the number of ways to choose which x trials succeed (the binomial coefficient) times p^x(1-p)^(n-x). It requires a fixed number of trials, two outcomes per trial, constant p, and independence; the maximum likelihood estimate of p from data is x/n.
    Failure modes: The independence and constant-p assumptions fail under regime changes, serial correlation, or sampling without replacement, in which case binomial probabilities misstate tail odds. For large n and small p it is better approximated by the Poisson distribution, and for large n with moderate p by the normal.
    Misconceptions: The binomial counts successes without regard to order or payoff size - it cannot price P&L, only event counts. And 'more wins than expected' over n trades is not proof of skill; with p = 0.5, 12+ wins in 20 trials still occurs about 25% of the time.
    Example: A strategy wins with p = 0.55 per trade; the probability of exactly 15 wins in 20 trades is C(20,15) * 0.55^15 * 0.45^5, about 0.037.
    P(X = x) = C(n,x) * p^x * (1-p)^(n-x) for x = 0..n, where C(n,x) = n!/(x!(n-x)!); mean = np, variance = np(1-p).
    Aliases: B(n, p), binomial probability distribution

    Poisson distribution #

    The discrete probability distribution of the number of events occurring in a fixed interval of time or space when events occur independently at a constant average rate lambda. Both its mean and its variance equal lambda.
    Intuition: It models rare, independent arrivals: 'If on average 2 news shocks hit per day, what is the chance of 5 tomorrow?' One rate parameter determines everything.
    Mechanics: The probability of x events decays factorially in x: e^(-lambda) lambda^x / x!. Key properties: mean = variance = lambda, standard deviation sqrt(lambda), and the maximum likelihood estimate of lambda is the sample mean of counts; inter-arrival times follow the exponential distribution.
    Failure modes: It assumes a constant rate and independence, so clustered events - such as volatility spikes or order-flow bursts that bunch together - are badly fit: real count data often show variance exceeding the mean (overdispersion), which Poisson cannot represent.
    Misconceptions: Poisson is not a model for any count data: if events cluster or the rate varies over time (non-stationarity), the equal mean-variance property breaks. Also, lambda need not be small - the distribution works for large rates too and approaches normality as lambda grows.
    Example: If a stock experiences on average lambda = 3 price gaps per month, the probability of a month with zero gaps is e^(-3) = 0.0498, about 5%.
    P(X = x) = e^(-lambda) * lambda^x / x! for x = 0, 1, 2, ..., where lambda is the average number of events per interval; mean = lambda, variance = lambda.
    Aliases: Poisson

    Exponential distribution #

    A continuous probability distribution on the non-negative reals modeling the waiting time between events that occur at a constant rate, with a constant hazard function. It is the unique continuous memoryless distribution: the probability of waiting t more time units is independent of time already waited.
    Intuition: It answers 'how long until the next event?' when events strike at a steady average rate. Past waiting tells you nothing about remaining waiting - the process has no memory.
    Mechanics: With scale parameter beta (mean waiting time), the density decays exponentially from its start; mean and standard deviation both equal beta and the hazard rate is the constant 1/beta. It is the inter-arrival-time distribution of a Poisson process and is widely used in reliability and duration modeling.
    Failure modes: The constant-hazard (memoryless) assumption fails whenever risk changes with age or state - trade durations and default times typically show time-varying hazard, making Weibull or other models more appropriate. It also cannot represent zero-inflated or multi-modal waiting data.
    Misconceptions: Memorylessness does not mean events are 'due' after a long quiet period - the gambler's-fallacy intuition contradicts the model. And the exponential distribution is not 'the distribution of an exponential trend'; it describes random waiting times.
    Example: If order-book price changes arrive at an average rate of one every beta = 30 seconds, the probability that the next change takes more than 60 seconds is exp(-60/30) = e^(-2) = 0.135.
    f(x) = (1/beta) * exp(-x/beta) for x >= 0, where beta > 0 is the scale parameter (mean waiting time); CDF F(x) = 1 - exp(-x/beta); mean = SD = beta. (Rate form: lambda = 1/beta.)
    Aliases: negative exponential distribution
