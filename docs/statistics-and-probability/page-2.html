    Skewness #

    A measure of the asymmetry of a distribution about its mean, defined as the third standardized moment. Zero indicates symmetry, positive values indicate a longer right tail, and negative values a longer left tail.
    Intuition: It tells you which side the surprises live on: negatively skewed returns have occasional large crashes amid small gains; positively skewed returns have occasional large wins amid small losses.
    Mechanics: Average the cubed deviations from the mean and divide by the cube of the standard deviation; cubing preserves sign, so the heavy tail determines the sign. Software typically reports the sample-size-adjusted Fisher-Pearson coefficient, with the correction factor approaching 1 for large N.
    Failure modes: Sample skewness is extremely sensitive to outliers - NIST's Cauchy example yields a sample skewness of 70 from a symmetric distribution - and multimodality can flip its sign, so it cannot be read at face value without a histogram. It is also undefined when the third moment does not exist.
    Misconceptions: Skewness zero does not prove normality (many non-normal distributions are symmetric), and the sign describes the tail, not where most of the mass sits - a right-skewed distribution typically has most observations below its mean.
    Example: Daily equity-index returns commonly show skewness around -0.3 to -1, quantifying the crash-prone left tail that a symmetric normal model would miss.
    g1 = [SUM_{i=1..N}(Y_i - Y_bar)^3 / N] / s^3, where Y_bar is the mean and s is the standard deviation computed with N in the denominator; adjusted version G1 = [sqrt(N(N-1))/(N-2)] * g1.
    Aliases: third standardized moment, Fisher-Pearson coefficient of skewness

    Kurtosis #

    A measure of whether data are heavy-tailed or light-tailed relative to a normal distribution, defined as the fourth standardized moment. High kurtosis indicates heavy tails (frequent outliers); low kurtosis indicates light tails, and the normal distribution has kurtosis 3.
    Intuition: It quantifies tail risk: two distributions with the same mean and variance can differ enormously in how often extreme moves occur, and kurtosis captures that.
    Mechanics: Average the fourth powers of the deviations from the mean and divide by the fourth power of the standard deviation. The fourth power makes the measure almost entirely driven by tail observations; the standard normal value of 3 is the reference point.
    Failure modes: Kurtosis is even more outlier-sensitive than skewness - NIST's Cauchy example produces a sample kurtosis of 6,693 - and it does not exist for distributions lacking a finite fourth moment. It conflates tail weight with peakedness, so high values do not diagnose which feature dominates.
    Misconceptions: Kurtosis is not a measure of 'peakedness' alone; modern interpretation emphasizes tail weight. And a kurtosis of 3 is not 'zero kurtosis' - 3 is the normal benchmark, which is why excess kurtosis (minus 3) is often reported instead.
    Example: A double-exponential (Laplace) sample has kurtosis about 5.9 versus the normal's 3, reflecting its heavier tails; daily stock returns often show sample kurtosis of 5-10, far above normal.
    kurtosis = [SUM_{i=1..N}(Y_i - Y_bar)^4 / N] / s^4, where Y_bar is the mean and s the standard deviation computed with N in the denominator; for a standard normal distribution the value is 3.
    Aliases: fourth standardized moment

    Excess kurtosis #

    The kurtosis of a distribution minus 3, the kurtosis of the standard normal distribution, so that the normal reference value becomes zero. Positive excess kurtosis indicates heavier-than-normal tails; negative values indicate lighter-than-normal tails.
    Intuition: It is kurtosis re-zeroed at the bell curve: 0 means normal-like tails, positive means fatter tails than normal, negative means thinner.
    Mechanics: Compute the fourth standardized moment and subtract 3. Many software packages and financial texts report excess kurtosis while calling it simply 'kurtosis', so the convention in use must be checked before comparing numbers.
    Failure modes: It inherits all the outlier sensitivity and existence problems of kurtosis, amplified by convention confusion: comparing an excess-kurtosis figure against a raw-kurtosis figure creates a spurious difference of 3. Small samples produce wildly unstable estimates.
    Misconceptions: A reported 'kurtosis of 2' may mean raw kurtosis 2 (thinner tails than normal) or excess kurtosis 2 (fatter tails, raw = 5) - the two conventions say opposite things, and sources often fail to state which they use.
    Example: Daily S&P 500 returns over long samples show excess kurtosis well above 0 (often 5 or more), quantifying crash-frequency beyond what a normal model predicts.
    excess kurtosis = [SUM_{i=1..N}(Y_i - Y_bar)^4 / N] / s^4 - 3, i.e., the fourth standardized moment minus the normal value of 3.
    Aliases: excess

    Quantile #

    Cut points that divide the range of a probability distribution (or an ordered sample) into intervals of equal probability. The p-quantile Q(p) is the value below which a proportion p of the probability mass falls, i.e., the inverse of the cumulative distribution function; special cases include quartiles, deciles, and percentiles.
    Intuition: Quantiles answer 'what value marks the bottom 5%?' - the 0.05-quantile of a return distribution is the loss level that is exceeded only 5% of the time, the basis of Value-at-Risk.
    Mechanics: For a known distribution, Q(p) = F^{-1}(p), the inverse CDF evaluated at p. From a sample of N ordered values, quantiles are estimated by rank indexing or interpolation between adjacent order statistics; Hyndman and Fan document nine different conventions used across software packages, so the same query can give slightly different numbers.
    Failure modes: Sample quantiles of the extreme tails (p near 0 or 1) are estimated very imprecisely and depend heavily on the interpolation convention, so tail risk figures differ across software. For heavy-tailed data, extreme quantiles shift drastically when a few observations change.
    Misconceptions: Quantiles are not uniquely defined by 'the value below which p of the data falls' - different packages use different order-statistic interpolations (Hyndman-Fan types), which matters for small samples. A quantile is also a statement about the distribution, not a forecast of an individual outcome.
    Example: For 10 ordered values [3, 6, 7, 8, 8, 10, 13, 15, 16, 20], the first quartile Q1 (0.25-quantile) is 7 under the nearest-rank rule, while interpolated methods give values between 6.75 and 7.75.
    Q(p) = F^{-1}(p) = inf{ x : F(x) >= p }, where F is the cumulative distribution function and 0 < p < 1; the k-th q-quantile is Q(k/q).
    Aliases: fractile, percent point

    Percentile #

    The 100-quantiles of a distribution or ordered data set: the kth percentile is the value at or below which k percent of the observations fall. Percentiles express the relative standing of a value within a data set.
    Intuition: Scoring in the 90th percentile means 90% of values are at or below yours and 10% above - a rank statement, not a statement about the score itself.
    Mechanics: Order the data; for the kth percentile compute the rank index i = (k/100)(n + 1) - if i is an integer take that data value, otherwise average the two neighboring ordered values (other packages use different interpolation rules). Quartiles are the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles.
    Failure modes: Percentile values depend on the interpolation convention and are unstable in small samples, especially near the extremes. In non-stationary series, a percentile rank computed on one regime says little about standing in another.
    Misconceptions: The 90th percentile does not mean scoring 90% - it is a statement of relative position. Nor is the difference between the 90th and 10th percentiles 'the middle 80% of values' by magnitude; it is by count.
    Example: A fund's one-day return of -4% is worse than 99% of its historical daily returns, putting it at the 1st percentile - an extreme-tail day by its own history.
    Index for the kth percentile: i = (k/100) * (n + 1), where n is the number of ordered observations; the percentile is the ith ordered value if i is integral, else the average of the two bracketing values (one common convention of several).
    Aliases: centile

    Interquartile range #

    The difference between the third quartile (75th percentile) and the first quartile (25th percentile) of a data set; the spread of the middle 50% of the data. It is a robust measure of scale that ignores the tails entirely.
    Intuition: It measures how wide the 'typical middle' of the data is, untouched by extreme values - the width of the box in a box plot.
    Mechanics: Compute Q1 and Q3 from the ordered data and subtract. Because it uses only ranks of central observations, it is unaffected by outliers; it is also the basis of the standard 1.5*IQR fences used to flag potential outliers.
    Failure modes: By construction it discards all tail information, so two return series with identical IQR can have completely different crash risk; it also says nothing about asymmetry. Quartile definitions vary slightly across conventions, shifting the IQR in small samples.
    Misconceptions: A small IQR does not mean low risk - it means the central half of outcomes is tight, which is compatible with rare but enormous tail events. The IQR is also not 'half the range'.
    Example: For 90 ordered observations with Q1 = 429.75 and Q3 = 742.25, NIST computes IQR = 312.5 and uses fences at Q1 - 1.5*IQR and Q3 + 1.5*IQR to screen for outliers.
    IQR = Q3 - Q1, where Q1 is the 25th percentile (lower quartile) and Q3 is the 75th percentile (upper quartile).
    Aliases: IQR, midspread, middle fifty

    Outlier #

    A data point that is significantly different from the other observations in a sample - potentially an error, an abnormality, or a key to understanding the data. The standard box-plot rule flags points more than 1.5 times the interquartile range beyond the quartiles as potential outliers (and beyond 3*IQR as extreme outliers).
    Intuition: It is an observation that does not fit the pattern of the rest - the one day in a quiet series when the market moved 10%.
    Mechanics: Under the Tukey box-plot convention, compute Q1, Q3, and IQR; points below Q1 - 1.5*IQR or above Q3 + 1.5*IQR are mild outliers, and points beyond 3*IQR from the quartiles are extreme outliers. Flagged points require investigation, not automatic deletion.
    Failure modes: The 1.5*IQR rule assumes roughly symmetric, light-tailed data; for genuinely heavy-tailed financial returns it flags legitimate tail events as 'outliers', inviting the deletion of exactly the observations that drive risk. Masking effects also occur: multiple outliers can inflate the IQR so that none are flagged.
    Misconceptions: An outlier is not automatically a data error to be removed - in finance the extremes are often the most informative points (crashes, halts). Conversely, 'far from the mean' is not the definition: the fences are defined from quartiles, and in skewed data far points may be perfectly normal.
    Example: In NIST's 90-observation data set with Q1 = 429.75 and Q3 = 742.25, the upper inner fence is 1211.0; the value 1441 exceeds it and is flagged as a mild outlier, with no extreme outliers present.
    Mild outlier if x < Q1 - 1.5*IQR or x > Q3 + 1.5*IQR; extreme outlier if x < Q1 - 3*IQR or x > Q3 + 3*IQR, where Q1 and Q3 are the quartiles and IQR = Q3 - Q1.
    Aliases: extreme value, anomalous observation

    Z-score #

    The number of standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean of its distribution, obtained by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. Z-scores standardize values from different distributions onto a common scale.
    Intuition: It converts any measurement into 'how many sigmas from average' - a -2 day means a move twice the typical size in the down direction, whatever the asset's units.
    Mechanics: Compute z = (x - mu)/sigma using the population parameters, or (x - x_bar)/s with sample statistics. Under a normal model the z-score maps directly to tail probabilities via the standard normal CDF (z = +/-1, +/-2, +/-3 contain about 68%, 95%, 99.7% of mass).
    Failure modes: Tail probabilities read off a z-score assume approximate normality; for fat-tailed returns a -5-sigma event is vastly more common than the normal table implies, so z-scores understate tail risk. They are also unreliable when the mean and standard deviation are themselves unstable or estimated from few observations.
    Misconceptions: A z-score is not itself a probability, and |z| > 2 is a rough rule of thumb for 'far from average', not a rigorous outlier test - OpenStax notes it is approximate, not a rigid rule. Also, z-scoring does not make non-normal data normal.
    Example: With mean daily return 0.04% and sigma 1%, a day returning -2.96% has z = (-2.96 - 0.04)/1 = -3.0, i.e., three standard deviations below the mean.
    z = (x - mu) / sigma, where x is the value, mu the mean, and sigma the standard deviation; sample form z = (x - x_bar)/s.
    Aliases: standard score, standardized value

    Standard error #

    The standard deviation of the sampling distribution of a statistic - most commonly the standard error of the mean, which measures how much a sample mean would vary from sample to sample. It shrinks with the square root of the sample size.
    Intuition: The standard deviation describes spread of individual data points; the standard error describes the spread of your estimate. It answers 'how precisely does my sample pin down the average?'
    Mechanics: For the sample mean of n independent observations with population standard deviation sigma, the standard error is sigma/sqrt(n), estimated in practice by s/sqrt(n). Quadrupling the sample size halves the standard error, and it is the denominator ingredient of t-statistics and confidence-interval widths.
    Failure modes: The sigma/sqrt(n) form assumes independent observations; with autocorrelated returns (volatility clustering, momentum), true standard errors are larger and naive intervals are too narrow. It also measures only sampling variability, not model or estimation bias.
    Misconceptions: Standard error is not the standard deviation of the data and does not shrink the spread of individual returns as n grows - only the uncertainty of the mean. A small SE also does not validate the model; biased sampling yields precise but wrong estimates.
    Example: Daily returns with sigma = 1.5% over n = 250 trading days give a standard error of the mean of 1.5%/sqrt(250) = 0.095%, so the estimated mean daily return carries about +/-0.19% (two SE) of sampling uncertainty.
    SE(x_bar) = sigma / sqrt(n), where sigma is the population standard deviation and n the sample size; estimated as s / sqrt(n) from a sample.
    Aliases: SE, standard error of the mean

    Confidence interval #

    An interval estimate for a population parameter constructed so that a stated proportion (the confidence level, e.g., 95%) of intervals built the same way from repeated samples would contain the true parameter value. For the mean with unknown variance it uses the t distribution with N - 1 degrees of freedom.
    Intuition: Instead of one best-guess number, it gives a plausible range whose width communicates how uncertain the estimate is: narrow means precise, wide means noisy.
    Mechanics: For a mean: take the sample mean plus and minus a t critical value times the standard error s/sqrt(N). The width is driven by sample size (1/sqrt(N)) and by the data's standard deviation; the confidence level 1 - alpha is set before computing (commonly 0.95).
    Failure modes: The stated coverage assumes the sampling model (independence, approximate normality of the mean) holds; autocorrelated or heavy-tailed returns produce intervals with true coverage below the nominal level. Intervals also inherit any bias in how the data were collected (e.g., survivorship bias in fund samples).
    Misconceptions: A 95% confidence interval does NOT mean there is a 95% probability that this particular interval contains the true mean - it either does or does not; the 95% refers to the long-run success rate of the method across repeated samples (a point NIST states explicitly).
    Example: With N = 195, mean 9.2615, s = 0.0228, and t = 1.972, NIST computes a 95% CI of (9.2582, 9.2647); because 5 lies outside it, H0: mu = 5 is rejected at the 5% level - the duality between intervals and tests.
    Y_bar +/- t_{1-alpha/2, N-1} * s / sqrt(N), where Y_bar is the sample mean, s the sample standard deviation, N the sample size, and t the percentile of the t distribution with N-1 degrees of freedom at confidence level 1 - alpha.
    Aliases: CI, interval estimate

    Null hypothesis #

    The default statement in a hypothesis test that there is no effect or no difference - the status quo that sample evidence must argue against. It always contains a statement of equality (e.g., mu = mu_0) and is the claim that is rejected or not rejected, never 'proven'.
    Intuition: It is the skeptical position: 'this strategy has no edge, the apparent performance is luck.' Testing asks whether the data are surprising enough under that assumption to abandon it.
    Mechanics: The null specifies a parameter value or distribution (e.g., H0: mu = mu_0). A test statistic is computed from the sample, and if its value falls in the critical region defined by the significance level alpha, H0 is rejected in favor of the alternative; otherwise the conclusion is 'do not reject', not 'accept'.
    Failure modes: Failing to reject H0 does not establish it as true - an underpowered test on few trades will fail to reject almost any null. The framework also only evaluates the stated null, so a misspecified model (wrong distribution, ignored costs) makes rejection or non-rejection meaningless.
    Misconceptions: One never 'accepts the null hypothesis'; absence of evidence against H0 is not evidence for it. Also, the null need not be 'zero effect' in every application - it is whatever default statement the analyst sets up to test.
    Example: Testing a trading signal, H0: mean daily excess return = 0; with T = 2.8 exceeding the 5% critical value 1.97, the null of no edge is rejected - though this says nothing about whether the edge covers transaction costs.
    Typical form: H0: mu = mu_0 (or H0: p <= p_0), versus the alternative Ha: mu != mu_0 (or p > p_0); test statistic for a mean: T = (Y_bar - mu_0) / (s/sqrt(N)).
    Aliases: H0

    Alternative hypothesis #

    The claim about the population that contradicts the null hypothesis - typically what the researcher suspects or hopes to demonstrate - and the conclusion adopted when the null is rejected. It never contains an equality, taking forms mu != mu_0, mu > mu_0, or mu < mu_0.
    Intuition: It is the contender: 'this strategy does have an edge.' You get to assert it only by showing the data are inconsistent with the null.
    Mechanics: The choice of alternative determines the rejection region: a two-sided Ha (mu != mu_0) splits alpha into both tails, while one-sided alternatives (mu > mu_0 or mu < mu_0) place the entire critical region in one tail, changing the critical value and the power against effects in that direction.
    Failure modes: Choosing the alternative after seeing the data (deciding on a one-sided test because the sign is known) invalidates the stated error rates. A test is only powerful against alternatives in the direction specified, so a wrong-sided or overly narrow Ha can fail to detect a real effect.
    Misconceptions: Rejecting H0 does not prove Ha is true or economically important - it only means the data are sufficiently inconsistent with H0. Nor may the alternative contain an equality; by convention the equality always belongs to the null.
    Example: For a mean-reversion signal the analyst sets H0: mu >= 0 against Ha: mu < 0 (expected negative mean spread change); a one-sided rejection region in the left tail concentrates all power on detecting the predicted direction.
    Forms: Ha: mu != mu_0 with rejection region |T| > t_{1-alpha/2}; Ha: mu > mu_0 with T > t_{1-alpha}; Ha: mu < mu_0 with T < t_{alpha}, where T is the test statistic and t the appropriate t-distribution critical value.
    Aliases: Ha, H1

    P-value #

    In null-hypothesis significance testing, the probability - computed assuming the null hypothesis is true - of obtaining a test statistic at least as extreme as the one actually observed. The smaller the p-value, the more incompatible the data are with the null model.
    Intuition: It answers: 'If there were really no effect, how often would luck alone produce a result this strong?' A p-value of 0.01 means only 1% of no-effect worlds give data this extreme.
    Mechanics: Specify H0, a test statistic, and whether the test is one- or two-tailed; then compute the tail probability of the observed statistic under the null sampling distribution. H0 is rejected at significance level alpha when p <= alpha; the 0.05 convention traces to Fisher (1925).
    Failure modes: P-values shrink with sample size regardless of economic importance, so a trivially small edge becomes 'significant' with enough trades. They are invalidated by data snooping, optional stopping, and multiple testing (testing many signals guarantees small p-values by chance), and they assume the null model, including its distribution, is correctly specified.
    Misconceptions: A p-value is NOT the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor the probability the result arose by chance alone; and p > 0.05 is not evidence of no effect (absence of evidence). The ASA's 2016 statement stresses that p-values measure neither effect size nor the importance of a result.
    Example: A fair coin lands heads 14 times in 20 flips; the two-tailed p-value under H0: p = 0.5 is 0.115, above 0.05, so fairness is not rejected - one more head (15/20) would give p = 0.041 and a rejection.
    p = P(T >= t_obs | H0) for a right-tailed test (analogously for left- or two-tailed tests), where T is the test statistic and t_obs its observed value.
    Aliases: p value, observed significance level

    Type I error #

    Rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true - a false positive. Its probability is denoted alpha and is controlled by the chosen significance level of the test.
    Intuition: It is declaring a lucky strategy to be skill: you concluded there is an edge when in truth there is none.
    Mechanics: Setting the significance level alpha (commonly 0.05) caps the probability of a Type I error under the null at alpha: the null is rejected only when the test statistic falls in the extreme critical region, which happens with probability alpha when H0 holds.
    Failure modes: The nominal alpha holds per test: running many tests (signal mining across hundreds of strategies) multiplies false-positive chances far beyond alpha unless corrected. Model misspecification (wrong null distribution for fat-tailed returns) also breaks the guarantee that the true Type I rate equals the stated alpha.
    Misconceptions: A Type I error is not 'the data were unlucky' after the fact - it is a property of the testing procedure, not of any single outcome. Nor does a small alpha eliminate false positives; it only caps their rate among true nulls.
    Example: With alpha = 0.05, a backtest of a genuinely useless signal (H0: zero edge, true) will still produce a 'significant' t-statistic about 5% of the time - a Type I error that can launch a doomed strategy.
    alpha = P(reject H0 | H0 is true); by construction of the test, alpha equals the significance level.
    Aliases: false positive, alpha error

    Type II error #

    Failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is actually false - a false negative. Its probability is denoted beta, and 1 - beta is the power of the test.
    Intuition: It is missing a real edge: the strategy genuinely works, but your sample was too small or noisy for the test to detect it.
    Mechanics: Beta depends on the true effect size, the sample size, the significance level, and the noise level: larger effects and larger samples shrink beta, while a stricter alpha (to avoid Type I errors) increases it. The two error types trade off against each other for a fixed design.
    Failure modes: Small samples are the main culprit: with 20 trades, only a very large edge is detectable, so most genuinely positive strategies fail to reach significance. Heteroskedastic or autocorrelated returns inflate the effective noise and raise beta beyond the textbook value.
    Misconceptions: Failing to reject H0 is not evidence that H0 is true - a high-beta test simply could not see the effect. Also, alpha and beta are not complements (alpha + beta != 1); they are error rates under different states of the world.
    Example: A strategy with a true 0.1% mean daily edge tested on 50 days will usually yield p > 0.05 - a Type II error - because the standard error swamps the small true effect at that sample size.
    beta = P(do not reject H0 | H0 is false); power = 1 - beta.
    Aliases: false negative, beta error

    Statistical power #

    The probability that a hypothesis test correctly rejects the null hypothesis when it is false, equal to 1 - beta where beta is the Type II error probability. It measures the test's ability to detect a real effect of a given size.
    Intuition: It is the sensitivity of your detector: high power means that if a strategy truly has an edge, your test will almost surely notice; low power means real edges slip through as 'not significant'.
    Mechanics: Power increases with the true effect size, the sample size, and the significance level alpha, and decreases with the noise (standard deviation) of the data. Prospective power analysis solves for the sample size needed to reach a target power (often 0.8 or 0.9) for a hypothesized effect.
    Failure modes: Power computed under idealized assumptions (independence, normality) overstates real power on autocorrelated, fat-tailed return data. Underpowered backtests not only miss true effects but also inflate the effect sizes of the few that do reach significance (winner's curse).
    Misconceptions: High power does not make a significant result economically meaningful - with huge samples even trivial edges are detected reliably. Nor is power a property of a single result; it is a property of the test design relative to a specified true effect.
    Example: Detecting a 0.1% mean daily edge with daily sigma 1.5% at alpha = 0.05 (two-sided) requires roughly (1.96 + 0.84)^2 * (1.5/0.1)^2 ~ 1,764 trading days (~7 years) for 80% power - far more history than most backtests use.
    Power = 1 - beta = P(reject H0 | H0 is false), where beta is the Type II error probability; increasing the sample size increases power.
    Aliases: power of a test, power

    Effect size #

    A quantitative measure of the magnitude of a phenomenon, such as the correlation between two variables, a mean difference, or a risk difference, reported separately from its statistical significance. Standardized versions like Cohen's d express the effect in units of standard deviation so studies can be compared.
    Intuition: The p-value asks 'is there anything there?'; the effect size asks 'how much is there?' A strategy can be statistically significant yet economically trivial - effect size is what reveals that.
    Mechanics: For two groups, Cohen's d divides the difference in means by the pooled standard deviation; correlation-based measures (r, r^2) express shared variance. Cohen's rough benchmarks (d = 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) are context-dependent conventions, not laws.
    Failure modes: Effect sizes estimated from small or selected samples are biased upward (publication and winner's-curse effects), and standardized measures like d become unstable when the reference standard deviation is regime-dependent - a 'large' effect in a low-volatility regime may be unremarkable in a high-volatility one. Cohen's benchmarks are frequently misapplied across domains.
    Misconceptions: Statistical significance is not effect size: with enough data, a 1-basis-point edge is 'significant' but negligible after costs. Conversely, a large observed effect that is not significant can still be real - just not yet distinguishable from noise.
    Example: Strategy A beats its benchmark by 0.6% per month with a monthly standard deviation of 3% (d = 0.2, a 'small' standardized effect), while strategy B adds 1.5% with the same volatility (d = 0.5); the effect sizes, not the p-values, drive the capital-allocation decision.
    Cohen's d = (x_bar_1 - x_bar_2) / s_pooled, where s_pooled = sqrt( [ (n1-1)s1^2 + (n2-1)s2^2 ] / (n1 + n2 - 2) ), with group means x_bar_i, standard deviations s_i, and sizes n_i.
    Aliases: magnitude of effect, Cohen's d (for mean differences)

    Sample mean #

    The arithmetic average of the observations in a sample, denoted x-bar. It is the standard estimator of the population mean and is unbiased: its expected value equals the population mean.
    Intuition: It is your best single-number guess of the true average when you can only see a slice of the data - the mean of the 250 daily returns you observed standing in for the mean of the return-generating process.
    Mechanics: Sum the sample values and divide by the sample size n. Its own sampling distribution has mean mu and standard deviation sigma/sqrt(n) (the standard error), becoming approximately normal for large n by the central limit theorem.
    Failure modes: As an estimator it inherits the arithmetic mean's fragility: a handful of extreme observations (a crash day) can dominate it, and for heavy-tailed distributions without a finite mean (Cauchy) collecting more data never improves it. Serial correlation in returns inflates its true standard error beyond sigma/sqrt(n).
    Misconceptions: The sample mean is a random variable, not the population mean - it differs from mu on every sample, and quoting it without a standard error hides that uncertainty. 'Unbiased' also does not mean 'accurate for your sample'; it is a statement about averaging over many hypothetical samples.
    Example: For n = 20 daily returns summing to 2.1%, the sample mean is 0.105% per day; with sigma = 1.5%, its standard error is 0.34%, so the estimate is statistically indistinguishable from zero.
    x_bar = (1/n) * SUM_{i=1..n} x_i, where x_i are the sample observations and n is the sample size; E[x_bar] = mu and Var(x_bar) = sigma^2/n for i.i.d. samples.
    Aliases: x-bar, empirical mean

    Population mean #

    The mean of the entire population (or data-generating process), denoted mu; equivalently, the expected value of the underlying probability distribution. It is a fixed but typically unknown parameter that sample statistics estimate.
    Intuition: It is the 'true average' you would get if you could observe every possible outcome - the quantity your sample mean is trying to approximate.
    Mechanics: For a finite population of N items, mu = (1/N) SUM x_i; for a probability distribution, mu = E[X] (the probability-weighted average). Because the population is rarely fully observable, mu is estimated by the sample mean, with uncertainty quantified by the standard error and confidence intervals.
    Failure modes: In non-stationary markets the population mean is a moving target - the 'true' mean return of one regime does not describe the next - so even a perfectly estimated historical mu has limited forward content. For distributions without a finite first moment, mu does not exist at all.
    Misconceptions: Mu is not 'the average of a big sample'; it is a parameter of the underlying process, conceptually independent of any data. And a long sample does not guarantee the sample mean is close to a current mu when the process drifts.
    Example: The sample mean of 10 years of daily equity returns is an estimate of mu, but the 2020 volatility regime change means the pre-2020 mu may no longer describe the current process.
    Finite population: mu = (1/N) * SUM_{i=1..N} x_i. Distributional: mu = E[X] = SUM x*P(x) (discrete) or integral x*f(x) dx (continuous).
    Aliases: mu, true mean

    Sample variance #

    The average squared deviation of sample observations from the sample mean, computed with divisor n - 1 (Bessel's correction). The n - 1 divisor makes it an unbiased estimator of the population variance; the naive n-divisor version is biased downward.
    Intuition: It is the variance you compute from the data you have, adjusted slightly upward because estimating the mean from the same data eats one degree of freedom.
    Mechanics: Compute the sample mean, sum the squared deviations from it, and divide by n - 1. Dividing by n instead systematically underestimates sigma^2 by the factor (n-1)/n because the sample mean sits closer to the sample points than the true mean does; its square root is the sample standard deviation.
    Failure modes: Like all moment-based spread measures it is dominated by tail observations and unreliable for heavy-tailed data; for returns with volatility clustering it also varies strongly across sub-periods, so a single full-sample s^2 averages over regimes. It is undefined (as a population quantity) when the second moment does not exist.
    Misconceptions: The n - 1 divisor is not a small-sample 'fudge factor' to ignore; it is exactly the correction that removes the downward bias, and with n = 30 the naive estimator is still about 3% low. Also, unbiasedness of s^2 does not carry over to its square root: the sample standard deviation remains slightly biased for sigma.
    Example: Five daily returns with sum of squared deviations from their mean equal to 0.001 give s^2 = 0.001/4 = 0.00025 (s = 1.58% daily); dividing by n = 5 instead would give 0.0002, an underestimate.
    s^2 = SUM_{i=1..n} (x_i - x_bar)^2 / (n - 1), where x_bar is the sample mean and n the sample size; the uncorrected version (divisor n) has E[S^2] = ((n-1)/n) * sigma^2.
    Aliases: s^2, empirical variance

    Estimator bias #

    The difference between the expected value of an estimator and the true value of the parameter it estimates. An estimator with zero bias is called unbiased; bias is an objective, long-run property of the estimation rule, not of any single estimate.
    Intuition: It measures systematic error: if you repeated your estimation procedure on many fresh samples, would the average of the estimates land on the truth (unbiased) or consistently above/below it (biased)?
    Mechanics: Bias(θ_hat) = E[θ_hat] - θ. Classic example: the sample variance with divisor n has expected value ((n-1)/n)*sigma^2, a downward bias removed by Bessel's correction (divisor n - 1). Bias combines with variance into mean squared error: MSE = bias^2 + variance, so a slightly biased estimator can be the better estimator overall.
    Failure modes: Unbiasedness is not preserved under nonlinear transformations: the square root of the unbiased sample variance is a biased estimator of the standard deviation. In finance, biases also enter through data selection - survivorship-biased fund samples make any return estimator upward biased regardless of its algebraic properties.
    Misconceptions: Unbiased is not automatically better: a biased estimator with much lower variance can have lower mean squared error (e.g., shrinking variance estimates). Bias also says nothing about any single estimate - an unbiased estimator can be far off in your one sample.
    Example: Estimating sigma^2 from n = 10 returns with the n-divisor gives E[S^2] = 0.9*sigma^2, a 10% downward bias; switching to the n - 1 divisor removes it, which is why s^2 = SS/(n-1) is standard.
    Bias(θ_hat, θ) = E[θ_hat] - θ, where θ_hat is the estimator and θ the parameter; unbiased if this equals zero for all θ. MSE(θ_hat) = Bias^2 + Var(θ_hat).
    Aliases: bias of an estimator, statistical bias

    Consistency #

    A property of a sequence of estimators: as the sample size grows without bound, the estimates converge in probability to the true parameter value (weak consistency; almost-sure convergence gives strong consistency). The distribution of the estimates concentrates ever more tightly around the truth.
    Intuition: A consistent estimator is one that is guaranteed to home in on the true value if you feed it enough data - the more you observe, the closer you get, with probability approaching certainty.
    Mechanics: Formally, T_n is weakly consistent for θ if P(|T_n - θ| > epsilon) -> 0 as n -> infinity for every epsilon > 0. The law of large numbers is the workhorse for proving consistency (e.g., the sample mean is consistent for mu); the continuous mapping theorem carries consistency through continuous transformations.
    Failure modes: Consistency is an asymptotic guarantee that says nothing about the sample sizes actually available - decades of daily data may still be 'small' for slow-moving parameters. It also collapses under structural change: no amount of data makes an estimator converge to a parameter that itself keeps moving (non-stationarity).
    Misconceptions: Consistent does not mean unbiased, and vice versa: the n-divisor sample variance is biased yet consistent, while the estimator T_n = x_n (use only the last observation) is unbiased for the mean but not consistent. 'Converges' also does not say how fast.
    Example: The sample mean of daily returns is consistent for the true mean under stationarity: its variance sigma^2/n shrinks to zero, so with 25 years of data it is tightly concentrated around mu - assuming mu has not drifted over those 25 years.
    T_n converges in probability to θ: for all epsilon > 0, P(|T_n - θ| >= epsilon) -> 0 as n -> infinity, where T_n is the estimator based on n observations.
    Aliases: consistent estimator, asymptotic consistency

    Estimator efficiency #

    A measure of the quality of an estimator based on its variance: a more efficient estimator achieves the same precision with fewer observations. An unbiased estimator is called efficient if its variance attains the Cramer-Rao lower bound, the smallest variance any unbiased estimator can have.
    Intuition: Given two honest ways to estimate the same thing, the efficient one wastes less data - its estimates bounce around the truth less, so you need a shorter track record to be sure.
    Mechanics: For unbiased estimators, efficiency of T is the Cramer-Rao bound (reciprocal of the Fisher information) divided by Var(T), so e(T) <= 1; relative efficiency of two estimators is the ratio of their variances and can be read as the ratio of sample sizes needed for equal precision. Asymptotically, efficient estimators are consistent, approximately normal, and have variance no worse than any rival.
    Failure modes: Efficiency is distribution-specific: the sample mean is efficient for normal data but highly inefficient for heavy-tailed or contaminated data, where robust estimators (median, trimmed mean) do better. Optimizing efficiency under a wrong model can therefore amplify estimation error precisely when tails matter.
    Misconceptions: Most efficient is not the same as best in practice: an efficient estimator can be fragile to outliers, so a slightly less efficient robust estimator may be preferable for financial returns. Efficiency also concerns variance, not bias - an efficient biased estimator may beat the efficient unbiased one on mean squared error.
    Example: For Gaussian data, estimating the center with the sample median instead of the mean requires about pi/2 ~ 57% more observations to reach the same standard error - the price of the median's robustness.
    e(T) = [1 / I(θ)] / Var(T) <= 1, where I(θ) is the Fisher information of the sample; relative efficiency of T1 vs T2 = Var(T2)/Var(T1). Example: for normal data the sample mean is efficient, while the sample median has asymptotic relative efficiency 2/pi ~ 0.64.
    Aliases: efficient estimator, statistical efficiency

    Law of large numbers #

    The theorem that, as the number of independent trials grows, the sample average (or relative frequency of an event) converges to the expected value (or theoretical probability). The difference between the theoretical probability and the observed relative frequency approaches zero as trials increase.
    Intuition: Luck averages out with repetition: a few trades can go anywhere, but over thousands of trades your win rate and mean return settle toward their true underlying values.
    Mechanics: For i.i.d. variables with finite mean mu, the sample mean converges to mu (in probability for the weak law, almost surely for the strong law). OpenStax's canonical illustration: Karl Pearson tossed a fair coin 24,000 times and got 12,012 heads - 50.05% versus the theoretical 50%.
    Failure modes: The LLN requires independent draws from a fixed distribution with a finite mean; serial dependence, regime shifts, or infinite-mean (Cauchy-like) behavior all break it. It is also asymptotic - it guarantees nothing about how many trials are 'enough', which in trading can exceed any feasible track record.
    Misconceptions: The LLN does not say deviations get 'corrected' - after a run of losses, the expected remaining number of losses is unchanged (no gambler's fallacy balancing). Convergence is in proportions, not in raw counts: the absolute gap between heads and tails can keep growing while the percentage gap shrinks.
    Example: A strategy with a true 52% win rate may show 45% over 40 trades but will approach 52% as the number of trades grows into the thousands - if its edge and the market regime remain stable.
    For i.i.d. X_1, ..., X_n with E[X_i] = mu: (1/n) * SUM X_i -> mu as n -> infinity (in probability, weak law; almost surely, strong law).

    Central limit theorem #

    The theorem that the sampling distribution of the sample mean (or sum) of independent observations approaches a normal distribution as the sample size grows, regardless of the shape of the original distribution - provided its variance is finite. The limiting normal has the population mean mu and standard deviation sigma/sqrt(n).
    Intuition: Averages of many small, independent influences look bell-shaped even when the individual influences are not - which is why so many statistics can be treated as approximately normal with enough data.
    Mechanics: Draw random samples of size n and compute their means; the distribution of those means centers on mu with spread sigma/sqrt(n) (the standard error) and becomes increasingly normal in shape as n grows. A common rule of thumb takes n >= 30 as 'large enough' for roughly symmetric populations, with more needed for skewed ones.
    Failure modes: The CLT requires independence and finite variance: heavy-tailed return distributions with infinite variance never converge to normal, and autocorrelated data converge far more slowly than the i.i.d. rate. The approximation is also weakest in the tails - exactly where financial risk questions live - so CLT-based tail probabilities can be badly wrong even at moderate n.
    Misconceptions: The CLT does not say the data themselves become normal, only the distribution of the mean or sum; individual returns can stay fat-tailed forever. Nor is n = 30 a law - skewed or heavy-tailed populations need much larger n, and no n suffices when the variance does not exist.
    Example: Daily trade P&Ls are right-skewed, but the mean P&L of 100 independent trades is approximately normal with standard deviation sigma/10, enabling a z-test on the strategy's average edge.
    For i.i.d. X_i with mean mu and standard deviation sigma, the sample mean x_bar is approximately N(mu, sigma^2/n) for large n; equivalently z = (x_bar - mu) / (sigma/sqrt(n)) approaches the standard normal distribution.
