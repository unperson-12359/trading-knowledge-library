Gross domestic product reviewed

The total value of the final goods and services produced within a country during a given period. In the United States it is estimated quarterly by the Bureau of Economic Analysis as the signature measure of the National Income and Product Accounts.
Intuition: GDP is the economy's scoreboard: it tells you how much the country actually produced in a quarter or year, and its growth rate is the standard gauge of whether the economy is expanding or contracting.
Mechanics: BEA sums spending on final goods and services (consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports), counting only final output to avoid double-counting intermediates. Each quarter is estimated three times (advance, second, third) as more source data arrive, and figures are seasonally adjusted and reported at annual rates.
Failure modes: GDP is revised repeatedly, so the first estimate traders react to can change materially; it is also backward-looking, ignores non-market activity and income distribution, and annualized quarterly rates can exaggerate one-off swings.
Misconceptions: GDP does not measure well-being or total wealth, and a high GDP level does not imply strong growth; what markets trade on is the growth rate relative to expectations, not the level.
Example: When BEA reported Q1 2020 GDP falling at a 5.0% annual rate, it confirmed the pandemic contraction; traders compared each release against consensus to reprice Fed policy expectations.
GDP = C + I + G + (X - M), where C = personal consumption expenditures, I = gross private domestic investment, G = government consumption and investment, X = exports, M = imports (expenditure approach).

Real GDP reviewed

Gross domestic product adjusted to remove the effects of price changes, so that output in different periods can be compared in constant purchasing-power terms. When media report 'GDP growth' they almost always mean the percent change in real GDP.
Intuition: Real GDP answers: did the country actually produce more stuff, or did prices just go up? It strips out inflation so growth reflects volume, not price changes.
Mechanics: BEA deflates each component of nominal spending using detailed price indexes (producing chained-dollar estimates), so real GDP changes reflect changes in quantities produced. Growth rates are computed from these chained series, seasonally adjusted at annual rates.
Failure modes: Deflation choices and base-year/index updates can revise history; real GDP can rise while living standards for typical households stagnate if gains accrue narrowly or population grows faster.
Misconceptions: Real GDP is not 'GDP with inflation subtracted at the CPI'; BEA uses its own chain-type price indexes, so the implied GDP deflator differs from CPI inflation.
Example: If nominal GDP rises 5% but prices rise 3%, real GDP rises roughly 2% — the growth that economists and the Fed treat as actual output growth.
Real GDP = Nominal GDP / GDP price index x 100 (conceptually); in practice BEA uses chained (Fisher) quantity indexes rather than a single deflator.
Aliases: Chained-dollar GDP, Constant-dollar GDP

Nominal GDP reviewed

Gross domestic product measured at the market prices prevailing during the period being measured, without adjustment for inflation. It moves with both the quantity of output and the price level.
Intuition: Nominal GDP is the raw dollar size of the economy; it grows even if the country produces the same amount, simply because everything costs more.
Mechanics: BEA values current-period final output at current-period prices (current-dollar estimates). Comparing nominal GDP across periods mixes volume and price changes, so analysts deflate it to obtain real GDP.
Failure modes: In high-inflation episodes nominal GDP can look strong while real output stagnates; cross-period or cross-country comparisons in nominal terms conflate price-level differences with size differences.
Misconceptions: Nominal GDP is not 'wrong' or useless — it is the right denominator for debt-to-GDP and tax-revenue ratios, since debts and revenues are nominal; it is only misleading when used to infer real growth.
Example: U.S. nominal GDP grew briskly in 2021-2022, but a large share of that increase was price inflation, which is why real GDP grew much more slowly over the same period.
Nominal GDP = sum over goods i of (P_i,t x Q_i,t), where P_i,t and Q_i,t are current-period prices and quantities of final goods and services.
Aliases: Current-dollar GDP

Inflation reviewed

A sustained increase in the general level of prices for goods and services over time, equivalently a decline in the purchasing power of money. In the U.S. it is most commonly measured by the 12-month percent change in the Consumer Price Index or the PCE price index.
Intuition: Inflation means your dollar buys less this year than last year; central banks target a low, stable rate (the Fed aims for 2%) because both very high inflation and deflation damage the economy.
Mechanics: Statistical agencies track a fixed basket of consumer goods and services and compute the average price change over time; the 12-month percent change of the index is the headline inflation rate. Persistent inflation is ultimately associated with money supply growing faster than output, though shocks (energy, supply chains) drive short-run swings.
Failure modes: Headline rates are dominated by volatile food and energy swings; substitution, quality change, and housing measurement make any single index an approximation, and base effects can make year-over-year rates jump mechanically.
Misconceptions: A fall in the inflation rate does not mean prices are falling (that is deflation) — it means prices are rising more slowly; also, one expensive item is not inflation, which refers to the general price level.
Example: U.S. CPI inflation peaked at 9.1% year-over-year in June 2022, prompting the Federal Reserve's 2022-2023 rate-hiking cycle.
Inflation rate (12-month) = (CPI_t / CPI_{t-12} - 1) x 100, where CPI_t is the consumer price index in month t.

Consumer price index reviewed

A measure of the average change over time in the prices paid by urban consumers for a market basket of consumer goods and services, published monthly by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. The headline series is the CPI for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U).
Intuition: The CPI is a giant shopping basket whose total cost is tracked every month; how fast that basket's cost rises is the most-watched U.S. inflation reading.
Mechanics: BLS collects prices for thousands of items across urban areas, weights them by consumer spending patterns from the Consumer Expenditure Surveys, and aggregates them into an index (reference base 1982-84=100). Monthly releases report seasonally adjusted month-over-month and unadjusted 12-month changes, including the 'all items less food and energy' core series.
Failure modes: CPI covers only urban consumers' out-of-pocket spending, uses owners' equivalent rent for housing, and embeds substitution and quality-adjustment assumptions, so it can diverge from any household's lived inflation and from the Fed's preferred PCE measure.
Misconceptions: The CPI is not a cost-of-living index in the strict sense and does not measure price levels across cities; a higher index in one period means prices rose, not that every item did.
Example: In June 2026, BLS reported CPI-U fell 0.4% month over month (seasonally adjusted) while rising 3.5% over 12 months — the 12-month figure is the headline inflation rate.
Percent change = (Index_t / Index_{t-1} - 1) x 100; the index itself is a weighted average of item-level price relatives with expenditure weights.
Aliases: CPI, CPI-U

Producer price index reviewed

A family of indexes published monthly by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics measuring the average change over time in selling prices received by domestic producers of goods and services. It measures price change from the seller's perspective, in contrast to the CPI's purchaser perspective.
Intuition: PPI tracks what producers charge at the factory gate or for their services; because it captures price pressure before the retail level, traders watch it as a possible early signal of consumer inflation.
Mechanics: BLS surveys over 16,000 establishments for roughly 64,000 price quotes per month and publishes indexes by industry, by commodity, and by the Final Demand-Intermediate Demand (FD-ID) system, which groups goods, services, and construction by buyer type and processing stage. Weights come from value-of-shipments data in the economic censuses.
Failure modes: PPI excludes most imports and part of the service sector, margins between producer and consumer prices vary, and pass-through to CPI is incomplete and lagged, so PPI spikes do not map one-for-one into consumer inflation.
Misconceptions: PPI is not a direct predictor of next month's CPI; sellers' and purchasers' prices differ due to taxes, subsidies, and distribution costs, and many services in CPI have no PPI counterpart.
Example: When the PPI for final demand jumped in early 2021-2022 amid supply bottlenecks, analysts read it as pipeline pressure that preceded the broader CPI surge.
PPI percent change = (PPI_t / PPI_{t-1} - 1) x 100 for a given index; indexes are weighted averages of establishment price relatives.

Core inflation reviewed

An inflation measure that excludes the volatile food and energy components, intended to reveal the underlying, persistent trend in prices. In the U.S. the standard readings are 'all items less food and energy' in the CPI and the core PCE price index.
Intuition: Gasoline and grocery prices whipsaw month to month; stripping them out gives a cleaner read on whether inflation is really embedded in the economy, which is what central banks care about.
Mechanics: Statistical agencies compute the same price index over the basket minus food and energy items and report its monthly and 12-month changes alongside headline figures. The concept of excluding food and energy originated in the 1970s when oil shocks made headline inflation noisy; the Fed's 2% target is defined on PCE, with core PCE watched as the trend gauge.
Failure modes: Food and energy shocks can persist and pass through into core (second-round effects), so core can understate true inflation pressure for long stretches; shelter measurement lags also distort core readings.
Misconceptions: Core inflation is not a claim that food and energy 'don't matter' to households — it is a signal-extraction device for policy, not a measure of the cost of living.
Example: In June 2026, headline CPI rose 3.5% over 12 months while the index for all items less food and energy rose 2.6%, showing energy drove much of the headline overshoot.
Core inflation rate = (CoreIndex_t / CoreIndex_{t-12} - 1) x 100, where the core index is the CPI (or PCE index) excluding food and energy components.
Aliases: Core CPI, Core PCE, Inflation excluding food and energy

Deflation reviewed

A sustained general decline in the prices of goods and services, equivalently an increase in the purchasing power of money. It is typically associated with contracting money and credit or collapsing aggregate demand and is considered more dangerous than moderate inflation because it raises the real burden of debt and encourages delayed spending.
Intuition: When prices fall broadly, cash gains value just by sitting still, so consumers postpone purchases and borrowers must repay debts with more valuable money — a self-reinforcing drag on the economy.
Mechanics: Deflation shows up as a negative 12-month change in a price index (e.g., CPI below zero). Causes include falls in aggregate demand, contractions in money and credit (as in the 1930s U.S. bank failures), or strong productivity gains; policymakers counter it with rate cuts, asset purchases, and fiscal stimulus.
Failure modes: As a concept it can mislead when driven by productivity or technology (good deflation) rather than demand collapse; debt-deflation dynamics described by Fisher apply mainly when falling prices coincide with high leverage.
Misconceptions: Cheaper prices are not always good: economy-wide deflation raises real debt burdens and real interest rates even at zero nominal rates, which is why central banks fight it as hard as inflation.
Example: The U.S. last experienced significant deflation in the early 1930s after widespread bank failures shrank the money supply; Japan suffered prolonged deflation in the 1990s.
Deflation occurs when (CPI_t / CPI_{t-12} - 1) < 0, i.e., a negative inflation rate.

Disinflation reviewed

A decrease in the rate of inflation — prices are still rising, but at a slower pace. It is distinct from deflation, which is an actual decline in the price level.
Intuition: Disinflation is easing off the accelerator, not going in reverse: inflation falling from 9% to 3% is disinflation even though everything still costs more than before.
Mechanics: Measured as a decline in the 12-month percent change of a price index while it remains positive. It typically follows tighter monetary policy (higher interest rates) or the fading of supply shocks; the 1980s Volcker disinflation and the 2023 cooldown from the June 2022 CPI peak of 9.1% are canonical episodes.
Failure modes: Disinflation driven by collapsing demand can precede recession or slide into deflation; base effects can also make the inflation rate fall mechanically even as monthly price gains stay firm.
Misconceptions: Disinflation does not mean prices are coming down — confusing it with deflation leads to wrong expectations about purchasing power and policy.
Example: After CPI inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022, the U.S. experienced disinflation through 2023 as the Fed's rate hikes slowed price increases without prices falling.
Disinflation: inflation_t < inflation_{t-1} with inflation_t > 0, e.g., CPI 12-month change falling from 3% to 2%.

Stagflation reviewed

The simultaneous occurrence of high or rising inflation with stagnant output and elevated unemployment — a combination that contradicts the simple Phillips-curve trade-off. The term is identified with the 1970s U.S. experience, when inflation and unemployment rose together after the oil shocks.
Intuition: Stagflation is the worst of both worlds: the economy stops growing and jobs disappear while prices keep climbing, leaving policymakers with no painless option.
Mechanics: It typically arises when adverse supply shocks (e.g., the 1973 and 1979 oil crises) raise costs and prices while reducing output, and monetary accommodation of those shocks entrenches inflation expectations. Fighting unemployment with stimulus worsens inflation; fighting inflation with tightening worsens unemployment — the 1970s dilemma that ended only with the Volcker tightening of 1979-82.
Failure modes: The label is often misapplied to any period of above-target inflation with mediocre growth; true stagflation requires sustained inflation together with economic stagnation, and the 1970s lesson (unstable Phillips trade-off) may not generalize to anchored-expectation regimes.
Misconceptions: Stagflation is not simply 'high inflation' or 'a recession'; its defining feature is the coexistence of both, which standard demand-management policy cannot fix from one side.
Example: By summer 1980, U.S. inflation was near 14.5% while unemployment exceeded 7.5% — the textbook stagflation that followed a decade of oil shocks and accommodative policy.
