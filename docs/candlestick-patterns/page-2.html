    Tweezer bottom #

    A pattern of two or more candlesticks whose lows match (or nearly match), forming an even 'tweezer' line at a price floor, typically appearing in a downtrend. Technical analysts read the repeated rejection of the same low as a minor bullish reversal signal, the mirror of the tweezer top, which gains weight when combined with other reversal shapes.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The market probed the exact same floor twice and held both times, as if supported by tweezers at that price, suggesting buyers are defending that level.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: two or more candles (consecutive or separated) share essentially equal lows; the first is typically a black candle and the second a white candle in the classic downtrend form. The matching lows define an ad-hoc support level whose break invalidates the bullish reading.
    Failure modes: Matching lows are common by coincidence, so isolated tweezer bottoms carry little weight. In strong downtrends the double bottom at the lows often gives way to further declines.
    Misconceptions: The candles need not be adjacent or similar in size; only the lows must match. It is a minor confirmation-level signal, not a major standalone reversal pattern.
    Example: A stock falls and prints a black candle with a low of 22.00, then the next session dips to 22.00 again before closing higher as a white candle. The tweezer bottom marks 22.00 as support and hints at a possible bounce.
    Aliases: Tweezer bottoms

    Three inside up #

    A three-candlestick bullish reversal pattern introduced by Gregory Morris as a confirmed bullish harami: a long black body in a downtrend is followed by a small white body contained within it (the harami), and then a third candle closing above the second candle's close. The third candle's higher close is the confirmation that distinguishes it from the unconfirmed two-candle harami.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: First the trend's selling stalls inside the prior big candle, then the next candle actually closes higher, confirming that buyers, not sellers, won the standoff.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a long black body; candle 2 is a white (non-doji) body engulfed by body 1; candle 3 closes above candle 2's close (many implementations require a white body). Shadow lengths are disregarded. Practitioners often seek further confirmation via a break of nearby resistance or a trendline.
    Failure modes: Backtested occurrence statistics show only moderate efficiency; many instances fail in strong downtrends despite the built-in confirmation. Signals forming far from support or on low volume underperform.
    Misconceptions: It is not just 'any three rising candles'; the first two must form a genuine bullish harami with body containment. It is also a Morris-era formalization, not one of the classical Japanese patterns.
    Example: After a decline, a stock prints a long black candle (50.00 to 47.00), a small white candle inside it (47.50 to 48.30), then a third candle closing at 48.90, above the second close. The three inside up confirms the harami as a bullish reversal signal.
    Three inside up when C1 < O1 (long body), O1 > O2, C2 > C1, min(O2, C2) > C1, max(O2, C2) < O1, and C3 > C2.

    Three inside down #

    A three-candlestick bearish reversal pattern introduced by Gregory Morris as a confirmed bearish harami: a long white body in an uptrend is followed by a small black body contained within it (the harami), and then a third candle closing below the second candle's close. The third candle's lower close is the confirmation that distinguishes it from the unconfirmed two-candle harami.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: First the rally stalls inside the prior big candle, then the next candle actually closes lower, confirming that sellers, not buyers, won the standoff.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a long white body; candle 2 is a black (non-doji) body engulfed by body 1; candle 3 closes below candle 2's close (many implementations require a black body). Shadow lengths are disregarded; a close of candle 3 below candle 1's close is considered stronger confirmation.
    Failure modes: Even with the built-in confirmation, many instances fail in strong uptrends where the dip is immediately bought. Empirical frequency-efficiency statistics (e.g., CandleScanner on S&P 500 data) show only moderate predictive power.
    Misconceptions: It is not any three declining candles; the first two must form a genuine bearish harami with the second body inside the first. It is a modern formalization by Morris, not a classical Japanese pattern.
    Example: After a rally, a stock prints a long white candle (60.00 to 63.00), a small black candle inside it (62.50 to 61.70), then a third candle closing at 61.20, below the second close. The three inside down confirms the harami as a bearish reversal signal.
    Three inside down when C1 > O1 (long body), O2 < C1, C2 > O1, min(O2, C2) > O1, max(O2, C2) < C1, and C3 < C2.

    Three outside up #

    A three-candlestick bullish reversal pattern introduced by Gregory Morris as a confirmed bullish engulfing: a black body in a downtrend is engulfed by a larger white body (the engulfing), and then a third white candle closes above the second candle's close. The third candle's higher close confirms the engulfing signal.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Buyers first swallow the prior down candle whole, then prove it was no fluke by closing higher again the very next session.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a black body; candle 2 is a long white body engulfing body 1 (O2 <= C1, C2 >= O1); candle 3 is a white candle closing above C2. Shadows are disregarded. Further confirmation may be sought in a break of nearby resistance or a trendline.
    Failure modes: Despite the extra confirmation candle, the pattern still fails regularly in persistent downtrends; published S&P 500 scan statistics show a substantial share of false signals. Late entry after the third candle can leave a poor risk-reward location.
    Misconceptions: It is not a synonym for bullish engulfing; it is the engulfing plus a confirming third candle. It is also a modern Morris construction rather than a classical Japanese pattern.
    Example: In a downtrend, a stock prints a black candle (45.00 to 43.50), a long white candle engulfing it (43.20 to 45.50), then a third white candle closing at 46.10. The three outside up is read as a confirmed bullish reversal.
    Three outside up when C1 < O1, O2 <= C1, C2 >= O1, C2 > O2, and C3 > C2 (with candle 3 white).

    Three outside down #

    A three-candlestick bearish reversal pattern introduced by Gregory Morris as a confirmed bearish engulfing: a white body in an uptrend is engulfed by a larger black body (the engulfing), and then a third black candle closes below the second candle's close. The third candle's lower close confirms the engulfing signal; it is the mirror of three outside up.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Sellers first swallow the prior up candle whole, then prove it was no fluke by closing lower again the very next session.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a white body; candle 2 is a long black body engulfing body 1 (O2 >= C1, C2 <= O1); candle 3 is a black candle closing below C2. Shadows are disregarded. Higher-than-usual volume on the third candle is considered supportive.
    Failure modes: In strong uptrends the confirmed pattern still fails often as the dip is bought; scan statistics show a meaningful fraction of false signals. Entering after the third candle can also place the stop far away, hurting risk-reward.
    Misconceptions: It is not simply a bearish engulfing; the confirming third candle is part of the definition. Like the other Morris extensions, it is a modern formalization rather than a classical Japanese pattern.
    Example: In an uptrend, a stock prints a white candle (70.00 to 72.00), a long black candle engulfing it (72.30 to 69.50), then a third black candle closing at 68.80. The three outside down is read as a confirmed bearish reversal.
    Three outside down when C1 > O1, O2 >= C1, C2 <= O1, C2 < O2, and C3 < C2 (with candle 3 black).

    Rising three methods #

    A five-candlestick bullish continuation pattern: a long white body is followed by three small bodies (normally black) that all remain within the first candle's range, and then a final long white body closing above the first candle's close. Technical analysts read it as a brief, contained pause within an ongoing advance; it is a continuation, not reversal, formation.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The market surges, then drifts sideways-to-down for three small sessions without giving back the surge, then surges again, showing the pullback was only profit-taking inside a healthy uptrend.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a long white body; candles 2-4 are small bodies (typically black) whose highs and lows stay inside candle 1's range; candle 5 is a long white body closing above candle 1's close. The containment of the middle candles within the first candle's range is the defining test.
    Failure modes: It is rare in its strict form, and relaxed matches (middle candles escaping the first candle's range) are much less reliable. If the fifth candle fails to exceed the first candle's close, the supposed continuation often reverses instead.
    Misconceptions: The middle three candles must stay inside the first candle's range; three strong down candles that break below it are not a 'rising' anything. It is also a continuation pattern: looking for it as a bottom reversal misapplies the concept.
    Example: A stock jumps with a long white candle (50.00 to 53.00), then prints three small black candles between 52.90 and 51.20, then a long white candle closing at 54.10. The rising three methods is read as the uptrend pausing, then resuming.
    Aliases: Bullish 3-method formation, Rising three

    Falling three methods #

    A five-candlestick bearish continuation pattern: a long black body is followed by three small bodies (normally white) that all remain within the first candle's range, and then a final long black body closing below the first candle's close. It is the bearish mirror of rising three methods, read as a contained pause within an ongoing decline.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The market drops, then drifts sideways-to-up for three small sessions without recovering the drop, then drops again, showing the bounce was only short covering inside a healthy downtrend.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a long black body; candles 2-4 are small bodies (typically white) whose highs and lows stay inside candle 1's range; candle 5 is a long black body closing below candle 1's close. Containment of the middle candles within the first candle's range is the defining test.
    Failure modes: Strict instances are rare, and loose matches fail more often. If the middle candles climb above the first candle's high, or the fifth candle fails to break below its close, the bearish continuation reading is invalidated.
    Misconceptions: It is a continuation pattern, not a reversal signal, and the three middle candles must remain contained in the first candle's range; strong white candles escaping upward negate the pattern.
    Example: A stock drops with a long black candle (60.00 to 57.00), then prints three small white candles between 57.20 and 58.80, then a long black candle closing at 55.90. The falling three methods is read as the downtrend pausing, then resuming.
    Aliases: Bearish 3-method formation, Falling three

    Abandoned baby #

    A rare three-candlestick reversal pattern similar to a morning/evening doji star but stricter: the middle doji must gap away from both neighboring candles such that its shadows do not overlap theirs at either side. The bullish version (doji gapped below between a black and a white candle) and bearish version (doji gapped above between a white and a black candle) are read as major reversal signals.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A lone doji sits isolated on the chart with empty space on both sides, like an abandoned baby; the market gapped away from the trend, froze, and then gapped back the opposite way.
    Mechanics: Bullish form: long black candle, then a doji whose high is below the prior candle's low, then a white candle whose low is above the doji's high. Bearish form mirrors this with gaps above. Because it requires two full shadow gaps around a doji, it is extremely rare: multi-decade S&P 500 scans find only a handful of occurrences.
    Failure modes: Its rarity makes it almost unusable in practice and prevents meaningful statistical validation; tiny samples give unreliable efficiency estimates. In gapless 24-hour markets genuine instances are nearly nonexistent, and relaxed matches degrade into ordinary doji stars.
    Misconceptions: It is not just a morning or evening doji star: the doji's entire shadow range must be separated by gaps from both neighbors, not merely its body. Its textbook rarity also means most chart 'sightings' do not meet the strict definition.
    Example: A stock in a downtrend prints a long black candle (low 28.00), then a doji trading entirely between 27.20 and 27.60, then a white candle whose low is 27.80 and which closes at 29.50. The fully gapped doji makes this a bullish abandoned baby rather than a morning doji star.
    Bullish abandoned baby when H2 < L1 (doji = candle 2) and L3 > H2; bearish when L2 > H1 and H3 < L2.
    Aliases: Abandoned baby pattern, Sute go

    Kicker pattern #

    A two-candlestick reversal pattern consisting of two opposite-colored marubozu-type candles: the first moves with the prevailing trend, and the second gaps open at or beyond the first candle's open and drives in the opposite direction, nullifying it. Technical analysts regard it as one of the strongest reversal formations, typically attributed to a sudden news-driven shift in sentiment.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The market is moving firmly one way, then overnight something changes so completely that price gaps to the other side of the prior open and runs the opposite way all session, kicking the trend into reverse.
    Mechanics: Bullish kicker: black candle (close near its low) in a downtrend, then a white candle that gaps up to open at or above the first candle's open and closes near its high, with no overlap of the two real bodies. Bearish kicker mirrors this. The gap plus zero body overlap is the defining feature; the gap is usually driven by a news event.
    Failure modes: In markets that rarely gap (spot forex, 24-hour crypto) genuine kickers almost never occur. Even in equities, a news-driven gap can be fully faded in the following sessions, so the 'strongest reversal' reputation does not make it reliable without context.
    Misconceptions: It is not an engulfing pattern: the second candle opens beyond the first candle's open via a gap, rather than engulfing the body from within. The two candles also conventionally show little or no shadows (marubozu-like).
    Example: A stock slides, closing near its low at 32.00 after opening at 34.00. Overnight, positive earnings news gaps it up to open at 34.10, and it rallies to close near its high at 36.50. The bullish kicker is read as an abrupt sentiment reversal.
    Bullish kicker when C1 < O1, O2 >= O1, C2 > O2, and the bodies do not overlap; bearish kicker mirrors with C1 > O1, O2 <= O1, C2 < O2.
    Aliases: Kicker, Bullish kicker, Bearish kicker

    Inside bar #

    A bar whose entire high-low range is contained within the range of the preceding bar (the 'mother bar'): its high is at or below the prior high and its low is at or above the prior low. Price-action traders read it as volatility compression and indecision, traded as a breakout setup in either direction rather than as an inherently bullish or bearish signal.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The market took a breath: the latest bar traded entirely inside the previous bar's range, coiling like a spring while buy and sell stop orders pile up on both sides of the mother bar.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: High_t < High_{t-1} and Low_t > Low_{t-1} (strict; some conventions allow equality). The body color is irrelevant. Common usage places stop orders beyond the mother bar's high and low and treats the triggered side as a continuation or reversal entry depending on context.
    Failure modes: On low timeframes inside bars form constantly and produce many false breakouts; whipsaws around the mother bar's extremes are common in choppy markets. The pattern gives no directional edge by itself, so breakout entries fail roughly as often as they succeed without trend or level confluence.
    Misconceptions: An inside bar is not inherently bullish or bearish, and it is not the same as a harami: the harami compares real bodies, while the inside bar compares full high-low ranges. A doji whose shadows exceed the mother bar is not an inside bar.
    Example: A daily bar trades between 100 and 104; the next bar trades only between 101 and 103. A trader places a buy stop at 104.10 and a sell stop at 99.90, letting the market's break of the mother bar's range decide the trade direction.
    Inside bar when H_t < H_{t-1} and L_t > L_{t-1} (strict form).
    Aliases: Inside day, IB

    Outside bar #

    A bar whose high exceeds the prior bar's high and whose low falls below the prior bar's low, so its full range engulfs the previous bar's range. Price-action traders read it as volatility expansion and a battle between buyers and sellers, with the close's location indicating which side won; it differs from the engulfing candlestick pattern, which compares only real bodies.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The market explored beyond both edges of the previous session in one bar, a burst of expanded volatility where both sides were tested and the close reveals the winner.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: High_t > High_{t-1} and Low_t < Low_{t-1}. The close near the high is read bullishly and near the low bearishly, especially at support/resistance; a mid-range close is considered weak. Its wide range makes stop placement and position sizing a central practical issue.
    Failure modes: The wide range forces wide stops, so a single failed trade is expensive; mid-range closes are especially prone to whipsaw. In trendless chop, outside bars frequently mark two-sided stop-runs rather than genuine reversals or continuations.
    Misconceptions: An outside bar is not identical to an engulfing pattern: engulfing requires only the real body to be engulfed, while the outside bar requires the entire shadow range to be engulfed. Nor is every outside bar a reversal signal; many act as continuation bars within trends.
    Example: After an uptrend stalls at resistance, a bar trades from a low of 47.50 to a high of 50.50, fully outside the prior bar's 48.00-50.00 range, and closes at 47.80 near its low. Traders read this bearish outside bar as supply overwhelming demand at resistance.
    Outside bar when H_t > H_{t-1} and L_t < L_{t-1}.
    Aliases: Outside day, Engulfing bar

    Pin bar #

    A single-candlestick price-action pattern with a small real body and one very long shadow protruding in one direction, the name deriving from Martin Pring's 'Pinocchio bar' because the long wick 'lies' about market direction by showing a rejected probe. Traders read the long shadow as rejection of a price level and use it as a reversal or continuation trigger, typically in confluence with support and resistance.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Price made a quick dash far in one direction and was slapped back before the close, leaving a long nose; the dash is treated as a lie that reveals where the market refuses to go.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: a common heuristic requires the dominant shadow to be at least two-thirds of the bar's total range, with the small body at the opposite end; body color is secondary. In strict price-action usage the bar only 'counts' as a tradeable pin bar when its wick penetrates a meaningful support/resistance level and closes back on the other side, trapping breakout traders.
    Failure modes: Pin-shaped bars occur constantly on all timeframes, so most are noise; without a key level or trend context the pattern has little edge. Failed pin bars are common enough that some traders specifically trade the failures, and the wide stop beyond the wick can produce poor risk-reward.
    Misconceptions: A pin bar is not defined by shape alone in rigorous usage; the same bar is a hammer, hanging man, shooting star, or inverted hammer in classical candlestick taxonomy, with context determining the label. It is also not a standalone strategy: practitioners emphasize that context and confirmation matter more than the bar's geometry.
    Example: EUR/USD pokes above a well-watched 1.1000 resistance to 1.1040 intraday but closes back at 1.0985, leaving a long upper wick. Price-action traders read the bearish pin bar as a rejected breakout and consider shorts below the bar's low with a stop above 1.1040.
    Bearish pin bar heuristic: (H - max(O, C)) >= (2/3) * (H - L); bullish pin bar: (min(O, C) - L) >= (2/3) * (H - L).
    Aliases: Pinocchio bar, Pinbar
