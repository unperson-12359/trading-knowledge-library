    Doji #

    A single-candlestick formation in which the open and close are equal or virtually equal, producing a very small or nonexistent real body. In technical analysis it is read as a sign of indecision between buyers and sellers; as with all candlestick patterns, it is an interpretive framework, not a proven predictive law.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Buyers and sellers fought to a draw during the period: price traveled up and down but ended essentially where it started, so neither side is in control.
    Mechanics: Computed directly from OHLC data: the real body |close - open| is at or near zero relative to the period's range (a common convention classifies a candle as a doji when the body is no more than a few percent of the high-low range). The position of the open/close within the range defines subtypes (long-legged, dragonfly, gravestone).
    Failure modes: A lone doji is a neutral indicator with little information content; it appears frequently in sideways markets where it says nothing about direction. Reversal interpretations often fail without confirmation from the next candle or other indicators.
    Misconceptions: A doji does not by itself signal a reversal; it signals indecision, and the prevailing trend can just as easily continue. It is also not rare in the absolute sense on low-liquidity or very short timeframes.
    Example: A stock rallies for a week, then prints a daily candle that opens at 50.10 and closes at 50.12 with a high of 51.00 and low of 49.20. Analysts read the doji as waning buying momentum and wait for a bearish confirmation candle before treating it as a reversal warning.
    Doji when |C - O| <= epsilon * (H - L), where O, H, L, C are the period's open, high, low, close and epsilon is a small threshold (often ~0.05).

    Long-legged doji #

    A doji variant with long upper and lower shadows and open and close at or near the same price, typically near the middle of the period's range. It is interpreted in technical analysis as strong two-sided pressure ending in a standoff, often read as a sign that the prior trend is losing conviction.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Price was pushed far up and far down during the period, but neither side could hold its gains; the market ended exactly where it began after a wide-ranging battle.
    Mechanics: Identified from OHLC data as a candle with a near-zero real body (open approximately equal to close) whose upper and lower shadows are both long relative to the body and typically to recent candles. No strict numerical threshold is canonical; charting packages apply their own body/range rules.
    Failure modes: It only shows indecision, not direction; in choppy or low-volume markets long-legged dojis are common and meaningless. Treating it as a standalone reversal signal produces frequent false positives.
    Misconceptions: It is not inherently bearish or bullish; its meaning depends entirely on the preceding trend and on confirmation from subsequent candles.
    Example: After a two-week uptrend, a stock opens at 100, trades between 95 and 105, and closes at 100.05. The long-legged doji marks exhaustion of both sides; traders watch the next session to see which way the stalemate resolves.

    Dragonfly doji #

    A doji variant in which the open, high, and close are equal or nearly equal, leaving a long lower shadow and little or no upper shadow (a T shape). Technical analysts read it as intraperiod selling that was fully absorbed by buyers, and treat it as a possible bullish reversal sign when it appears after a decline; it is an interpretive framework, not a validated predictor.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Sellers drove price sharply lower during the period, but buyers pushed it all the way back to the opening level, showing demand underneath the market.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: open, high, and close cluster at or near the same price (small tolerance), while the low sits far below, so the lower shadow dominates the range. It is the single-candle mirror of the gravestone doji.
    Failure modes: Its reversal implication fails often in strong downtrends, where the bounce back to the open is only temporary short covering. In quiet markets it occurs frequently and carries no signal.
    Misconceptions: A dragonfly doji is not automatically bullish; after an advance it can mark exhaustion instead. It requires confirmation from the following candle before it supports any directional hypothesis.
    Example: In a downtrend, a stock opens at 40.00, falls to 37.50 intraday, then recovers to close at 40.00. The resulting dragonfly doji suggests sellers failed to hold the lows, and traders watch for a higher close the next day as confirmation.

    Gravestone doji #

    A doji variant in which the open, low, and close are equal or nearly equal, leaving a long upper shadow and little or no lower shadow (an inverted T). Technical analysts read it as intraperiod buying that was fully rejected by sellers, and treat it as a possible bearish reversal sign when it appears after an advance; it is an interpretive framework, not a validated predictor.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Buyers drove price sharply higher during the period, but sellers forced it all the way back down to the opening level, showing supply sitting above the market.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: open, low, and close cluster at or near the same price (small tolerance), while the high sits far above, so the upper shadow dominates the range. It is the single-candle mirror of the dragonfly doji.
    Failure modes: In strong uptrends the rejection is often temporary profit-taking and the trend resumes, so reversal readings fail. It is also common and meaningless in flat, low-volume sessions.
    Misconceptions: A gravestone doji is not automatically a sell signal; after a decline it can instead mark seller exhaustion. It needs bearish confirmation from the next candle.
    Example: After a rally, a stock opens at 60.00, spikes to 63.00, and is sold back down to close at 60.00. The gravestone doji shows the highs were rejected; traders look for a lower close the following session before acting on a bearish reading.

    Spinning top #

    A single candlestick with a small real body vertically centered between upper and lower shadows that are both noticeably longer than the body. Like the doji, it is read as indecision between buyers and sellers and is treated in technical analysis as a possible sign of trend exhaustion when it follows an extended move; it is a hypothesis requiring confirmation, not a predictive law.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Both bulls and bears pushed price around during the period, but neither could keep it anywhere; the close near the open shows the tug-of-war ended in a draw.
    Mechanics: Identified from OHLC data: the real body |close - open| is small relative to the total range (larger than a doji's near-zero body; a common convention allows up to roughly 5% of the range before calling it a doji instead), with meaningful shadows on both sides. The close may be above or below the open.
    Failure modes: Spinning tops are extremely common, so most occurrences are inconsequential, especially within ranges. Even after extended trends, many spinning tops are followed by continuation rather than reversal.
    Misconceptions: A spinning top does not predict a reversal on its own; the following candle must confirm. It is also not the same as a doji: its body is small but visible.
    Example: After a long advance, a stock opens at 80.00, trades between 78.50 and 81.50, and closes at 80.20. The spinning top hints that buyers are losing control; a strong down candle the next day would confirm a reversal reading.

    Marubozu #

    A long-bodied candlestick with no (or almost no) shadows, so that the open and close coincide with the period's extreme prices: a white/green marubozu opens at the low and closes at the high, and a black/red marubozu opens at the high and closes at the low. It is read as one-sided dominance throughout the period and is usually interpreted as a continuation signal.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: One side controlled the entire session from the first trade to the last: buyers never let price dip below the open (bullish marubozu) or sellers never let it rise above the open (bearish marubozu).
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: the real body spans essentially the full high-low range, with upper and lower shadows at or near zero. Partial variants exist: an opening marubozu lacks a shadow only at the open side, a closing marubozu only at the close side.
    Failure modes: A single marubozu late in an extended move can mark exhaustion (a climactic final push) rather than continuation. On very short timeframes or illiquid instruments, shadowless candles occur routinely and carry little information.
    Misconceptions: It is not a guarantee the trend continues; context matters, and a marubozu into strong resistance or support often precedes a pullback rather than immediate follow-through.
    Example: A stock opens at 25.00, its session low, and grinds upward all day to close at 26.40, its session high. This white marubozu shows uninterrupted buying pressure and is read as supporting continuation of the advance.
    Aliases: Marubozu candlestick

    Hammer #

    A single candlestick with a small real body near the top of the period's range, a long lower shadow (conventionally at least twice the body length), and little or no upper shadow, appearing after a downtrend. Technical analysts interpret it as a potential bullish reversal signal because sellers drove price down but buyers recovered nearly all of the loss; it is a hypothesis that requires confirmation.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Sellers smashed price down during the period, but buyers hammered it back up to close near the open, showing the decline was rejected.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: the lower shadow (min(O, C) - L) is at least about twice the real body |C - O|, the upper shadow (H - max(O, C)) is very small, and the body sits near the high. The identical shape after an uptrend is called a hanging man; trend context, not shape, distinguishes them.
    Failure modes: In a strong downtrend a hammer is often just a pause before further declines, so unconfirmed long entries fail frequently. Its reliability also drops on low volume or away from support levels.
    Misconceptions: The body color is not decisive (a green close is modestly stronger, but a red hammer is still a hammer), and the shape alone is not the pattern: without a preceding downtrend it is not a hammer.
    Example: After a month-long slide, a stock opens at 30.00, falls to 27.50, then rallies to close at 29.90. The next day a strong white candle closes above 30.50, confirming the hammer and prompting reversal-focused traders to go long with a stop below 27.50.
    Hammer heuristic: (min(O, C) - L) >= 2 * |C - O| and (H - max(O, C)) <= |C - O|, after a downtrend.

    Hanging man #

    A single candlestick with a small real body near the top of the range, a long lower shadow (conventionally two or more times the body), and little or no upper shadow, appearing after an uptrend. It has the same shape as a hammer; technical analysts read it as a potential bearish reversal warning because the deep intraperiod selloff revealed latent selling pressure, though the reading requires bearish confirmation.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Price was dumped hard during the session and only partly recovered; the fact that sellers could push it that low at all, after a long rise, is taken as a crack in the uptrend.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: identical geometry to the hammer (small body near the high, lower shadow >= ~2x body, minimal upper shadow); the distinguishing feature is context, since it must follow an advance. Confirmation is typically a lower close on the following candle.
    Failure modes: The recovery to near the open shows buyers are still active, so many hanging men are followed by further gains; acting short without confirmation has a high failure rate. In strong trends it repeatedly fails as a topping signal.
    Misconceptions: The shape alone does not make a hanging man: the same candle after a decline is a hammer, a bullish sign. Nor is a hanging man an immediate sell signal; it is a warning that needs a confirming down candle.
    Example: After a steady six-week climb, a stock opens at 70.00, plunges to 66.00 intraday, and closes at 69.80. Chartists flag the hanging man and wait; a close below 68.50 the next day would confirm the bearish reading.

    Inverted hammer #

    A single candlestick with a small real body near the bottom of the range, a long upper shadow, and little or no lower shadow, appearing after a downtrend. Technical analysts interpret it as a potential bullish reversal signal: buyers mounted an intraperiod rally that sellers could not fully erase, hinting that demand is emerging; confirmation by a higher close next period is normally required.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: In a falling market, buyers finally staged a real push upward during the session; even though it was sold back toward the open, the attempt shows bulls are waking up.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: the upper shadow (H - max(O, C)) is at least about twice the real body |C - O|, the lower shadow (min(O, C) - L) is very small, and the body sits near the low, after a preceding decline. The same shape after an uptrend is a shooting star.
    Failure modes: Because it closes near its low, the inverted hammer by itself still shows sellers winning the session, and downtrends often continue. Without bullish confirmation it is one of the weaker single-candle reversal candidates.
    Misconceptions: It is not a shooting star: the two have identical geometry but opposite context and meaning. And it is not a buy signal on its own; the standard use requires the next candle to close higher.
    Example: In a downtrend, a stock opens at 15.00, spikes to 16.20, and settles back to close at 15.05. A close above 15.50 the next session would be taken as confirmation that the inverted hammer marked a bottoming attempt.

    Shooting star #

    A single candlestick with a small real body near the bottom of the range, a long upper shadow, and little or no lower shadow, appearing after an uptrend. Technical analysts read it as a potential bearish reversal signal: buyers drove price sharply higher intraperiod but sellers forced it back near the open, showing the rally was rejected; it is a hypothesis that needs confirmation.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The market shot up during the session but fell back to earth before the close, leaving a long tail that shows buyers could not hold the highs.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: the upper shadow is at least about twice the real body, the lower shadow is minimal, and the body sits near the period's low, after a preceding advance. Its geometry is identical to the inverted hammer; the uptrend context is what makes it a shooting star.
    Failure modes: In strong bull trends shooting stars frequently mark only brief pauses before new highs. A close near the high of the next candle negates the pattern entirely.
    Misconceptions: A shooting star is not confirmed bearish until the next candle closes lower; the pattern alone shows rejection, not a completed reversal. It also cannot exist without a preceding uptrend.
    Example: After a two-week rally, a stock opens at 90.00, surges to 94.00, and closes at 90.20. Traders flag the shooting star and treat a close below 89.00 the next day as confirmation of a downside reversal.

    Bullish engulfing #

    A two-candlestick pattern in which a small black (down) body is followed by a larger white (up) body whose real body completely contains the prior real body, appearing after a downtrend. Technical analysts treat it as a potential major bottom reversal signal because buyers overwhelmed the prior session's entire price range; it is an interpretive framework, not a proven predictor.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: After a falling session, buyers arrived with enough force to open lower and close above where the previous session opened, swallowing the sellers' progress whole.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: C1 < O1 (down candle), then O2 < C1 and C2 > O1, so the second real body engulfs the first. Engulfing is defined on bodies, not shadows (when the full high-low range is engulfed it is an outside bar). Confirmation is sought in follow-through buying on the next candle.
    Failure modes: In persistent downtrends many bullish engulfing patterns mark only one-day short-covering bounces before new lows. Signals formed in choppy, range-bound markets are especially unreliable.
    Misconceptions: The shadows do not need to be engulfed; only the real bodies matter for the classic definition. It is also not a standalone buy signal; practitioners typically demand confirmation or confluence with support.
    Example: A stock in a downtrend closes at 48.00 after opening at 49.00; the next day it opens at 47.50 and closes at 49.50. The white body engulfs the prior black body, and traders watch for follow-through above 49.50 as confirmation.
    Bullish engulfing when C1 < O1, C2 > O2, O2 <= C1, C2 >= O1 (bodies strictly compared per convention).
    Aliases: Engulfing bullish

    Bearish engulfing #

    A two-candlestick pattern in which a small white (up) body is followed by a larger black (down) body whose real body completely contains the prior real body, appearing after an uptrend. Technical analysts treat it as a potential major top reversal signal because sellers overwhelmed the prior session's entire price range; it is an interpretive framework, not a proven predictor.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: After a rising session, sellers hit the market hard enough to open higher and close below where the previous session opened, erasing the buyers' progress in one move.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: C1 > O1 (up candle), then O2 > C1 and C2 < O1, so the second real body engulfs the first. Only the bodies must overlap; shadow containment is not required. Follow-through weakness on the next candle is used as confirmation.
    Failure modes: In strong bull markets bearish engulfing patterns often mark one-day profit-taking before the advance resumes. They are also common noise in sideways ranges where neither direction follows through.
    Misconceptions: The pattern does not require the second candle's shadows to engulf the first candle's range. And it is not an automatic short signal; context (resistance, volume, prior trend maturity) and confirmation matter.
    Example: A stock in an uptrend closes at 55.00 after opening at 54.00; the next day it opens at 55.50 and closes at 53.50. The black body engulfs the prior white body, and a lower close the following day would confirm the bearish reading.
    Bearish engulfing when C1 > O1, C2 < O2, O2 >= C1, C2 <= O1 (bodies strictly compared per convention).
    Aliases: Engulfing bearish

    Piercing line #

    A two-candlestick pattern in a downtrend: a long black candle is followed by a white candle that opens below the prior low (ideally with a gap) and closes more than halfway up into the prior black body. Technical analysts read it as a potential bottom reversal signal, a weaker cousin of the bullish engulfing pattern.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Sellers gapped the market down at the open, but buyers drove price back up through more than half of the previous session's losses, piercing deep into their territory.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a long black body; candle 2 opens at or below L1 and closes above the midpoint (O1 + C1) / 2 of the first body but below O1 (otherwise it would be a bullish engulfing). The deeper the close into the first body, the stronger the conventional reading.
    Failure modes: A close that only just passes the midpoint is weak and often fails, especially in strong downtrends. On 24-hour markets like forex where gaps are rare, the opening-gap requirement is often relaxed, which dilutes the pattern.
    Misconceptions: Closing above the prior candle's high or open turns it into an engulfing pattern, not a piercing line; the close must stop within the first body. It is also not a confirmed reversal without follow-through.
    Example: A stock falls, printing a candle from 44.00 open to 42.00 close (low 41.80). The next day opens at 41.50 and closes at 43.50, above the 43.00 midpoint of the prior body: a piercing line that bulls read as a bottoming attempt.
    Piercing line when C1 < O1, O2 <= L1, and (O1 + C1)/2 < C2 < O1.
    Aliases: Piercing pattern

    Dark cloud cover #

    A two-candlestick pattern in an uptrend: a long white candle is followed by a black candle that opens above the prior high (ideally with a gap) and closes more than halfway down into the prior white body. Technical analysts read it as a potential top reversal signal, the bearish mirror of the piercing line and a weaker cousin of the bearish engulfing pattern.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Buyers gapped the market up at the open, but sellers dragged price back down through more than half of the previous session's gains, casting a dark cloud over the rally.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a long white body; candle 2 opens at or above H1 and closes below the midpoint (O1 + C1) / 2 of the first body but above O1 (otherwise it would be a bearish engulfing). Deeper penetration of the first body strengthens the conventional bearish reading.
    Failure modes: Shallow penetration (barely past the midpoint) frequently fails, particularly in strong uptrends where the dip is bought. In gapless 24-hour markets the opening-gap condition is usually relaxed, weakening selectivity.
    Misconceptions: If the second candle closes below the first candle's open, the pattern is a bearish engulfing, not dark cloud cover. The name refers only to the partial-penetration configuration, and confirmation is still required.
    Example: A stock rises, printing a candle from 60.00 open to 62.00 close (high 62.20). The next day opens at 62.50 and closes at 60.50, below the 61.00 midpoint of the prior body: dark cloud cover, read as a warning of an impending top.
    Dark cloud cover when C1 > O1, O2 >= H1, and O1 < C2 < (O1 + C1)/2.

    Morning star #

    A three-candlestick bottom reversal pattern: a long black body is followed by a small-bodied candle (the star, of either color) that gaps below the first body, and then a long white body that closes well into the first candle's real body. Technical analysts treat it as a major bottom reversal signal marking a shift from selling to indecision to buying.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Sellers push hard, then the market stalls in a small indecisive candle, then buyers take over and recover most of the lost ground, like dawn after a dark night.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a long black body in a downtrend; candle 2 has a small real body (ideally gapping below body 1); candle 3 is a long white body closing above the midpoint of body 1. The gap between bodies 1 and 2 (and ideally between 2 and 3) is the classic requirement, often relaxed in gap-poor markets.
    Failure modes: In gapless markets (spot forex, crypto) the pattern is frequently identified without true gaps, which weakens it. A weak third candle that fails to close deep into the first body often precedes renewed decline.
    Misconceptions: The star need not be a doji; any small body qualifies (when it is a doji the pattern is specifically a morning doji star). The pattern is also not complete, and not actionable by convention, until the third candle closes.
    Example: A stock slides, printing a long black candle (52.00 to 50.00), then a tiny-bodied candle around 49.60-49.80, then a long white candle closing at 51.50, deep into the first body. Chartists read the morning star as a bottom reversal signal.

    Evening star #

    A three-candlestick top reversal pattern: a long white body is followed by a small-bodied candle (the star, of either color) that gaps above the first body, and then a long black body that closes well into the first candle's real body. Technical analysts treat it as a major top reversal signal marking a shift from buying to indecision to selling; it is the bearish mirror of the morning star.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Buyers push hard, then the market stalls in a small indecisive candle near the highs, then sellers seize control and erase much of the advance, like nightfall after a bright day.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a long white body in an uptrend; candle 2 has a small real body (ideally gapping above body 1); candle 3 is a long black body closing below the midpoint of body 1. Classic definitions want gaps around the star; these are commonly relaxed in gap-poor markets.
    Failure modes: In strong bull trends the third candle's dip is often bought, and the uptrend resumes. In 24-hour markets without gaps, loosely matched 'evening stars' generate many false tops.
    Misconceptions: The star candle can be either color and need not be a doji (a doji star makes it an evening doji star). The signal is only conventionally valid once the third candle has closed deep into the first body.
    Example: A stock climbs, printing a long white candle (70.00 to 72.00), then a tiny-bodied candle around 72.30-72.50, then a long black candle closing at 70.50. The evening star is read as a top reversal warning.

    Morning doji star #

    A three-candlestick bottom reversal pattern: a long black body is followed by a doji that gaps below it (the doji star), and then a long white body that closes well into the first candle's real body. It is the morning star with a doji as the middle candle, conventionally regarded as a stronger reversal signal because the doji marks a more complete stall in selling.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: After heavy selling, the market opens lower but goes absolutely nowhere (a doji), then buyers storm back and reclaim most of the decline; the dead-flat middle candle shows sellers are spent.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a long black body in a downtrend; candle 2 is a doji whose body (and classically whose range) sits below body 1; candle 3 is a long white body closing above the midpoint of body 1. When the doji also gaps away from both neighbors' shadows, the pattern becomes the rarer bullish abandoned baby.
    Failure modes: True gap-separated doji stars are rare in 24-hour markets, so relaxed versions are common and less reliable. Even textbook instances fail when the broader downtrend is powerful and the third candle lacks volume.
    Misconceptions: It is not interchangeable with the plain morning star; the middle candle must be a doji. It is also distinct from the bullish abandoned baby, which additionally requires the doji's shadows to gap clear of both neighbors.

It is the evening star with a doji as the middle candle, conventionally regarded as a stronger bearish signal because the doji marks a more complete stall in buying.

    Three white soldiers #

    A three-candlestick bottom reversal pattern consisting of three consecutive long white candles with progressively higher closes, each closing at or near its high and each opening within the prior candle's real body. Technical analysts read it as steady, sustained buying marking a bottom after a decline; it is an interpretive framework, not a validated predictor.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Three sessions in a row of determined buying, each marching higher and finishing near its highs, suggests the market has been taken over by confident bulls rather than one-day short covering.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: three long white bodies with C1 < C2 < C3; each candle opens within the previous body (no big gaps) and closes near its high (small upper shadows). Appearing after a downtrend or at support, the pattern's steady structure is meant to distinguish genuine accumulation from a spike.

    Harami #

    A two-candlestick pattern in which a small real body is completely contained within the prior session's large real body (the reverse of an engulfing pattern); 'harami' is Japanese for 'pregnant'. Read generically, it marks a sudden loss of momentum in the prevailing trend and is treated in technical analysis as a potential reversal warning, directional (bullish or bearish) according to the trend it interrupts.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: After a big, confident candle, the market suddenly shrinks to a small candle that fits entirely inside the prior body, as if the trend caught its breath and participants hesitated.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 has a large real body; candle 2's open and close both fall strictly inside candle 1's open-close range. Bullish harami: large black body then small body after a decline. Bearish harami: large white body then small body after an advance. If candle 2 is a doji, the pattern is a harami cross.
    Failure modes: Harami are frequent and low-powered: many are followed by trend continuation, making them one of the weaker reversal families without confirmation. In tight ranges they occur constantly and mean little.
    Misconceptions: A harami compares real bodies, not full ranges; a small candle inside the prior candle's full high-low range but not inside its body is an inside bar, a related but distinct concept. It is also a warning of stalling momentum, not a confirmed reversal.
    Example: In an uptrend, a stock prints a long white candle (50.00 to 53.00), then a small candle that opens at 52.20 and closes at 51.80, entirely inside the prior body. The harami warns that upside momentum has stalled pending the next candle's direction.
    Harami when min(O2, C2) > min(O1, C1) and max(O2, C2) < max(O1, C1), with body 1 large relative to recent candles.

Technical analysts read it as a potential bullish reversal sign, interpreting the sudden contraction of range as selling pressure drying up; confirmation from subsequent candles is normally required.

    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 is a long black body; candle 2 is a small white body with both open and close inside body 1's open-close range.     Harami cross #

    A two-candlestick pattern in which a large real body is followed by a doji contained entirely within that body, forming a harami whose second candle is a doji. Technical analysts regard it as a more potent reversal warning than a plain harami because the doji shows the trend's momentum has stalled into outright indecision; it is bullish after declines and bearish after advances.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A big confident candle is followed by a session that goes absolutely nowhere and hides inside the prior body, suggesting the driving force behind the trend has abruptly evaporated.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: candle 1 has a large body; candle 2 is a doji (open approximately equal to close) whose body lies inside body 1. Bullish harami cross: large black body then doji after a downtrend. Bearish harami cross: large white body then doji after an uptrend. Confirmation comes from the next candle's direction.
    Failure modes: Doji are common, so harami crosses appear often in directionless markets where they carry no reversal information. Even after trends, many instances resolve as continuation once the indecision breaks the prior way.
    Misconceptions: It is not the same as a doji star (morning/evening doji star): there the doji gaps away from the first body, while here it sits inside it. The second candle must be a true near-zero-body doji, not merely a small candle.
    Example: After a decline, a stock prints a long black candle (30.00 to 27.00), then a doji opening at 28.10 and closing at 28.12, inside the prior body. The bullish harami cross warns of a possible bottom, pending an up close the next session.

    Tweezer top #

    A pattern of two or more candlesticks whose highs match (or nearly match), forming an even 'tweezer' line at a price ceiling, typically appearing in an uptrend. Technical analysts read the repeated rejection of the same high as a minor bearish reversal signal, which gains weight when the candles themselves form other reversal shapes.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The market probed the exact same ceiling twice and failed both times, as if pinched by tweezers at that price, suggesting sellers are defending that level.
    Mechanics: From OHLC data: two or more candles (consecutive or separated) share essentially equal highs; the first is typically a white candle and the second a black candle in the classic uptrend form. The matching highs define an ad-hoc resistance level whose subsequent break invalidates the bearish reading.
    Failure modes: Matching highs occur constantly by chance, so isolated tweezer tops have low standalone significance. In strong uptrends the double test often precedes a breakout rather than a reversal.
    Misconceptions: The candles do not have to be adjacent, and their bodies can be any size; what matters is the matching highs. It is a minor signal on its own, not a major reversal pattern like an evening star.
    Example: A stock rallies and prints a white candle with a high of 77.00, then the next session trades up to 77.00 again but closes lower as a black candle. The tweezer top marks 77.00 as resistance and warns of a possible pullback.
