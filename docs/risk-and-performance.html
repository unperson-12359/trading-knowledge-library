Historical volatility reviewed

A backward-looking measure of the dispersion of an asset's returns over a past observation window, conventionally the sample standard deviation of logarithmic returns scaled to an annual figure. It quantifies how much price actually fluctuated over a specified historical period under a stated estimator and annualization convention.
Intuition: It answers 'how wild has this market actually been?' by measuring the spread of past price changes. Calm markets show low historical volatility; whipsawing, crash-prone markets show high.
Mechanics: Compute log returns over the chosen window, take their sample standard deviation, then annualize by multiplying by the square root of the number of periods per year (e.g. sqrt(252) for daily returns). The estimate is only as stable as the window and sampling frequency chosen.
Failure modes: It assumes the trailing window is representative, so regime shifts leave the estimate stale, and sqrt-of-time annualization breaks down when returns are autocorrelated or fat-tailed. Short windows are noisy; long windows lag.
Misconceptions: Historical volatility is not a forecast: it describes what happened, not what will happen, and it says nothing about direction because it measures dispersion, not trend.
Example: If daily log returns of a stock over the last 21 sessions have a standard deviation of 1.2%, annualized historical volatility is 1.2% * sqrt(252), about 19%.
sigma_ann = sigma_period * sqrt(P), where sigma_period = sqrt( (1/(n-1)) * sum_i (r_i - r_bar)^2 ), r_i = ln(P_i / P_{i-1}) are log returns over n observations, and P is periods per year (252 for daily).
Aliases: HV, statistical volatility, historic volatility

Realized volatility reviewed

Volatility computed from returns actually realized over an interval; near-synonymous with actual historical volatility, the term is usually reserved for estimators built from high-frequency intraday returns, where realized variance is the sum of squared intraday returns.
Intuition: Where close-to-close volatility uses one price per day, realized volatility exploits the full intraday path, extracting far more information about how much the price truly moved.
Mechanics: Sample returns at a fixed intraday frequency (e.g. 5-minute), square and sum them over the day to obtain realized variance, and take the square root for realized volatility, optionally annualized by the square-root-of-time rule. Sampling too finely imports microstructure noise; too coarsely wastes information.
Failure modes: At very fine sampling, bid-ask bounce and microstructure noise inflate the estimate, while sparse or halted trading distorts it. It remains backward-looking and is an estimator with sampling error, not the 'true' volatility.
Misconceptions: Realized volatility is not implied volatility: one measures what happened, the other what option prices imply about the future. Nor is higher-frequency always better — beyond a point the estimator measures market microstructure, not price risk.
Example: Summing squared 5-minute log returns over a trading day gives realized variance of 0.0004, so daily realized volatility is sqrt(0.0004) = 2%, roughly 31.7% annualized.
RV_t = sum_{i=1..M} r_{t,i}^2, where r_{t,i} are M intraday log returns on day t; realized volatility = sqrt(RV_t), annualized as sqrt(252 * RV_t) for a daily measure.
Aliases: realised volatility, RV

Implied volatility reviewed

The forward-looking volatility value that, when input to an option pricing model such as Black-Scholes, equates the model price to the observed market price of the option. It is the market's consensus expectation of future volatility embedded in option premiums.
Intuition: Options cost more when traders expect big moves, so the premium can be read backward: the volatility that justifies today's option price is what the market collectively expects.
Mechanics: Holding spot, strike, rate, and time to expiry fixed, invert the pricing model numerically (bisection or Newton-Raphson) on the observed option price; no closed-form inverse exists for Black-Scholes. Index-level composites such as VIX aggregate implied volatilities across strikes.
Failure modes: IV is model-dependent and inherits the pricing model's assumptions; it can embed skew, event premium, and supply-demand distortions rather than pure volatility expectations. Being an expectation, it can diverge widely from the volatility that actually realizes.
Misconceptions: IV is not a directional forecast: it says how much movement is expected, not which way. High IV does not mean price will fall, and low IV does not mean it must rise.
Example: If an at-the-money one-month option's premium solves Black-Scholes only at sigma = 25%, the implied volatility is 25% annualized; if the underlying then realizes 12% over the year, IV overshot.
IV = sigma such that C_BS(S, K, r, T, sigma) = C_market, where C_BS is the Black-Scholes call price with spot S, strike K, rate r, and time to expiry T.

Downside volatility reviewed

The dispersion of returns computed only over outcomes below a chosen threshold (zero, the mean, or a minimum acceptable return), isolating the variability investors actually dislike. Its squared form is the lower semivariance, and its target-relative version is the below-target semi-deviation used as the risk denominator of the Sortino ratio.
Intuition: Upside surprises are welcome, so counting them as risk overstates danger; downside volatility measures only the harmful half of the distribution.
Mechanics: Take the return series, retain deviations below the target T (with above-target deviations set to zero), square and average them, then take the square root. The result, downside deviation, replaces total standard deviation in measures such as the Sortino ratio.
Failure modes: With few below-target observations the estimate is statistically fragile, and results are sensitive to the arbitrary target chosen. It also cannot distinguish frequency from severity: two distributions with equal downside deviation can have very different tail shapes.
Misconceptions: Downside volatility is not simply the standard deviation of the negative returns: it is computed against a target across all observations with above-target deviations zeroed, a distinction that materially changes the number.
Example: For monthly returns against a 0% target, if squared shortfalls average 0.0009, downside deviation is 3% per month; using it in place of total volatility in a Sortino ratio rescales the risk-adjusted score.
DR = sqrt( E[ (r - T)^2 * 1{r < T} ] ), where r is return, T the target (minimum acceptable) return, and 1{} the indicator function; DR^2 is the target semivariance.
Aliases: downside deviation, downside risk (measure)

Semivariance reviewed

The expected squared deviation of returns below a reference point (the mean, or a stated target), restricted to the unfavorable half of the distribution; its square root is the semi-deviation. Introduced through Roy's 1952 'safety-first' framework and endorsed by Markowitz as a more plausible risk measure than full variance.
Intuition: Variance punishes big gains as much as big losses; semivariance counts only the outcomes that hurt, matching how investors actually perceive risk.
Mechanics: Average the squared deviations of observations falling at or below the reference point: mean semivariance uses the mean as reference, target semivariance a chosen threshold T. The quantity feeds downside-deviation measures and the Sortino ratio.
Failure modes: Estimation requires many below-reference observations, so short samples are unreliable, and results depend on the reference convention (mean versus target). It discards upside information, so it cannot distinguish distributions whose lower halves match but whose upside differs.
Misconceptions: Semivariance is not half of variance: for asymmetric return distributions it can be much more or much less than variance/2, which is precisely what makes it informative.
Example: If returns below the mean have squared deviations averaging 0.0016 while full variance is 0.0025, semivariance is 0.0016 and semi-deviation 4%, indicating loss-side concentration of risk.
SV = E[ (X - E[X])^2 * 1{X <= E[X]} ]; target semivariance = E[ (X - t)^2 * 1{X <= t} ], where 1{} is the indicator function.
Aliases: semi-variance, lower partial moment of order 2

Drawdown reviewed

The decline in the value of an account, portfolio, or strategy from a historical peak to a subsequent point before a new peak is reached, typically expressed as a percentage of the peak value. Formally, for an equity process X(t), the drawdown at time T is the running maximum of X up to T minus X(T).
Intuition: It is the pain an investor endures between equity highs: how much of the peak value is given back before the account recovers.
Mechanics: Track the running maximum of the equity (NAV) series and at each point compute (peak - current) / peak. The series of these values forms the drawdown (underwater) curve, whose extreme values and durations define the maximum drawdown and drawdown duration statistics.
Failure modes: Drawdown is path- and sample-dependent: a different evaluation window changes every value, and one deep event dominates the statistic. For illiquid strategies, smoothed or stale marks flatter reported drawdowns.
Misconceptions: A drawdown is not a realized loss — positions need not be closed and the account can recover — and the deepest drawdown is not necessarily the longest-lived one.
Example: An account that peaks at $120,000 and falls to $96,000 before recovering is in a drawdown of $24,000, or 20% from peak.
D(T) = max_{t in (0,T)} X(t) - X(T); in percentage terms, DD%(T) = ( max_{t in (0,T)} X(t) - X(T) ) / max_{t in (0,T)} X(t).

Maximum drawdown reviewed

The largest observed peak-to-trough decline of an equity or NAV series over an evaluation period, usually quoted as a percentage of the running peak. Formally it is the maximum of the drawdown process over the sample.
Intuition: It is the worst pain the strategy has actually inflicted: the deepest hole from peak to trough that an investor would have had to sit through.
Mechanics: Scan the equity curve keeping a running peak, compute the percentage decline from that peak at every point, and take the maximum. It is a single-observation statistic driven by the worst episode in the window.
Failure modes: MDD grows with sample length and is dominated by one event, making it an unstable estimator of tail risk; a backtest without a severe episode shows a deceptively small MDD. For a positive-drift process, expected MDD grows with time, so MDDs across different horizons are not comparable.
Misconceptions: MDD is not 'the most you can lose' going forward — future drawdowns can exceed the historical maximum — and it is not necessarily the drawdown that lasted longest.
Example: If an equity curve runs 100 -> 130 -> 91 -> 140, the maximum drawdown is (130 - 91) / 130 = 30%, even though the series later makes new highs.
MDD(T) = max_{tau in (0,T)} [ max_{t in (0,tau)} X(t) - X(tau) ], commonly expressed as a percentage of max_{t in (0,tau)} X(t).
Aliases: max drawdown, MDD

Drawdown duration reviewed

The length of time an equity curve spends below its previous peak: the span from the peak at which a drawdown begins until equity recovers to a new high (or to the end of the sample if it has not recovered). The maximum drawdown duration is the longest such peak-to-peak interval observed.
Intuition: Depth tells you how much it hurt; duration tells you for how long. Long recoveries test investor patience and capital commitments even when losses are shallow.
Mechanics: Mark each new equity high and measure the elapsed time (or number of periods) between consecutive highs during which equity sits below the last peak. The longest of these intervals is the maximum drawdown duration, which need not coincide with the deepest drawdown.
Failure modes: Windows ending in an unrecovered drawdown censor the measurement (the end date is unknown), biasing durations downward in short samples. The statistic also depends on the sampling frequency of the equity series.
Misconceptions: Maximum drawdown duration is commonly misdefined as the duration of the deepest drawdown; it is the longest time between equity highs, which can belong to a shallower episode.
Example: A fund that peaked in January, troughed at -8% in June, and regained its high the following March has a drawdown duration of 14 months.
Duration of drawdown i = t_recovery,i - t_peak,i, where t_peak is when the running peak was set and t_recovery the first later time equity reaches or exceeds it; max drawdown duration = max_i of those spans.

Time under water reviewed

The time an investment's equity spends below its high-water mark — the duration view of drawdown, visualized by the underwater curve and summarized by longest or average episode length.
Intuition: It measures how long investors are 'drowning' relative to their peak, capturing the opportunity cost and psychological burden that depth-only statistics miss.
Mechanics: Compute the running drawdown series; each contiguous stretch of nonzero drawdown is one time-under-water episode with a start (last peak), a depth path, and an end (recovery to the peak). Reported statistics include longest episode, current episode, and average episode length.
Failure modes: Like all drawdown-path statistics it is sample- and frequency-dependent, and unrecovered drawdowns at the sample end censor the measurement. Within an episode it ignores magnitude unless paired with depth statistics.
Misconceptions: Time under water is not the same as losing money continuously: equity can rise during most of an episode while still sitting below the old high-water mark.
Example: A strategy that made its last equity high ten months ago and is still 5% below it has a current time under water of ten months.
TUW over (0,T) = integral_0^T 1{D(t) > 0} dt, where D(t) is the drawdown at time t and 1{} the indicator function.

Win rate reviewed

The proportion of closed trades in a sample that produce a profit, conventionally expressed as a percentage of all closed trades. It is a core trading-performance statistic but is meaningful only in combination with the sizes of wins and losses.
Intuition: It answers 'how often do I win?' — a psychologically salient number that, on its own, says almost nothing about whether a system makes money.
Mechanics: Count winning trades (PnL above zero after costs) and divide by total closed trades over the evaluation period. Whether breakeven trades are excluded or counted must be stated, because conventions differ.
Failure modes: A high win rate can coexist with negative expectancy if average losers dwarf average winners (short-volatility systems are the classic case), and on small samples the rate is dominated by luck. Cherry-picked windows and survivorship bias inflate it.
Misconceptions: The belief that profitable trading requires winning most of the time is false: trend-following systems often win about 40% of trades yet have positive expectancy because winners are much larger than losers.
Example: 70 winners out of 100 closed trades is a 70% win rate; by contrast, with a 2:1 reward-to-risk structure a strategy only needs to win about 34% of the time to break even before costs.
Win rate = (number of winning trades) / (total number of closed trades).

Loss rate reviewed

The proportion of closed trades that produce a loss; the complement of the win rate when every trade is classified as a win or a loss.
Intuition: The mirror of win rate: how frequently the system takes its planned (or unplanned) losses.
Mechanics: Count losing trades (PnL below zero after costs) and divide by total closed trades. Together with the win rate it defines the probability mix that, weighted by payoff sizes, determines expectancy.
Failure modes: On its own the rate is uninformative about profitability and unstable in small samples; profitable systems can deliberately accept high loss rates in exchange for outsized winners.
Misconceptions: A high loss rate does not imply a bad system, nor a low loss rate a good one: only the combination with average win and average loss — the expectancy — determines viability.
Example: If a system loses on 60 of 100 trades the loss rate is 60%; if the 40 winners average 3R against 1R losers, expectancy is still +0.6R per trade.
Loss rate = (number of losing trades) / (total closed trades); equals 1 - win rate when breakeven trades are excluded.

Breakeven rate reviewed

The win rate at which a strategy's expected profit per trade is exactly zero for a given payoff ratio (average winner to average loser), before costs. Above it the strategy has positive expectancy; below it, negative.
Intuition: It converts a reward-to-risk structure into a batting-average target: with 2R winners against 1R losers, you only need to win one trade in three.
Mechanics: Set expectancy (p * avgWin) - ((1 - p) * avgLoss) = 0 and solve for the win rate p. After commissions and slippage the effective breakeven rate is higher, because costs shrink each trade's net payoff.
Failure modes: It treats win rate and payoff as fixed when both shift with market regime, and it ignores costs, which can turn a marginally 'profitable' system negative. It also assumes the payoff structure is actually achieved at exit.
Misconceptions: The breakeven rate is not 50% by default: it depends entirely on the payoff ratio, and widening the reward target lowers the required win rate rather than raising it.
Example: With average winners of $600 and average losers of $300 (b = 2), the breakeven win rate is 1/3, about 33.3%; winning 40% of trades then yields positive expectancy.
p_BE = avgLoss / (avgWin + avgLoss) = 1 / (1 + b), where b = avgWin / avgLoss is the payoff ratio.

Average winner reviewed

The mean profit of the winning trades in a sample: total gross profit divided by the number of winning trades. It is one of the two size components (with the average loser) that, combined with the win rate, determine expectancy.
Intuition: It measures how big the typical win is — the win leg of the win-size/loss-size asymmetry that makes or breaks a system.
Mechanics: Sum the PnL of all trades with positive results (net of costs) and divide by their count. It is reported in currency, percent, or R-multiples for comparability across systems and sizes.
Failure modes: Highly sensitive to outliers: one outsized winner can dominate the mean and mask a mediocre typical win, so medians or trimmed means are often more robust. Backtested average winners shrink when slippage and fees are added.
Misconceptions: A large average winner does not guarantee profitability: if winners are rare enough (low win rate), expectancy can still be negative.
Example: If 40 winning trades made $12,000 in total, the average winner is $300; against an average loser of $150 the payoff ratio is 2.
Average winner = gross profit / number of winning trades.

Average loser reviewed

The mean loss of the losing trades in a sample: total gross loss divided by the number of losing trades. It benchmarks how large the typical loss is and anchors the unit of risk (1R) used in R-multiple analysis.
Intuition: Knowing your average loser tells you the real cost of being wrong — and whether your stops are actually doing their job.
Mechanics: Sum the absolute PnL of all negative trades and divide by their count; compare it against planned per-trade risk to detect slippage or stop violations (an average loser persistently larger than planned 1R signals execution problems).
Failure modes: A few tail losses can dominate the average, and an average loser exceeding planned risk usually reflects gaps and slippage rather than edge decay. Sample sensitivity is high at low trade counts.
Misconceptions: Keeping losers small is necessary but not sufficient: obsessively tight stops can raise the loss rate so much that expectancy turns negative.
Example: 60 losing trades losing $9,000 in total give an average loser of $150; if planned risk was $120 per trade, slippage and poor exits are adding $30 per loss.
Average loser = gross loss / number of losing trades, with gross loss taken as a positive number.

Payoff ratio reviewed

The ratio of the average winning trade to the average losing trade in a sample, measuring the realized asymmetry between wins and losses. Combined with the win rate it determines expectancy and the breakeven win rate.
Intuition: It is the reward per unit of pain a system actually delivers: two systems with equal win rates differ radically if one pays 3R winners and the other 0.5R.
Mechanics: Compute the average winner and average loser over the closed-trade sample and divide. It is the realized analogue of the planned risk-reward ratio, which is set before entry; the two diverge whenever exits occur away from the planned stop and target.
Failure modes: Outlier wins or losses distort both components, and ratio estimates are noisy on small samples. The realized payoff often differs markedly from the planned reward-to-risk because exits rarely occur exactly at stops and targets.
Misconceptions: Payoff ratio and risk-reward ratio are routinely conflated: the former is computed from realized average wins and losses, the latter from intended entry, stop, and target prices.
Example: With an average winner of $450 and an average loser of $150, the payoff ratio is 3; the strategy then breaks even at a 25% win rate.
Payoff ratio = average winner / average loser; expectancy in units of average losers = p * payoff - (1 - p), with p the win rate.

Profit factor reviewed

The ratio of gross profit to gross loss over a sample of trades: total winnings divided by total losses. A value above 1.0 indicates the system earned more than it lost; below 1.0 it lost money.
Intuition: It compresses the whole trade distribution into one leverage-like number: for every dollar the system loses, how many dollars does it make?
Mechanics: Sum the PnL of all winners (gross profit) and the absolute PnL of all losers (gross loss), then divide. Because it uses totals rather than counts, it implicitly blends win rate and payoff ratio into a single figure.
Failure modes: Extremely sensitive to outliers — one huge win can lift the ratio above 1 for a structurally losing system — and to sample size; values above ~3 over small samples usually indicate overfit backtests or data errors. It ignores trade frequency and the drawdown path.
Misconceptions: Higher is not always better in practice: maximizing profit factor by over-filtering trades can cut trade count so much that total profit falls, and a ratio of 1.0-1.2 is typically breakeven or worse once commissions and slippage are included.
Example: Winners total $8,500 and losers total $5,200, giving a profit factor of 1.63 — $1.63 made for every dollar lost.
Profit factor = gross profit / gross loss = (win rate * average winner) / (loss rate * average loser).

Trade expectancy reviewed

The average amount a trading system can be expected to make or lose per trade: the probability-weighted mean of trade outcomes, win rate times average winner minus loss rate times average loser. In Van Tharp's framework it is expressed as the mean R-multiple — expected profit per dollar of initial risk.
Intuition: It is the single number that says whether a system has an edge: how much, on average, each trade adds to or subtracts from the account.
Mechanics: From a closed-trade sample compute the win rate, average win, and average loss and combine them; equivalently, take the mean of all trade PnLs or R-multiples. Multiply by trade frequency (opportunity) for expected profit per period; position sizing then converts expectancy into actual returns.
Failure modes: Expectancy estimated from few trades is dominated by sampling error, and backtested expectancy rarely survives costs, slippage, and regime change. It is a mean: it hides variance and tail risk, so a positive-expectancy system can still be ruined by reckless sizing.
Misconceptions: Positive expectancy does not mean every sequence of trades profits, and expectancy is not a fixed property of the rules — the same system in a different regime produces a different expectancy. Position sizing cannot rescue a negative-expectancy system.
Example: A system that wins 40% of trades with 3R average winners and 1R average losers has expectancy 0.4 * 3 - 0.6 * 1 = +0.6R per trade.
E = p * avgWin - (1 - p) * avgLoss; in R terms, E[R] = (1/N) * sum_i R_i, where R_i is trade i's PnL divided by its initial risk.

Risk-reward ratio reviewed

The ratio of a trade's planned potential loss (entry to stop-loss) to its planned potential gain (entry to take-profit target), frequently quoted in inverse form as reward-to-risk (e.g. 2:1). It is set before entry and defines the price structure of the trade.
Intuition: It frames each trade as a bet: how much am I willing to lose in order to make how much? A 1:3 risk/reward means risking $1 to make $3.
Mechanics: Compute the distance from entry to stop (risk) and from entry to target (reward); their ratio is the planned risk/reward. It interacts with the win rate through the breakeven rate: a higher reward-to-risk lowers the win rate needed for profitability.
Failure modes: It is a planning construct: realized outcomes differ because of slippage, gaps, partial exits, and trailing stops, so the realized payoff ratio usually diverges from the planned ratio. Tightening the stop to 'improve' the ratio raises the probability of being stopped out.
Misconceptions: A higher reward-to-risk target is not automatically better: pushing the target further away lowers the probability of ever reaching it, and the ratio has no value without its associated win rate.
Example: Buying at $20 with a stop at $15 and a target at $30 risks $5 per share to make $10 — a 1:2 risk/reward ratio.
Risk/reward ratio = (entry price - stop price) / (target price - entry price) for a long trade; reward-to-risk is its reciprocal.

R-multiple reviewed

The profit or loss of a trade expressed as a multiple of the initial risk taken on it, where initial risk (1R) is the dollar distance between entry price and the predetermined stop-loss for the position. Van Tharp's normalization device, it makes trades comparable across instruments, sizes, and time.
Intuition: Instead of asking 'did I make $500?', ask how many units of risked capital were captured: a $500 gain on $100 of risk (5R) and on $2,000 of risk (0.25R) are entirely different trades.
Mechanics: Define R as the entry-to-stop distance times position size — the planned dollar loss. Divide the realized trade PnL by R. The distribution of R-multiples over many trades yields the system's expectancy (mean R) and variability, which drive position-sizing decisions.
Failure modes: It assumes the stop executes near the planned price; gaps, slippage, and fast markets make realized losses exceed 1R, corrupting the R-multiple distribution. It also presumes R was honestly fixed before entry — widening the stop after the fact falsifies the statistics.
Misconceptions: R is not a fixed percentage of account equity: it is the per-trade dollar risk defined by stop distance and size, which coincides with a percent of equity only under percent-risk sizing. Nor does a large-R winner prove a good entry — R measures outcome relative to initial risk, not signal quality.
Example: Entering at $50 with a stop at $48 risks $2 per share; exiting at $56 yields +$6, or +3R, while a slipped stop filled at $47 yields -1.5R.
R-multiple = realized trade PnL / initial risk, where initial risk R = |entry price - stop price| * position size.

Position sizing reviewed

The process of determining how large each trade or position should be — the 'how much' decision of a trading system — expressed as units, contracts, or the fraction of equity risked per trade. In the practitioner literature (Tharp) it is identified, rather than entry selection, as the dominant determinant of whether a system meets its objectives.
Intuition: Two traders running the identical profitable system can end with wildly different outcomes — one compounding steadily, one ruined — purely because of how much each risked per trade.
Mechanics: Given account equity, per-trade risk tolerance, and the trade's initial risk (stop distance times point value), compute the size so a stopped-out trade loses the intended fraction of equity. Models include percent risk (fixed fractional), percent volatility (ATR-based), fixed ratio, and Kelly-style fractions; aggregate caps such as daily loss limits sit on top.
Failure modes: Sizing rules calibrated on a historical R-multiple distribution fail when the regime shifts; percent-risk models suffer lot-rounding distortions on small accounts, and correlated simultaneous positions make aggregate risk far larger than per-trade math suggests.
Misconceptions: Position sizing cannot turn a negative-expectancy system into a winner — it only determines how the edge (or its absence) is expressed in the equity curve. Fixed lot sizes are not a neutral default: they break compounding and drawdown protection.
Example: With $50,000 equity, 1% risk per trade, and a 4-point stop worth $200 per contract, the percent-risk size is $500 / $200 = 2 contracts.
Position size = (account equity * risk fraction) / (per-unit risk), where per-unit risk = |entry - stop| * value per point (per contract).

Fixed fractional sizing reviewed

A position-sizing method that risks a fixed fraction (percentage) of current account equity on each trade — Tharp's percent risk model — so position size scales automatically with the equity curve.
Intuition: Bet a constant slice of the bankroll every time: you bet more as you win and less as you lose, which compounds gains and cushions losing streaks.
Mechanics: Choose a risk fraction f (commonly 0.5%-2%), compute dollar risk = f * current equity, and divide by per-unit risk (stop distance times point value) to get size, recalculated as equity changes. This anti-martingale structure grows size during winning periods and shrinks it during drawdowns.
Failure modes: Very small accounts cannot express small fractions after lot rounding, distorting risk, and a long losing streak still compounds into a large drawdown (20 consecutive losers at 2% risk is roughly a 33% drawdown). The model assumes stops execute near planned levels.
Misconceptions: Fixed fractional is not fixed ratio sizing: the former risks a constant share of current equity per trade, the latter adds contracts at fixed cumulative-profit increments. Nor is any single percent universally safe — it must be calibrated to the system's losing-streak profile.
Example: At $100,000 equity risking 1% with a $250 per-contract stop, size = $1,000 / $250 = 4 contracts; after equity falls to $90,000 the same rule sizes to 3 contracts.
Size = (f * E) / r, where f is the risk fraction of equity, E current account equity, and r risk per unit (|entry - stop| * value per point).

Fixed ratio sizing reviewed

Ryan Jones's position-sizing method in which contracts are added or removed at fixed profit increments per contract (the 'delta') rather than as a percentage of equity: the account must earn delta dollars per existing contract before one more contract is traded.
Intuition: Growth is paid for out of accumulated profit rather than account percentage: the equity must prove it can carry one more unit before that unit is added.
Mechanics: Set a delta; the equity required to trade N contracts is starting equity plus (N * (N - 1) / 2) * delta. Size steps up as profits pass each threshold and steps down in reverse as equity falls back — though the model is criticized for not cutting size automatically in drawdowns without modification.
Failure modes: Without modification it does not reduce size quickly in drawdowns, and the choice of delta relative to system expectancy is non-intuitive and easily miscalibrated. Compared with percent risk it can lag in early growth or out-scale dangerously in hot streaks, depending on delta.
Misconceptions: Fixed ratio is often confused with fixed fractional sizing; they scale on different state variables — cumulative profit steps versus current equity percentage — and behave very differently through winning and losing streaks.
Example: With starting equity $25,000 and delta $5,000, the account trades 1 contract until $30,000, 2 contracts until $40,000, 3 until $55,000, and so on.
Equity required for N contracts = E0 + (N * (N - 1) / 2) * delta, where E0 is starting equity and delta the profit per contract required to add the next contract.

Volatility targeting reviewed

A portfolio management rule that scales exposure inversely to an estimate of current volatility so that portfolio risk stays near a constant target level: taking less risk when volatility is high and more when it is low.
Intuition: If the market is twice as wild, hold half as much: keeping risk (not capital) constant smooths the return stream and prevents risk from ballooning exactly in crises.
Mechanics: Estimate volatility (trailing realized or exponentially weighted), set the exposure weight w = sigma_target / sigma_estimate, rebalance periodically, and cap the weight by a leverage ceiling. Moreira and Muir document that such volatility-managed portfolios historically raised factor Sharpe ratios because volatility changes were not fully offset by proportional changes in expected returns.
Failure modes: Volatility estimates lag regime shifts, leaving exposure highest right before a vol spike when the estimation window is slow, and gaps can move prices faster than rebalancing de-risks. Leverage caps and transaction costs constrain implementation, and the documented Sharpe improvement is an empirical finding, not a law.
Misconceptions: Volatility targeting is not return forecasting: it times risk, not direction. A constant-volatility portfolio is not a low-risk portfolio when the target itself is set aggressively.
Example: With a 10% volatility target, an asset estimated at 20% volatility is held at 0.5 weight; if estimated volatility later falls to 8%, the weight rises to 1.25, subject to the leverage cap.
w_t = min( sigma_target / sigma_hat_t, w_max ), where sigma_target is the desired annualized volatility and sigma_hat_t the current volatility estimate; exposure exceeds 1 (leverage) when sigma_hat_t < sigma_target.

Risk parity reviewed

A portfolio construction approach that allocates so that each asset or asset class contributes equally to total portfolio volatility, rather than allocating equal capital. Its best-known form is the equally-weighted risk contributions portfolio; leveraged implementations (e.g. Bridgewater's 1996 All Weather fund) scale the balanced portfolio to a target return.
Intuition: A 60/40 stock-bond portfolio carries roughly 90% of its risk in stocks; risk parity rebalances so bonds, equities, and other assets each carry an equal share of the portfolio's ups and downs.
Mechanics: Given covariance matrix Sigma, portfolio volatility sigma(w) = sqrt(w' Sigma w) decomposes by Euler's theorem into per-asset risk contributions RC_i = w_i (Sigma w)_i / sigma(w); the ERC solution equalizes RC_i across all i, typically via convex optimization. Lower-risk assets receive larger capital weights, often topped up with leverage to reach return targets.
Failure modes: It depends on estimated volatilities and correlations; correlation regime shifts — stocks and bonds falling together, as in Q1 2020 or the 2022 tightening cycle — hit leveraged, bond-heavy allocations hard. The approach embeds leverage and duration risk, and its diversification premise fails exactly when cross-asset correlations jump toward one.
Misconceptions: Risk parity is not equal weights and not necessarily low risk: equalizing risk contributions usually requires levering the low-volatility assets, so the portfolio can carry substantial embedded leverage and interest-rate sensitivity.
Example: If equity volatility is 16% and bond volatility 6%, an unlevered two-asset risk-parity portfolio holds roughly 27% equities and 73% bonds so each side contributes half of portfolio volatility, versus 60/40 by capital.
ERC condition: w_i * (Sigma w)_i = w_j * (Sigma w)_j for all i, j, i.e. RC_i = sigma(w) / N, with sigma(w) = sqrt(w' Sigma w) and Sigma the return covariance matrix.
Aliases: risk premia parity, equal risk contribution, ERC

Kelly criterion reviewed

A bet-sizing rule that maximizes the long-run expected logarithm of wealth — equivalently the expected geometric growth rate — by choosing the fraction of capital to risk on each favorable bet. Introduced by John L. Kelly Jr. of Bell Labs in 1956 and applied to gambling and markets by Edward Thorp.
Intuition: It answers 'how much of the bankroll should I bet?' by balancing edge against odds: bet too little and growth is slow; bet more than Kelly and volatility drag lowers long-run growth while ruin risk climbs.
Mechanics: For binary bets, f* = p/l - q/g with win probability p, loss probability q = 1 - p, fractional gain g and fractional loss l. For continuous returns under a Gaussian approximation, f* = (mu - r) / sigma^2. Full Kelly is maximally aggressive; practitioners typically use half- or quarter-Kelly to cut drawdowns and buffer estimation error.
Failure modes: Kelly assumes known, constant probabilities and payoffs; in markets these are estimates, and overestimating the edge pushes the computed fraction above optimal and sharply raises the risk of ruin. Full Kelly produces deep, prolonged drawdowns as a matter of course — it maximizes growth, not comfort — and ignores transaction costs and non-stationarity.
Misconceptions: Kelly does not eliminate risk or guarantee the best finite-horizon outcome: a full-Kelly bettor can expect to halve the bankroll at some point with substantial probability. Also, the market form f* = (mu - r)/sigma^2 uses the arithmetic drift mu, not the log return — confusing the two is a documented common error.
Example: A bet winning 60% of the time at even money has f* = 0.6 - 0.4 = 0.2: bet 20% of the bankroll each trial; in the classic biased-coin experiment this grows $25 at the geometric-optimal rate of about 2.03% per round.
Binary: f* = p/l - q/g, with p win probability, q = 1 - p, g the fractional gain on a win, l the fractional loss on a loss. Continuous (Gaussian): f* = (mu - r) / sigma^2, with mu the expected return, r the risk-free rate, sigma^2 the return variance.
Aliases: Kelly strategy, Kelly bet, growth-optimal fraction

Fractional Kelly reviewed

Betting a fixed fraction (commonly one-half or one-quarter) of the full Kelly fraction rather than the full amount, trading some expected growth for a large reduction in volatility, drawdown depth, and sensitivity to estimation error.
Intuition: Because the growth-rate curve is nearly flat near its peak, half-Kelly sacrifices only about a quarter of the growth rate while roughly halving volatility — a bargain most practitioners gladly take.
Mechanics: Compute the full Kelly fraction f* and apply a multiplier c in (0,1): f = c * f*. Fractional Kelly corresponds to maximizing expected utility for power (CRRA) utility functions and is the standard practical answer to uncertain edge estimates.
Failure modes: It reduces but does not remove the core Kelly problem: wrong edge estimates still produce wrong sizes, and the 'safe' fraction is itself chosen judgmentally. Even half-Kelly can produce severe drawdowns when the true edge is smaller than believed.
Misconceptions: Fractional Kelly is not a different formula — it is a scaled Kelly bet — and half-Kelly is not half as risky in the tail sense: it mainly tempers the path, not the possibility of large drawdowns.
Example: If full Kelly on an estimated edge says risk 24% of equity, a half-Kelly practitioner risks 12%, retaining about three-quarters of the maximum growth rate for roughly half the volatility.
f = c * f*, where f* is the full Kelly fraction and c the chosen fraction (e.g. 0.5 for half-Kelly).

Stop-loss level reviewed

The predetermined price at which an open position is closed to cap its loss, implemented as a stop (stop-loss) order that triggers a market — or, for stop-limit, a limit — order once the stop price trades. It defines the trade's initial risk (1R).
Intuition: It is the pre-commitment to admit being wrong at a specific price, converting an open-ended loss into a bounded, planned one.
Mechanics: A sell-stop sits below the market for long positions (a buy-stop above for shorts); when the stop price trades, the order becomes a market order and fills at the next available price. Stops are placed by price structure (support/resistance), percentage, or volatility (ATR multiples), and the entry-to-stop distance drives position size.
Failure modes: Triggered stops become market orders and can fill far through the stop price in gaps or fast markets — realized loss can be several times the planned 1R. Stops at obvious levels cluster and invite stop-running, and overly tight stops raise loss frequency enough to destroy expectancy.
Misconceptions: A stop-loss does not guarantee the exit price — it guarantees execution, not price — and stops do not create edge: they define per-trade risk, which is different from making a system profitable.
Example: Buying at $50 with a sell-stop at $48 caps the planned loss at $2 per share; in an overnight gap to $45 the market order fills near $45, tripling the planned loss.
Initial risk per unit = |entry price - stop price|; position size = (equity * risk fraction) / (initial risk per unit).

Take-profit level reviewed

The predetermined price at which a position is closed to lock in a planned gain, implemented as a limit (take-profit) order resting at the target price. Together with the stop-loss it defines the trade's planned risk-reward ratio.
Intuition: It is the pre-commitment to get paid: exit automatically when the market hands you the planned profit instead of hoping for more.
Mechanics: A limit order is placed at the target (above entry for longs, below for shorts) and executes only at the target price or better. Targets are typically set from technical structure (resistance, measured moves) or as a multiple of initial risk (2R-3R).
Failure modes: Targets execute at the limit price regardless of subsequent behavior, capping the upside of large trends (opportunity cost) — a chronic drag on trend-following expectancy. Price can also reverse a tick in front of the target, turning a planned win into a full stop-out.
Misconceptions: A take-profit order is not a guarantee of profit — the market may never reach it — and mechanically tight targets raise the win rate while quietly degrading total expectancy.
Example: Long at $20 with a stop at $15 and a take-profit limit at $30 creates a 1:2 risk/reward; if price tags $30 the limit order fills and the position closes for a 2R gain.
Planned reward per unit = |target price - entry price|; planned reward-to-risk = |target - entry| / |entry - stop|.

Trailing stop reviewed

A stop order whose trigger price follows the market at a fixed distance in points or percent, ratcheting in the direction of profit and never retreating, so it locks in progressively more of an unrealized gain while leaving room for the trade to run.
Intuition: It is a stop that only moves your way: each new favorable extreme drags the exit level behind it, converting paper profits into protected profits.
Mechanics: For a long position the trailing stop sits at the highest price since entry minus the trail amount and updates upward with each new high but never downward; a touch of the stop price converts it to a market order (or limit order for the trailing stop-limit variant). Trail distances are set in absolute terms, percent, or volatility units (ATR multiples).
Failure modes: In sideways chop the trailing stop repeatedly exits at local reversals (death by a thousand cuts), and like any stop it fills through the trigger in gaps. Too tight a trail strangles trends; too loose surrenders most of the open profit.
Misconceptions: A trailing stop does not trail the current price downward — for longs it never moves down — and it is not guaranteed to execute at the trailed price, since it becomes a market order once triggered.
Example: Bought at $10 with a $1 trailing stop (initial trigger $9); if price rises to $15 the stop ratchets to $13.50, and a later fall to $13.50 exits with a $3.50 gain.
Stop_t = max( Stop_{t-1}, P_max,t - d ) for a long, where P_max,t is the highest price since entry and d the trailing distance in points or percent.

Time stop reviewed

An exit rule that closes a position after a predetermined amount of time has elapsed, regardless of price, on the rationale that a setup whose expected move has not occurred within its typical window no longer justifies the capital at risk.
Intuition: Good trades usually work promptly; if nothing has happened by the time the edge should have played out, free the capital for a better opportunity.
Mechanics: Define a maximum holding period per setup, ideally from backtested MFE-duration statistics (e.g. most winners peak within N bars), and exit at market when time expires if neither target nor stop has been hit. It is usually combined with price-based exits, whichever triggers first.
Failure modes: A time stop can systematically cut slow-developing winners just before they move if the window was set from a non-representative sample, and it ignores regime changes that stretch or compress the useful holding period.
Misconceptions: A time stop is not a prediction that price will reverse: it is a capital-efficiency rule, and unlike a stop-loss it bounds no loss — loss size must still be limited by a price stop.
Example: If 90% of a scalp setup's winners reach their maximum favorable excursion within 5 minutes, the trader exits any such trade still open after 7-8 minutes, win or lose.
Exit at t_entry + T_max if no price-based exit has triggered, where T_max is the preset holding limit in minutes, bars, or sessions.

Volatility stop reviewed

A stop-loss placed at a multiple of a volatility measure — typically Average True Range — away from entry or from a reference extreme, so the exit distance adapts to current market conditions instead of being a fixed number of points or percent.
Intuition: A 2-point stop is noise in a wild market and a chasm in a quiet one; sizing the stop by ATR keeps the exit outside normal random fluctuation in any regime.
Mechanics: Compute ATR (Wilder's average of true range, which incorporates gaps) and place the initial or trailing stop at k * ATR beyond the entry or the most favorable extreme (a chandelier-style exit trails the highest high by about 3 * ATR). Wider volatility widens the stop and, through position sizing, shrinks the position.
Failure modes: In volatility spikes the widened stop implies much larger dollar risk unless size is re-cut; in volatility collapses the stop tightens into noise and gets hit by routine fluctuation. ATR is backward-looking, so sudden regime jumps leave the stop miscalibrated.
Misconceptions: A volatility stop is not inherently wider or looser than a fixed stop — it is condition-adaptive — and an ATR-based distance does not protect through gaps: true range embeds gaps in the measurement, not in the execution.
Example: With a 14-day ATR of 2.5 points, a long entered at 100 carries a volatility stop at 100 - 2 * 2.5 = 95; if ATR later doubles, a new position's stop sits 10 points away instead.
Stop = entry - k * ATR(n) for a long (or highest high since entry - k * ATR(n) when trailing), with k typically 2-3 and n commonly 14; TR = max( H - L, |H - C_prev|, |L - C_prev| ).

Risk limit reviewed

A pre-set quantitative bound on the risk a trader, desk, or account may carry — expressed as maximum position size, leverage, loss amount, VaR, or margin usage — enforced by the firm, broker, or exchange to keep exposures within tolerance.
Intuition: Risk limits are the guardrails: they decide in advance how much damage one trader or one book is permitted to do before the system cuts it off.
Mechanics: Limits are set on measurable quantities (position value, daily loss, margin tier, VaR) and monitored in real time; breaches trigger warnings, order blocking, position reduction, or forced de-risking. On derivatives venues, tiered risk limits raise margin requirements as position value grows — Bybit's risk-limit ladder, which steps up initial and maintenance margin by tier, is a worked example.
Failure modes: Limits set off stale volatility or correlation assumptions are too loose exactly in crises, and hard limits concentrate forced selling at shared thresholds across many participants, amplifying moves. Gaps can blow through loss-based limits before any control can act.
Misconceptions: A risk limit is not a risk measure: it caps a quantity but says nothing about the distribution of outcomes within the cap, and compliance with limits is not the same as being safe.
Example: On Bybit, an account at 90x leverage on BTCUSDT is capped at a 2.6M position value; orders that would take the position to 3M are blocked unless leverage is reduced to raise the tier's cap.

Daily loss limit reviewed

A risk control that stops or restricts trading for the rest of the day once cumulative losses — realized plus, in strict versions, floating P/L on open positions — reach a predetermined amount, typically a fixed sum or a percentage of equity.
Intuition: It is the circuit breaker for a bad day: cap the damage a single session can do and force the trader to step away before tilt and revenge trading compound the loss.
Mechanics: Track day P/L against the limit; on breach, flatten positions and/or block new orders until the next session. Prop-firm variants compute the limit from a daily reference balance — e.g. FTMO: previous midnight balance minus 5% of initial capital, checked continuously against equity including open positions, commissions, and swaps.
Failure modes: Gaps and illiquidity can push losses well beyond the limit before positions can be closed, and limit-aware traders may size up recklessly early in the day or hoard risk into the reset. A daily cap does not bound total drawdown, which accumulates across many within-limit days.
Misconceptions: The limit is usually measured on equity, not closed balance — open floating losses count in real time — and it resets against the latest reference balance, so the floor moves day to day rather than staying fixed.
Example: On a $200,000 evaluation account with a 5% maximum daily loss, equity may never fall below $190,000 on day one; if the midnight balance rises to $204,000, the next day's floor moves to $194,000.

Weekly loss limit reviewed

A risk control that suspends or down-sizes trading for the remainder of the week once cumulative weekly losses reach a preset threshold, layering a slower-timescale brake on top of daily loss limits.
Intuition: Bad weeks cluster when a system is out of sync with the regime; the weekly cap forces a pause and review before a rough patch becomes a ruined month.
Mechanics: Aggregate realized (and, in strict implementations, unrealized) P/L from the week's reference equity; on breach, flatten and/or halt trading until the next week or cut size by a fixed factor such as 50%. Practitioner conventions commonly run about 3-6% of weekly starting equity.
Failure modes: Like any path-based cap it can be jumped by gaps and is arbitrary relative to the strategy's actual loss distribution; rigid weekly stops can also lock in losses at the worst point of a mean-reverting stretch.
Misconceptions: A weekly limit is not a substitute for per-trade risk control — a single oversized trade can consume it in minutes — and hitting it repeatedly signals a system problem, not merely bad luck.
Example: A trader with a 5% weekly limit on $100,000 who loses $5,000 by Thursday stops trading until Monday, when the limit resets against the new reference equity.

Maximum position limit reviewed

A cap on the size of the net long or net short position a single person or account may hold or control in a given instrument, set by regulators or exchanges (speculative position limits) and by firms internally, to curb concentration and market-cornering risk.
Intuition: No single player should be big enough to break the market or break themselves; the limit predetermines how much is too much.
Mechanics: U.S. federal speculative position limits (17 CFR Part 150) cap spot-month and non-spot-month positions in referenced commodity contracts on a futures-equivalent basis, with exemptions for bona fide hedging; positions under common ownership (10%+) or control are aggregated. Exchanges set their own limits or accountability levels, and crypto venues implement analogous tiered caps on position value per leverage tier.
Failure modes: Limits bind only within one venue or regime: exposure can be rebuilt in exempted instruments, offshore venues, or economically equivalent swaps before anti-evasion rules catch up. They also do nothing about many sub-limit positions correlating in a crisis.
Misconceptions: Position limits are not margin requirements — one caps quantity, the other prices collateral — and the visible cap is not the whole story, since bona fide hedgers and certain entities receive exemptions.
Example: Under 17 CFR 150.2, no person may hold or control spot-month positions in a core referenced futures contract above the Commission-set level, net long or net short, absent an exemption such as a bona fide hedge.

Gross exposure reviewed

The total absolute value of a portfolio's long and short positions, conventionally expressed as a percentage of capital: long exposure plus short exposure, without netting. Values above 100% indicate the use of leverage.
Intuition: It measures how much exposure the fund is carrying in total — the gross weight on the scale, regardless of how the bets offset each other.
Mechanics: Sum the absolute market values of all long and short positions and divide by net asset value. Gross exposure is the usual base for management-fee calculation and the direct gauge of how much capital is at work and how levered the book is.
Failure modes: Gross exposure says nothing about direction or hedging quality: a 250% gross book may be tightly hedged or one basis trade from disaster. It also treats all positions alike, ignoring their individual volatilities and correlations.
Misconceptions: Low net exposure does not mean low risk if gross exposure is high — a 0% net, 300% gross fund can lose heavily when longs and shorts move adversely at once — and gross above 100% by definition means leverage is in use.
Example: A fund with $200M capital holding $350M long and $150M short has gross exposure of $500M, or 250% of capital.
Gross exposure = (|long positions| + |short positions|) / net asset value * 100%.

Net exposure reviewed

The difference between a portfolio's long and short exposures, expressed as a percentage of capital: long exposure minus short exposure. It measures net directional sensitivity to broad market moves.
Intuition: It is the portfolio's lean: net long books gain when markets rise, net short books gain when they fall, and zero net is directionally market-neutral.
Mechanics: Subtract total short market value from total long market value and divide by net asset value. Managers adjust net exposure with their outlook; it is the first-order determinant of how the fund responds to a market-wide move, though it hides leverage, which gross exposure reveals.
Failure modes: Net exposure assumes longs and shorts respond to 'the market' symmetrically; when they decouple — crowded shorts squeezing while longs fall — a low-net book can still bleed heavily. It ignores factor, sector, and beta mismatches between the legs.
Misconceptions: Net zero exposure is not zero risk: it is only first-order directional neutrality, and leverage, factor tilts, and basis between the long and short legs remain fully exposed.
Example: A fund 60% long and 40% short has 100% gross and 20% net exposure: it participates in market direction at about one-fifth weight while carrying a full book of positions.
Net exposure = (|long positions| - |short positions|) / net asset value * 100%.

Directional exposure reviewed

The portion of a portfolio's exposure that profits or loses with the direction of the underlying market — the signed, unhedged sensitivity to price moves, as distinct from offsetting relative-value exposure.
Intuition: It answers 'which way does this book need the market to go?': the residual bet left after hedges and offsets are accounted for.
Mechanics: Estimated as net exposure or, more refined, beta-adjusted net exposure: signed position values divided by capital, weighting each position by its beta or factor sensitivity so that a high-beta long and a low-beta short are not treated as offsetting one-for-one.
Failure modes: Beta-adjusted versions inherit estimation error in betas, which are unstable and regime-dependent, while simple net-exposure versions miss factor tilts entirely. In dislocations, correlations converge and previously 'nondirectional' books reveal hidden directionality.
Misconceptions: Directional exposure is not the same as position count or gross size: a large two-sided book can carry little directional risk, while a modest unhedged position can be entirely directional.
Example: Long $1M of a broad index portfolio and short $200k of a large constituent: the short trims the book's directional (net) exposure by $200k against the index.
Directional (beta-adjusted) exposure = sum_i ( w_i * beta_i ), where w_i is the signed position weight and beta_i its sensitivity to the reference market; the simple form is net exposure = (longs - shorts) / NAV.
Aliases: market direction exposure, delta exposure (book level)

Effective leverage reviewed

The ratio of a portfolio's total position exposure to its equity — typically gross exposure divided by net asset value — expressing how many dollars of market exposure are controlled per dollar of capital.
Intuition: It is the real multiplier on your capital: effective leverage of 3x means a 10% adverse market move costs about 30% of equity, before hedging effects.
Mechanics: Sum absolute position values (or notionals for derivatives) and divide by account equity; for a single margined position it approximates position notional divided by margin posted. It changes dynamically as prices move and as equity or margin balances fluctuate.
Failure modes: Gross-based effective leverage ignores netting and correlation, overstating risk for hedged books, while notional-based versions understate risk for convex instruments such as options, whose delta-adjusted exposure moves. It also spikes passively when equity falls, tightening margin pressure in downturns.
Misconceptions: An account's leverage setting (e.g. 10x) is a cap, not the leverage actually in use: effective leverage is what the open positions imply at a given moment, and it drifts with every price tick.
Example: $200,000 of positions on $50,000 of equity is 4x effective leverage; a 5% adverse move then costs roughly $10,000, or 20% of equity.
Effective leverage = gross exposure / equity = (|longs| + |shorts|) / NAV; for one margined position, approximately position notional / margin posted.

Variation margin reviewed

The daily (or intraday) cash settlement of gains and losses on a margined derivatives position: as positions are marked to market, losers pay winners through the clearing system so that gains and losses are realized incrementally rather than at expiry.
Intuition: Instead of letting P/L accumulate as an IOU until the end, the clearinghouse settles up every day: yesterday's loss is today's cash payment.
Mechanics: At each settlement the change in the position's mark-to-market value is credited or debited to the margin account; if the balance falls below maintenance margin, the holder must restore it to the initial margin level (a margin call). This daily cash exchange is what keeps counterparty exposure near zero on cleared futures markets.
Failure modes: Variation margin demands intraday liquidity: even a hedged or ultimately profitable position can force ruinous cash calls during violent moves (the 2022 LME nickel episode is the canonical case). Procyclical variation-margin flows can force fire sales that deepen the move itself.
Misconceptions: Variation margin is not an extra cost or fee — it is the settlement of accrued P/L — and it differs from initial margin, which is collateral posted against future moves, not payment for past ones.
Example: Long one futures contract with a $50 multiplier: an 8-point rise in the settlement price credits $400 that day; a 10-point fall the next day debits $500 from the margin account.
Variation margin_t = (settlement price_t - settlement price_{t-1}) * contract multiplier * number of contracts, credited to winners and debited from losers.

Margin call reviewed

A demand by the broker or clearinghouse to deposit additional funds, or reduce positions, when account equity falls below the maintenance margin requirement, restoring the account to the initial margin level.
Intuition: It is the lender's alarm bell: your cushion is gone, so top it up now or positions start getting sold for you.
Mechanics: Positions are marked to market; when the account balance drops below maintenance margin, the call is issued for the shortfall back up to initial margin. If the call is not met, the broker may reduce or liquidate positions unilaterally to bring the account back into compliance.
Failure modes: In gap moves the account can jump straight past the margin-call stage to negative equity, leaving the client owing the broker; calls cluster in selloffs, forcing synchronized deleveraging that worsens the move (a margin spiral).
Misconceptions: Meeting a margin call does not recover the loss that triggered it — it merely restores collateral — and a margin call is not a suggestion: failing it hands the broker the right to liquidate without consent.
Example: With initial margin $8,000 and maintenance margin $6,500, an account falling to $4,200 triggers a call to deposit $3,800 back to the initial level or face liquidation (CME's worked example).
Call amount = initial margin requirement - current account equity, issued when equity < maintenance margin.

Liquidation price reviewed

The mark price at which a leveraged position's margin balance falls to the maintenance margin requirement, triggering the venue's liquidation process. On crypto derivatives venues it is derived from entry price, leverage, and the tiered maintenance margin rate.
Intuition: It is the line in the sand: if the market prints there, the exchange takes over and closes the position because your collateral can no longer safely cover the loss.
Mechanics: For an isolated-margin long, the liquidation price is approximately entry * (1 - 1/leverage) / (1 - MMR), symmetrically for shorts, where MMR is the maintenance margin rate for the position's tier. As price approaches it, laddered liquidation may first cut the position to a lower risk tier; the bankruptcy price — where the position margin is fully exhausted — lies beyond the liquidation price.
Failure modes: The displayed liquidation price is a live estimate, not a fixed level: funding fees, commissions, and unrealized P/L shift the margin balance and move it. In gaps or thin books the actual fill can be far worse than the trigger, and in cross-margin mode one position's losses can drag healthy positions into the same liquidation cascade.
Misconceptions: Liquidation price is not the bankruptcy price: liquidation begins while some margin remains (at the maintenance requirement), and the bankruptcy price is only reached if the engine cannot close the position in time.
Example: A 10x isolated long entered at $50,000 with a 0.5% maintenance margin rate has an approximate liquidation price of 50,000 * (1 - 0.1) / (1 - 0.005), about $45,226.
Long: P_liq ~= P_entry * (1 - 1/L) / (1 - MMR); Short: P_liq ~= P_entry * (1 + 1/L) / (1 + MMR), with L the leverage and MMR the maintenance margin rate for the position's risk tier.
Aliases: liq price, liquidation threshold

Forced liquidation reviewed

The mandatory closing of a position by the broker, clearinghouse, or exchange without the holder's consent, triggered when account equity falls below the maintenance margin and margin calls are not — or cannot be — met.
Intuition: When you can't or won't post more collateral, the venue closes the trade for you, at whatever price the market gives, to stop the loss from eating other people's money.
Mechanics: Once equity breaches maintenance margin, the liquidation process takes over: on futures venues the position may be liquidated automatically, and on crypto venues a laddered process first reduces the position to lower risk tiers. Any residual loss beyond the bankruptcy price passes to the insurance fund or, failing that, to auto-deleveraging.
Failure modes: Forced selling is pro-cyclical: cascades of liquidations push prices further, triggering more liquidations in a spiral, and fills occur at distressed prices with maximal slippage. In extreme moves the engine can fail to close above the bankruptcy price, creating bad debt.
Misconceptions: Forced liquidation is not a penalty or fee event — it is a risk-control mechanism — and the trader chooses neither timing nor price; partial liquidation to a lower tier can precede full liquidation, so it is not always all-or-nothing.
Example: An account whose balance falls below maintenance margin and cannot meet the call has its futures position liquidated automatically, as CME's margin rules describe; on crypto venues the engine reduces the position tier by tier before full close-out.
Trigger condition: account equity < maintenance margin requirement for the open position, with equity computed at the mark price.
Aliases: forced close-out, auto-liquidation

Deleveraging reviewed

The reduction of leverage — paying down debt or shrinking positions relative to equity — by a trader, firm, or, in aggregate, an economy; at the portfolio level it means cutting gross exposure to lower the amplification of gains and losses.
Intuition: Leverage multiplies both ways; deleveraging is deliberately shrinking the multiplier, usually after losses, in order to survive.
Mechanics: Raise cash by selling assets or reducing derivatives notionals and use it to repay borrowing or release margin. Forced deleveraging after drawdowns or margin hikes is self-reinforcing across participants: everyone selling into falling markets pushes prices lower, triggering further deleveraging in loss and margin spirals.
Failure modes: Synchronized deleveraging is a systemic amplifier: asset sales depress prices, which erodes equity, which forces more sales — the dynamic formalized in liquidity-spiral models. Individually, deleveraging locks in losses and forfeits the recovery.
Misconceptions: Deleveraging is usually involuntary, driven by lenders and margin calls rather than foresight, and during such episodes it is selling pressure — not fundamental revaluation — that dominates prices.
Example: A fund running 5x gross exposure that suffers a 20% drawdown and sees prime-broker margins raised might cut positions to 2x, selling into a falling market and mechanically worsening its own marks.
Leverage after deleveraging: L' = (debt or exposure) / equity, reduced by selling assets or adding equity; the target is often set by margin requirements or risk limits.
Aliases: de-gearing

Auto-deleveraging reviewed

A last-resort loss-allocation mechanism on leveraged crypto derivatives venues: when a liquidated position cannot be closed above its bankruptcy price and the insurance fund cannot absorb the shortfall, the exchange forcibly reduces profitable opposing positions at the bankruptcy price to cover the deficit.
Intuition: If the loser is bankrupt and the insurance fund is empty, the winners' positions are trimmed to make the books balance: the system socializes the residual loss to stay solvent.
Mechanics: ADL triggers when the insurance fund is depleted or its drawdown breaches venue-specific trigger lines. Opposing positions are ranked by leveraged return (profit percentage scaled by margin rate/effective leverage), and the highest-ranked positions are deleveraged first at the bankruptcy price; affected traders are notified and their open orders canceled. Queue-indicator lights show a position's ADL ranking.
Failure modes: ADL hits exactly the most profitable, most leveraged traders at the worst moment, destroying hedges — an ADL'd hedge leg leaves the other leg naked — and imposing execution at the bankruptcy price rather than market. The ranking is pro-cyclical: the better your trade, the more likely it is to be cut in a crash.
Misconceptions: ADL is not ordinary liquidation and not a fee: your position is closed against someone else's bankruptcy, not your own margin failure, and high ADL-indicator lights signal queue priority, not the probability of your own liquidation.
Example: When a bankrupt long's losses exceed the insurance fund, the exchange force-closes 5,000 contracts of the top-ranked profitable short at the bankruptcy price, shrinking that trader's position while keeping the venue solvent.
ADL ranking (isolated margin, in profit): leveraged return = position PnL(%) * position margin rate; selected positions are settled at the bankruptcy price of the liquidated position taken over.
Aliases: ADL, auto-deleveraging system

Insurance fund reviewed

A reserve pool maintained by a crypto derivatives exchange, funded by venue contributions and by residual margin from liquidations closed better than the bankruptcy price, used to cover losses when a liquidated position's losses exceed its margin (bad debt) before auto-deleveraging is invoked.
Intuition: It is the venue's shock absorber between ordinary liquidations and socialized losses: small bankruptcies are absorbed from the pot so that winning counterparties are never touched.
Mechanics: When a position is liquidated at a price better than its bankruptcy price, the residual margin flows into the fund; when a liquidation closes below the bankruptcy price, the fund pays the deficit. If the fund is exhausted, or its drawdown breaches trigger levels, the venue escalates to auto-deleveraging. Some venues run per-pair pools, others shared pools.
Failure modes: In cascading crashes the fund can drain faster than liquidations replenish it, at which point losses socialize via ADL anyway; fund adequacy is venue-specific and partly opaque, and per-pair pools can be exhausted while the venue's other pools sit full.
Misconceptions: The insurance fund does not insure the trader — it protects the venue and winning counterparties from bad debt — and a large advertised fund is not a guarantee against ADL in a systemic event.
Example: If a bankrupt position's $8,000 shortfall hits a $20,000 pair-level fund with a 30% drawdown trigger, the fund pays down to the trigger and auto-deleveraging covers the remaining $2,000 (Bybit's worked example).
Fund inflow = residual margin when a liquidation fills better than the bankruptcy price; outflow = (bankruptcy price - actual closing price) * size when fills are worse.

Mark-to-market reviewed

The practice of valuing positions and accounts at current market prices (fair value) rather than historical cost, recognizing gains and losses as prices change; in futures markets it is performed daily or more often and drives variation margin flows.
Intuition: Your account reflects what positions are worth now, not what you paid: profits and losses become real every day, not just when you sell.
Mechanics: At each settlement the clearinghouse reprices positions to the current settlement price and credits or debits the change; accounts whose balance falls below maintenance margin must be topped up. In accounting, fair-value frameworks (FAS 157 / ASC 820, IFRS 13) formalize exit-price valuation with a hierarchy of input reliability.
Failure modes: For illiquid or model-priced instruments the 'market' mark is really a model mark (mark-to-model), which can flatter risk metrics and collapse on revaluation; in crises, marking to distressed fire-sale prices forces write-downs and margin calls that feed the spiral — the core of the 2008 fair-value debate.
Misconceptions: Mark-to-market does not create losses — it reveals them on schedule rather than at sale — and marking an illiquid asset 'to market' can embed significant estimation discretion despite the name.
Example: A trader long a contract marked up 10 points at settlement sees the gain credited in cash that day; a 10-point mark-down the next day is debited, and if the balance then breaches maintenance margin, a margin call follows.
Daily P/L credited = (settlement_t - settlement_{t-1}) * multiplier * contracts; account equity_t = equity_{t-1} + daily P/L.
Aliases: MTM, fair value accounting, M2M

Concentration risk reviewed

The risk arising from an uneven distribution of exposures — to a single counterparty or name (name concentration) or to a sector, region, or product (sectoral concentration) — such that one adverse event can produce losses large enough to threaten the portfolio or institution.
Intuition: Concentration is the opposite of diversification: the more eggs in one basket, the more one dropped basket costs.
Mechanics: Monitored through concentration ratios (the share of the portfolio in the largest exposures) and indices such as the Herfindahl index; banks set internal limits and regulators attach higher capital charges to concentrated books. In trading, the analogues are per-position and per-sector exposure caps.
Failure modes: Concentration metrics treat categories as independent; in crises, apparently separate names correlate through shared factors or funding channels, so measured concentration understates true common exposure. Ratio-based limits also ignore tail severity within the concentrated bucket.
Misconceptions: Concentration risk is not eliminated by holding many small positions in the same theme — correlated positions are one bet — and high conviction does not repeal the arithmetic of undiversified downside.
Example: A portfolio of 10 loans where one loan is half the book has a single-name concentration ratio of 50% and a Herfindahl index of about 0.27, versus 0.10 for an equally sized book.
Single-name concentration ratio = exposure_i / total portfolio; portfolio level: Herfindahl index H = sum_i s_i^2, where s_i are exposure shares.

Counterparty risk reviewed

The risk that the other side of a contract — a bond issuer, derivative counterparty, or insurer — fails to meet its obligations, including default before final settlement of the transaction's cash flows. For derivatives it is termed counterparty credit risk and is managed with collateral, netting, and central clearing.
Intuition: A contract is only as good as the entity standing behind it: if your counterparty fails, your winning trade becomes an unsecured claim in bankruptcy.
Mechanics: Exposure is measured as the replacement cost (mark-to-market) of the contract plus potential future exposure; mitigants include margining, netting agreements, credit insurance, and central counterparties. Regulatory capital is computed under frameworks such as SA-CCR, the standardized approach for counterparty credit risk.
Failure modes: Exposure grows exactly when the counterparty weakens if the trade is correlated with their credit (wrong-way risk); collateral protects against price moves but not gaps, and central clearing concentrates rather than eliminates the risk into the CCP itself.
Misconceptions: Counterparty risk is not only about outright default on a loan: a mark-to-market gain on an OTC derivative is an unsecured exposure every day it remains unsettled, and collateral reduces but does not zero it because of margining lags and disputes.
Example: A bank long an interest-rate swap with a dealer sees the swap move $5M in the money; if the dealer defaults before settling, the bank holds a $5M claim in the bankruptcy estate rather than cash.
Exposure (conceptually) = max(V, 0) + potential future exposure, where V is the current mark-to-market value of the netting set.

Settlement risk reviewed

The risk that one party delivers its side of a transaction but does not receive the countervalue in return — most acutely principal risk in foreign exchange, where the two legs settle in different currencies and time zones. Also called Herstatt risk after the 1974 Bankhaus Herstatt failure.
Intuition: You pay out your currency in the morning and the counterparty fails before paying theirs in the afternoon: the whole principal, not just the P/L, is gone.
Mechanics: It arises because settlement of the two legs is not simultaneous; it is mitigated by payment-versus-payment (PvP) settlement through financial market infrastructures such as CLS, by netting, and by collateralizing mark-to-market exposure on physically settling FX forwards and swaps. BCBS guidance directs banks to manage principal risk, replacement cost risk, and related liquidity, operational, and legal risks.
Failure modes: PvP coverage is incomplete — many currency pairs and jurisdictions settle without it — and netting reduces but does not eliminate principal exposure; settlement failures cluster on stressed days when operational capacity is strained.
Misconceptions: Settlement risk is not merely an operational nuisance: the exposure is the full principal for hours at a time, dwarfing the trade's market risk, and it persists even with apparently solvent counterparties because failure can occur intra-day.
Example: A bank pays out EUR against USD to a counterparty that is declared insolvent between the EUR payment cut-off and the USD payment time, losing the entire EUR principal.
Principal exposure = full face value of the currency delivered, outstanding from the time the own-side payment becomes irrevocable until receipt of the countervalue is final.

Liquidity risk reviewed

The risk of being unable to fund increases in assets or meet obligations as they come due without incurring unacceptable losses (funding liquidity), or of being unable to exit positions at prevailing prices without moving the market (market/asset liquidity). BCBS principles treat its management as paramount because one institution's shortfall can have system-wide repercussions.
Intuition: Liquidity risk is the risk that you need cash or an exit right now and the market charges you ruinously — or does not trade at all.
Mechanics: Banks manage it through liquidity buffers, stress tests, contingency funding plans, and limits on maturity transformation; for traders it shows up as widening spreads, thinning depth, and slippage, typically spiking together with volatility and funding stress in self-reinforcing liquidity spirals.
Failure modes: Liquidity evaporates exactly when most needed: haircuts and margins rise, buyers vanish, and fire-sale prices make 'liquid' portfolios behave as illiquid. Models calibrated to normal-market depth systematically underestimate liquidation cost in stress.
Misconceptions: An asset that trades tight spreads in calm markets is not 'liquid' in the risk sense — liquidity is regime-dependent — and solvency does not protect against liquidity failure, since solvent-on-paper institutions can still collapse from a funding run.
Example: In 2007-08, banks that assumed contingent funding would always be available discovered that 'liquidity can evaporate and illiquidity can last for an extended period of time,' necessitating central bank support (BCBS).
No single formula; measured through liquidity coverage of obligations under stress (survival horizon) and market-side metrics such as bid-ask spread and market depth.

Market risk reviewed

The risk of losses in on- and off-balance-sheet positions arising from movements in market prices — interest rates, equities, foreign exchange, commodities, credit spreads, and volatilities. It is the risk class for which the Basel market-risk framework (FRTB) sets trading-book capital requirements.
Intuition: It is the risk that the market itself moves against you: the everyday exposure of any open position to prices, rates, and spreads.
Mechanics: Measured with sensitivities (delta, vega, basis-point value), VaR, and expected shortfall; capitalized under the Basel standardized or internal-models approaches, which in the revised framework use expected shortfall models, a defined trading-book/banking-book boundary, and separate charges for non-modellable risk factors.
Failure modes: Market-risk models assume continuity and measurable distributions; jumps, gaps, and correlation breakdowns violate both. Capital frameworks lag product innovation, and measures calibrated on quiet data underestimate stress-period co-movement.
Misconceptions: Market risk is not just 'the market going down' for a long-only book: short, hedged, and option books carry market risk in both directions and in second-order quantities (volatility, correlation, basis) that price level alone misses.
Example: A trading desk holding equity index futures loses in proportion to its delta on a 3% index drop; under the revised Basel framework its internal-models capital is set from expected shortfall at the trading-desk level.
Regulatory framing: capital is a function of expected shortfall at the 97.5% level over stressed horizons plus non-modellable risk factor charges; practically, P/L approximates sum_i (sensitivity_i * factor move_i).

Operational risk reviewed

The risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people, and systems or from external events — including fraud, execution errors, outages, and legal risk. It is the Basel definition used for bank capital purposes, and the risk is inherent in all products, activities, processes, and systems.
Intuition: It is the risk that something inside the machine — or an outside event hitting the machine — breaks: the rogue trade, the failed settlement, the system outage, the fat-finger order.
Mechanics: Managed through governance and control frameworks, risk and control self-assessments, loss-event data collection, and scenario analysis; capitalized under the Basel standardized approach for operational risk. For trading operations it includes order-entry errors, model implementation bugs, and venue or broker failures.
Failure modes: Operational losses are fat-tailed and event-driven, so historical loss data understates the worst cases; controls themselves fail under stress (the alert that never fires, the reconciliation nobody reads), and automation converts small errors into large, fast ones.
Misconceptions: Operational risk is not a vague residual category — it has a precise regulatory definition — and sophisticated technology raises rather than lowers its stakes, because failures then scale at machine speed.
Example: A misconfigured order router sending duplicated orders, or a clearing outage preventing end-of-day settlement, produces operational-risk losses unrelated to any market view.
No canonical concept-level formula; Basel capital uses a business-indicator-based standardized approach (business indicator component times internal loss multiplier).

Model risk reviewed

The potential for adverse consequences — financial loss, poor business decisions, reputational damage — from decisions based on model outputs that are incorrect or misused. Per Federal Reserve SR 11-7, model risk arises primarily when a model has fundamental errors or when it is used incorrectly or without regard for its limitations and assumptions.
Intuition: Every model is a simplified map of reality; model risk is what happens when you drive off the edge of the map while trusting it.
Mechanics: Managed through a model risk management framework: sound development and documentation, independent validation (conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring, outcomes analysis such as back-testing), 'effective challenge' by informed parties, model inventories, and senior-management governance. Risk grows with model complexity, input uncertainty, breadth of use, and potential impact.
Failure modes: Validation lags usage: models calibrated in benign regimes get trusted in stressed ones, and back-tests share the model's own assumptions. Widely used models create systemic crowding, with everyone de-risking on the same signal and amplifying the event the model failed to foresee.
Misconceptions: Passing validation does not make a model 'true': SR 11-7 stresses that all models are imperfect representations requiring conservative adjustments and informed use, and a precise-looking output is not an accurate one.
Example: A VaR model assuming normal returns understates tail losses in a crash; positions sized off its output suffer losses far beyond the reported number — the classic model-risk failure SR 11-7 warns about.
No single-quantity formula; SR 11-7 frames the sources as (1) fundamental model error and (2) incorrect or inappropriate use.

Basis risk reviewed

The risk that a hedge and the underlying exposure do not move in lockstep — that the basis (spot price of the hedged asset minus the futures or hedge price) changes unexpectedly — leaving residual profit or loss despite hedging.
Intuition: Your umbrella covers most of you but not exactly: basis risk is the part of the weather that still gets you wet.
Mechanics: Basis b = S - F varies with quality or grade differences, timing mismatches between hedge expiry and the exposure's date, and location or transportation differentials. Hedgers manage it by choosing contracts with the closest grade, location, and timing, or with tailored OTC derivatives, accepting that exchange-traded hedges rarely converge perfectly.
Failure modes: The basis can blow out precisely in crises, when hedges are most relied upon (cash-futures dislocations in stressed markets); cross-hedges over a different grade or underlying carry structural basis that can trend against the hedger for long periods.
Misconceptions: A hedge does not eliminate price risk — it converts it into (usually smaller) basis risk — and at expiry spot and futures converge only for the deliverable grade, not necessarily for the hedger's actual asset.
Example: A wheat producer hedges with exchange futures of a different grade and delivery point; if local cash prices fall 40 cents while futures fall only 30, the 10-cent basis widening is an unhedged loss.
Basis = spot price of hedged asset - futures price of hedge contract; basis risk is the variability of the basis over the hedge horizon.

Gap risk reviewed

The risk that a position's price jumps discontinuously from one trade to the next — typically between sessions, over weekends, or on sudden news — with no opportunity to transact at intermediate prices, so stops and risk limits execute far from their intended levels.
Intuition: Your stop at $48 doesn't help when the market closes at $50 and opens at $40: the loss is realized at the gap price, not the stop price.
Mechanics: Gaps form when news or events arrive while markets are closed (earnings, macro releases, geopolitics), concentrating repricing into the next opening print. Exposure is managed by cutting size into known events, hedging with options, or avoiding holding through closures; 24/7 crypto trading reduces session gaps but not jump risk.
Failure modes: All stop-based risk control degrades exactly in gaps: realized losses exceed planned 1R by large multiples, corrupting R-multiple statistics and breaching daily loss limits in one print. Leveraged positions can gap straight through the liquidation price into negative equity.
Misconceptions: Gap risk is not eliminated by stop-loss orders — they guarantee execution, not price — nor by diversification within one market that closes at the same time; and the claim that 'gaps always get filled' is folklore, not a rule.
Example: Buying 100 shares at $50 before an earnings miss that gaps the open to $40 realizes a $1,000 loss — twice the planned risk if the stop was at $45.
No canonical formula; measured as P/L impact = (fill price - intended exit price) * size, which can be a multiple of planned per-trade risk (R).
Aliases: jump risk, overnight gap risk

Tail risk reviewed

The risk of extreme outcomes in the tails of the return distribution — conventionally moves beyond about three standard deviations — which occur far more often in financial returns than a normal distribution implies. Because losses matter more than gains, practice focuses on the left tail.
Intuition: The bell curve says the crash should almost never happen; market history says otherwise. Tail risk is the danger hiding in the 'impossible' part of the distribution.
Mechanics: Fat tails arise from volatility clustering, jumps, leverage unwinds, and correlation spikes in crises. Tail risk is measured with VaR and CVaR and with stress scenarios, and mitigated through robust diversification across assets and strategies or asymmetric hedges (options, tail-risk funds) that pay off convexly in crashes.
Failure modes: Tail events are by definition rare, so estimates are data-starved and models extrapolate poorly; hedges carry decay costs (negative carry) and can fail exactly when counterparties or correlations break, and 'tail-hedged' portfolios sometimes discover their hedge was in the wrong tail.
Misconceptions: Fat tails do not make extreme events ignorable noise — they recur with striking regularity (1987, 1994, 1997-98, 2000-02, 2008-09) — and a three-sigma label understates them because sigma itself expands in crises.
Example: A normal-distribution model treats a -8% day as near-impossible; in practice such days arrive several times per decade, and left-tail episodes drive most long-run maximum drawdowns.
Heuristic boundary: returns beyond +/-3 sigma (about 0.3% of a normal distribution); measured via CVaR/expected shortfall in the tail region rather than by a single formula.
Aliases: fat tail risk, left-tail risk

Event risk reviewed

The risk that a discrete, often unforecastable event — a takeover, reorganization, credit downgrade, product recall, regulatory action, natural disaster, or overnight headline — abruptly reprices a security or portfolio, independent of the prevailing market trend.
Intuition: You can be right about the market and still get wiped out by the press release: event risk is the discrete shock that doesn't care about your chart.
Mechanics: Events reprice securities by jump rather than continuous path, so protection uses insurance-like instruments (options, credit default swaps), event-driven exposure cuts (flat before earnings), and stop orders — which only partially help because event moves often gap. In credit markets, event risk includes sudden downgrades that raise issuers' funding costs.
Failure modes: The events that matter are the ones not on the calendar — unscheduled shocks cannot be sidestepped with an earnings-date filter — and hedges carry carry costs and counterparty considerations of their own. Stop orders give limited protection because event moves gap through them.
Misconceptions: Event risk is not just volatility around scheduled news — its defining cases are unforeseen — and holding many names in one event-prone sector (e.g. biotech trial readouts) does not diversify the shared event factor.
Example: A merger-arbitrage position in a takeover target loses heavily when regulators unexpectedly block the deal; the spread gaps wider overnight with no chance to exit at yesterday's price.
No canonical formula; exposure is scenario-based: P/L impact = position sensitivity * event-induced price jump.
Aliases: headline risk, jump/event risk

Funding liquidity risk reviewed

The risk that a trader or institution cannot obtain or roll over the funding — cash, credit, or margin — needed to carry its positions, forcing sales or default even when the positions are economically sound. It is distinct from market liquidity (the ease of trading the asset), with which it interacts in liquidity spirals.
Intuition: It's not that your position is wrong — it's that the money carrying it gets pulled: when the funding disappears, the position dies with it.
Mechanics: Positions are financed through collateralized borrowing against margins and haircuts; when lenders raise haircuts or withdraw credit, the trader must post capital or sell. Brunnermeier and Pedersen model the feedback: tighter funding reduces market liquidity, which raises volatility and margins, which tightens funding further — the margin spiral and loss spiral.
Failure modes: Funding terms can change faster than positions can be unwound, and margin and haircut increases are procyclical — highest in crashes — so funding liquidity risk materializes simultaneously with market losses, the worst possible correlation (LTCM 1998; the August 2007 quant crisis).
Misconceptions: Solvency is not protection: a fund can be right on every position and still die from a funding run, and short positions still require margin capital — shorting does not free up funding.
Example: A levered basis trade financed at a 2% haircut is forced to sell into an illiquid market when the prime broker raises the haircut to 10%, crystallizing losses that mark-to-market alone never implied.
Constraint: sum over positions of margin_j(position_j) <= capital W; funding stress binds the constraint as margins and haircuts rise or credit lines are cut.
Aliases: funding risk, rollover risk (funding)

Wrong-way risk reviewed

The risk that exposure to a counterparty is adversely correlated with that counterparty's credit quality — exposure grows precisely as the counterparty's probability of default rises. It is divided into specific wrong-way risk (a direct link through the nature of the transactions, e.g. collateral issued by the counterparty itself) and general wrong-way risk (correlation through broad market factors).
Intuition: The worst kind of counterparty risk: the bigger your claim on them, the less likely they are to be able to pay it.
Mechanics: It arises when the trade's driver also drives the counterparty's credit — a producer selling its own commodity forward to a bank, or protection bought from a bank correlated with the reference entity. CVA and exposure models that assume independence between exposure and default probability systematically understate expected loss; Basel/CRR frameworks require identification, monitoring, and capital treatment of specific WWR.
Failure modes: WWR hides in structures that look collateralized or hedged: the collateral's value falls with the counterparty, so the protection evaporates in exactly the default state it was meant for. The dependence is hardest to measure in the tail where it bites, and general WWR emerges from macro correlations that no single-trade review catches.
Misconceptions: Netting and collateral do not cure wrong-way risk when the collateral or exposure driver is linked to the counterparty's credit — the classic error is counting self-referential collateral (a firm's own shares or bonds) at face value.
Example: A bank lends to an oil producer against oil-linked collateral, or buys protection on it from an oil-correlated bank: when oil collapses, exposure and default probability spike together — the 2014-16 energy downturn made this concrete.
Conceptual: expected loss = E[EE * PD * LGD], which exceeds E[EE] * E[PD] * LGD when exposure (EE) and default probability (PD) are positively dependent.
Aliases: WWR, adverse correlation risk

Value at risk reviewed

A quantile-based measure of portfolio loss: for a given confidence level and horizon, the loss level that will not be exceeded with that probability under stated assumptions — a one-day 95% VaR of $1M means a 5% chance of losing more than $1M in a day. Formalized at J.P. Morgan in the early 1990s and embedded in bank capital regulation by the 1996 Basel market-risk amendment.
Intuition: It compresses the whole loss distribution into one sentence: on 19 days out of 20, we expect to lose no more than this.
Mechanics: Estimated parametrically (variance-covariance), by historical simulation, or by Monte Carlo, over a fixed horizon (one day, ten days) and tail probability (1%, 5%). VaR systems are validated by backtesting: the long-run frequency of breaches — losses beyond VaR — should match the specified tail probability.
Failure modes: VaR says nothing about severity beyond the quantile — a 99% VaR is silent on whether the 1% day costs 1.1x or 50x VaR — and it is not subadditive, so a combined portfolio's VaR can exceed the sum of its parts. Normality assumptions, stale windows, and illiquid marks understate it in exactly the crises it is meant to capture.
Misconceptions: VaR is not the maximum you can lose: it marks the boundary of normal days, and institutions can and do lose multiples of it. A small VaR does not mean small risk when the tail beyond it is fat.
Example: A one-day 95% VaR of $1M means a loss of $1M or more is expected about one day in twenty; it is silent on whether that bad day loses $1.1M or $10M, which is what expected shortfall adds.
VaR_alpha(X) = -inf{ x : F_X(x) > alpha } = F_Y^{-1}(1 - alpha), where X is P/L with distribution F_X and Y = -X is the loss; i.e. the (1 - alpha) quantile of the loss distribution.
Aliases: VaR

Expected shortfall reviewed

The expected loss conditional on the loss exceeding a given VaR level — equivalently the average of VaR over all levels beyond the threshold. It is a coherent risk measure, more sensitive to tail shape than VaR, and it replaced VaR as the basis of Basel internal-models market-risk capital.
Intuition: Where VaR tells you where the bad region starts, expected shortfall tells you how bad the bad region is on average.
Mechanics: Compute the mean of the loss distribution beyond the alpha quantile; for continuous distributions this is E[L | L >= VaR_alpha]. Under the revised Basel market-risk framework, capital is based on ES at the 97.5% level calibrated to stressed periods, with VaR retained for backtesting.
Failure modes: ES is harder to backtest than VaR (no simple breach-frequency test) and more data-hungry in the tail; it still inherits the input distribution's blind spots — garbage scenarios in, precise-looking tail numbers out. Being a tail mean, it is also sensitive to single extreme observations.
Misconceptions: ES and CVaR/TVaR are often treated as identical, but the definitions diverge for distributions with atoms at the quantile; and ES does not fix VaR's dependence on assumed distributions — it deepens the measurement, it does not make the tail knowable.
Example: If the worst 5% of days average a $2M loss while the 95% VaR is $1.2M, the 5% expected shortfall is $2M; Basel internal-models capital now uses ES at the 97.5% confidence level.
ES_alpha = (1/(1 - alpha)) * integral_alpha^1 VaR_gamma(L) d_gamma; for continuous loss distributions, ES_alpha(L) = E[ L | L >= VaR_alpha(L) ].

Stress testing reviewed

A risk management technique that evaluates the impact of severe but plausible adverse scenarios — historical replays or hypothetical shocks to prices, rates, spreads, and liquidity — on a portfolio or institution, reaching beyond what statistically calibrated models capture. BCBS principles make it both a core bank risk-management tool and a supervisory instrument.
Intuition: Models tell you about ordinary Tuesdays; stress tests ask what happens on the once-a-decade Wednesday that bankrupts the unprepared.
Mechanics: Define objectives and governance, select scenarios (historical events such as 2008, or hypothetical factor shocks), apply them to positions through re-pricing or sensitivity models, and assess losses, liquidity, and capital adequacy; results feed limits, capital planning, and contingency funding plans. The principles require sufficiently severe stresses, granular data, fit-for-purpose models, and independent validation.
Failure modes: Stress tests are only as imaginative as their scenario designers: risks outside the chosen scenarios go unmeasured, and scenarios calibrated to the last crisis miss the next one. They also assume positions can be re-priced — in illiquid stress the very marks are hypothetical.
Misconceptions: Passing a stress test is not proof of resilience — it is one scenario's verdict, not a guarantee — and stress testing complements rather than replaces probabilistic measures like VaR, because tail probabilities are unreliable exactly where the test operates.
Example: Re-pricing a rates book under a 2008-style shock (rates -100bp, spreads +300bp, correlations toward one) to check the desk survives without breaching its capital or liquidity floor.
Scenario P/L = sum over positions of (re-priced value under shocked factors - current value); no single universal formula — severity and factor design define the test.

Scenario analysis reviewed

The evaluation of portfolio or firm outcomes under explicitly constructed states of the world — combinations of factor moves reflecting historical episodes, hypothetical narratives, or reverse-engineered loss targets — used to understand exposure to joint events rather than single-factor moves.
Intuition: It is structured what-if thinking: pick a coherent story (recession, rate shock, venue failure) and trace what it does to every position at once.
Mechanics: Build scenarios as joint moves in risk factors (prices, volatilities, correlations, liquidity), then re-value the book under each; reverse stress testing starts from a defined failure point, such as a capital breach, and works backward to the scenarios that produce it. Within stress-testing frameworks, scenario selection and severity are governance decisions tied to risk appetite.
Failure modes: Results are entirely conditional on scenario choice and internal consistency — factors that cannot co-move are often shocked together, and vice versa — and the correlation assumptions inside scenarios are judgmental and fail in real crises. It enumerates imagined risks and is silent on unimagined ones.
Misconceptions: Scenario analysis is not prediction and carries no probabilities by default — a scenario is a conditional exercise, not a forecast — and running more scenarios does not amount to covering the tail.
Example: Testing the book under a 'crypto venue failure' scenario — BTC -40%, stressed funding rates, stablecoin depeg, withdrawal halt simultaneously — to see which positions and counterparties break first.
Scenario impact = V(f_1', ..., f_k') - V(f_1, ..., f_k), where f_i are risk factors and primes denote their shocked values.

Sensitivity analysis reviewed

The measurement of how a portfolio's value or a model's output changes in response to a change in one input or risk factor at a time; the Greeks (delta, vega, rho), basis-point values, and one-at-a-time parameter perturbations are its standard forms.
Intuition: Rather than shocking the whole world, nudge one dial at a time and read the needle: how much does the book lose per basis point, per vol point, per day of decay?
Mechanics: Compute first-order (and higher-order) partial responses: bump a single input by a small amount and re-price, or use analytic derivatives. BCBS stress-testing principles require that models assessing the impacts of scenarios and sensitivities be fit for purpose; in practice, sensitivity limits (delta, DV01, and vega caps) operationalize the results.
Failure modes: One-at-a-time moves miss interaction effects — real crises move factors together nonlinearly — and linear sensitivities misstate exposure for convex positions such as options exactly when moves are large. Sensitivities are also local: they describe the neighborhood of current prices, not jumps.
Misconceptions: A book with zero delta is not risk-free — gamma, vega, and higher-order sensitivities remain — and sensitivity analysis is not a substitute for scenario analysis, since it assumes the other dials stay put.
Example: An options desk measures vega at +$50k per volatility point: a 5-point implied-volatility crush loses roughly $250k, a number no delta measure would reveal.
Sensitivity ~= [V(x + h) - V(x)] / h per unit of factor x; e.g. DV01 = change in value for a 1bp parallel rate move.

Sharpe ratio reviewed

The ratio of a fund's average differential (excess) return over a benchmark or risk-free rate to the standard deviation of that differential return — the reward-to-variability measure introduced by William Sharpe in 1966 and generalized in his 1994 exposition. It summarizes expected (ex ante) or realized (ex post) return per unit of total risk.
Intuition: It answers: for each unit of volatility endured, how much return above the risk-free alternative did the strategy deliver? Higher means more efficient conversion of risk into return.
Mechanics: Compute the differential return series (fund minus benchmark or risk-free rate), take its mean and standard deviation over the sample, and divide; annualize by scaling the mean by the period count and sigma by its square root. The ratio is scale-independent — leverage changes mean and sigma proportionally — which is why it suits zero-investment strategies.
Failure modes: It uses only the first two moments, so skewed or fat-tailed strategies (option selling, carry) show inflated Sharpes until the tail event arrives; smoothed or stale marks on illiquid assets understate sigma and overstate the ratio. It is also time-scale dependent and assumes the differential returns are comparable across the sample.
Misconceptions: A high historical Sharpe is not a forecast — ex post ratios are noisy estimates of ex ante performance — and the numerator must be the differential (excess) return: dividing the raw return mean by sigma loses the measure's meaning.
Example: An annual excess return of 6% with 15% volatility gives a Sharpe ratio of 0.40 — Sharpe's own illustration of a developed-country stock market index.
S = E[D] / sigma_D, where D_t = R_F,t - R_B,t is the differential return versus the benchmark or risk-free rate; annualized, S_ann = S_period * sqrt(P) for P periods per year.

Sortino ratio reviewed

A risk-adjusted performance measure that modifies the Sharpe ratio by penalizing only returns below a user-specified target (the minimum acceptable return): the excess of realized return over the target divided by the downside deviation (target semi-deviation).
Intuition: Upside volatility is profit, not danger; the Sortino ratio only charges the strategy for the harmful, below-target fluctuations.
Mechanics: Choose the target return T (e.g. 0% or the risk-free rate), compute the average realized excess over T and the downside deviation DR — the root of the probability-weighted squared below-target shortfalls — then divide. Developed by Frank Sortino and Brian Rom within post-modern portfolio theory.
Failure modes: Results hinge on the chosen target and on having enough below-target observations; short samples make DR statistically fragile. For near-symmetric distributions with T near the median it adds little over Sharpe, and discrete versus continuous conventions distort comparability.
Misconceptions: It is not Sharpe with a naive 'standard deviation of negative returns': the deviation is computed against a target across the whole distribution, and a higher Sortino than Sharpe does not mean the strategy is safer — only that its volatility skews to the upside.
Example: A strategy returning 28% annualized with a 7% downside deviation against a 5.25% target has a Sortino near 3.25 even though its total-volatility Sharpe is unimpressive — the classic asymmetric-payoff case.
S = (R - T) / DR, with DR = sqrt( integral_{-inf}^{T} (T - r)^2 f(r) dr ), where R is the average realized return, T the target (MAR), and f(r) the return distribution.

Calmar ratio reviewed

A drawdown-based performance measure: the average annual (compound) rate of return over a trailing 36-month window divided by the maximum drawdown over the same window, computed monthly. Created by Terry W. Young in 1991 for evaluating commodity trading advisors.
Intuition: It prices performance in units of worst pain: how much annual return do you get for each unit of the deepest hole the strategy digs in three years?
Mechanics: Compute the compound annualized return over the last 36 months and divide by the absolute maximum drawdown measured on that window; recalculate monthly so the ratio evolves gradually. A close relative, the MAR ratio, uses the full track record from inception instead of the rolling window.
Failure modes: The 36-month window makes it backward-window-dependent: a crisis rolling out of the window can improve the ratio without any change in the strategy, and young funds without a full window are unevaluable. Like all drawdown ratios it is driven by a single worst episode.
Misconceptions: Calmar and MAR ratios are not identical — Calmar uses a rolling 36-month window while MAR uses inception-to-date data — and a high Calmar can coexist with mediocre recent performance if the window's drawdown was shallow.
Example: A CTA with a 15% compound annual return and a 10% maximum drawdown over the trailing 36 months has a Calmar ratio of 1.5.
Calmar = CAGR(36 months) / |max drawdown(36 months)|.

Sterling ratio reviewed

A risk-adjusted return measure that divides the compound annual return by a function of average annual drawdown — originally, per Deane Sterling Jones (1981), the compound rate of return divided by the absolute value of (average annual drawdown minus 10%); modern variants use excess return over the average largest drawdown.
Intuition: Where Calmar charges only for the single worst drawdown, the Sterling ratio charges for the typical yearly drawdown: the routine pain, not just the record pain.
Mechanics: Compute each calendar year's worst peak-to-trough loss, average those drawdowns, and divide the compound annual return by that average adjusted by 10% (the original 1981 T-bill reference); the Sharpe-like modern form subtracts the risk-free rate in the numerator instead. Multiple definitions coexist in practice.
Failure modes: The definition is not standardized — the 10% adjustment, the averaging convention, and the drawdown definition vary — so quoted Sterling ratios are frequently non-comparable across sources. Averaging drawdowns also dilutes the impact of one catastrophic year.
Misconceptions: The 10% in the original denominator is not a universal constant — it was the 1981 T-bill yield used as the risk-free benchmark — and the Sterling ratio is not just Calmar under another name: they use different drawdown aggregates.
Example: A fund compounding 18% annually with a 12% average annual maximum drawdown has an original-form Sterling ratio of 18 / |12 - 10| = 9, versus 1.5 on the modern excess-return form — illustrating the convention problem.
Original: SR = CompoundROR / |AvgAnnualDD - 10%|; modern variant: SR = (Annual portfolio return - annual risk-free rate) / average largest drawdown.

Omega ratio reviewed

A performance measure defined by Keating and Shadwick (2002) as the probability-weighted ratio of gains to losses relative to a threshold return: the area of the return distribution above the threshold divided by the area below it. Because it uses the entire distribution, it captures all moments rather than only mean and variance.
Intuition: Split the whole return distribution at your 'good enough' line and weigh everything above against everything below — no moment is thrown away.
Mechanics: Partition the cumulative return distribution at threshold theta, integrate the tail masses above and below, and take their ratio. Omega of 1 at a given threshold means gains and losses balance; higher is better. Setting theta to zero recovers the Bernardo-Ledoit gain-loss ratio as a special case.
Failure modes: Empirical estimation needs enough data to characterize both tails, and the ratio is threshold-dependent — rankings can flip as theta moves. Its non-convex standard form complicates portfolio optimization, requiring transformation to linear-fractional programs.
Misconceptions: Capturing 'all moments' does not make Omega universally superior: with sparse data the tail integrals are the least reliably estimated part, and the threshold choice is as discretionary as the Sortino target it was meant to transcend.
Example: With threshold 0%, a strategy whose probability-weighted gains above zero total twice its probability-weighted losses has Omega = 2, summarizing skew and kurtosis that a Sharpe ratio would miss.
Omega(theta) = [ integral_theta^inf (1 - F(r)) dr ] / [ integral_{-inf}^theta F(r) dr ], where F is the cumulative distribution of returns and theta the threshold return.

Information ratio reviewed

The ratio of active return (portfolio return minus benchmark return) to active risk (the tracking error, i.e. the standard deviation of the active return). It measures the consistency of a manager's value added per unit of benchmark-relative risk and is the natural analogue of the Sharpe ratio for benchmarked mandates.
Intuition: It grades the active bet itself: how much did deviating from the index earn, per unit of deviation risk taken?
Mechanics: Compute the active return series against the agreed benchmark, take its mean and its standard deviation (tracking error), and divide; annualize both components. Top-quartile managers historically achieve annualized IRs around 0.5 (Grinold and Kahn). Using regression alpha and residual risk instead yields the appraisal ratio.
Failure modes: It is benchmark-dependent: the same manager posts different IRs against different indices, and gaming the benchmark (closet indexing with tilts) can flatter it. It uses arithmetic active returns and ignores leverage, so geometric-compounding effects can make a genuinely value-adding manager look negative.
Misconceptions: The IR numerator is simple active return, not Jensen's regression alpha — using regression alpha makes it the appraisal ratio, a different measure — and a high IR with tiny tracking error may reflect almost no real active management.
Example: A manager beating the benchmark by 2% per year with a 4% tracking error has an information ratio of 0.5, the level Grinold and Kahn associate with top-quartile performance.
IR = E[R_p - R_b] / sigma(R_p - R_b) = alpha / omega, where omega = sqrt(var(R_p - R_b)) is the tracking error.

Treynor ratio reviewed

A risk-adjusted performance measure relating a portfolio's excess return over the risk-free rate to its systematic risk (beta) rather than total volatility: the reward-to-volatility ratio introduced by Jack Treynor.
Intuition: It pays you only for market risk you can't diversify away: excess return per unit of beta, ignoring idiosyncratic swings a diversified investor needn't bear.
Mechanics: Compute the portfolio's average return minus the risk-free rate and divide by the portfolio's beta against the market. Meaningful comparisons assume the evaluated portfolios are sub-portfolios within a broader diversified whole; for undiversified holdings, total-risk measures such as Sharpe are more appropriate.
Failure modes: Beta is an unstable, window-dependent estimate, and the ratio says nothing about diversification quality — two portfolios with identical beta but different total risk rank the same even when one carries large unrewarded idiosyncratic risk. It is a ranking device, not a measure of value added.
Misconceptions: Treynor is not interchangeable with Sharpe: dividing by beta instead of sigma can rank the same funds in opposite orders, and a high Treynor for an undiversified portfolio is misleading because unsystematic risk is excluded from the denominator.
Example: A portfolio returning 20% with a 5% risk-free rate and beta 1.5 has a Treynor ratio of (0.20 - 0.05) / 1.5 = 0.10.
T = (r_i - r_f) / beta_i, where r_i is the portfolio return, r_f the risk-free rate, and beta_i the portfolio's market beta.

Jensen's alpha reviewed

The intercept of the regression of a portfolio's excess returns on the market's excess returns — the abnormal return over what the CAPM predicts for the portfolio's beta. Introduced by Michael Jensen in 1968 to evaluate mutual fund managers; it generalizes to multi-factor models as the return unexplained by the included factors.
Intuition: It is the residual: after accounting for the market exposure you took (beta), how much return was left over that the market can't explain?
Mechanics: Regress periodic excess portfolio returns on excess market returns; the estimated intercept is alpha, with its t-statistic indicating statistical significance. Adding factors (size, value, momentum) redefines alpha against the richer model — 3-factor, 4-factor alphas, and so on.
Failure modes: Alpha is model-dependent: it shrinks or vanishes as explanatory factors are added, so 'alpha' against CAPM may be beta against a better model. Estimated betas and alphas are noisy and non-stationary, and in small samples are statistically indistinguishable from luck; survivorship and backfill bias inflate reported fund alphas.
Misconceptions: A positive historical alpha is not proof of skill — significance requires many observations, and the efficient-markets critique holds that persistent alpha after costs is rare — nor is alpha the same as raw outperformance, which can come entirely from beta.
Example: A fund returns 12% when the risk-free rate is 3%, the market 10%, and the fund's beta is 1.2: alpha = 12% - [3% + 1.2 * (10% - 3%)] = +0.6%.
alpha_J = R_i - [ R_f + beta_i * (R_M - R_f) ] = (R_i - R_f) - beta_i * (R_M - R_f), with R_i the portfolio return, R_M the market return, R_f the risk-free rate, and beta_i the portfolio beta.

Tracking error reviewed

The standard deviation of a portfolio's active return relative to its benchmark — also called active risk — measuring how closely the portfolio follows its index. Measured historically it is ex post (realized) tracking error; model-predicted it is ex ante tracking error.
Intuition: It is the wobble between you and the index: an index fund hugs the benchmark (tracking error near zero) while an aggressive active manager wanders far from it.
Mechanics: Compute the active return series (portfolio minus benchmark) and take its standard deviation; in factor-model terms it is the non-systematic (residual) risk, and in weight space it equals sqrt((w_p - w_b)' Sigma (w_p - w_b)). Dividing mean active return by tracking error gives the information ratio.
Failure modes: Ex ante estimates from factor models can diverge from realized values by large factors when the model misses risks, and TE measured over benign windows understates active risk in regime shifts. A low-TE 'active' fund may simply be a closet indexer charging active fees.
Misconceptions: Tracking error is not a mistake or failure — for index funds it should be minimal, but for active mandates a larger TE is the deliberate price of seeking alpha — and it excludes market-direction risk, measuring only deviation from the benchmark.
Example: An active equity fund whose monthly returns deviate from its benchmark with a standard deviation of 0.67% has a tracking error of about 0.67% * sqrt(12), roughly 2.3% annualized.
TE = sqrt( var(r_p - r_b) ) = sqrt( (w_p - w_b)' Sigma (w_p - w_b) ), where r_p and r_b are portfolio and benchmark returns and (w_p - w_b) the vector of active weights.
Aliases: active risk, TE

Hit rate reviewed

The proportion of trades, forecasts, or periods that are 'hits' — profitable trades, correct directional calls, or outperforming intervals, depending on context. In trading-performance statistics it is synonymous with the win rate.
Intuition: The batting average of a strategy or forecaster: how often the call or the trade actually worked.
Mechanics: Count hits over total trials in the sample. For trading systems a hit is a trade with PnL above zero after costs (equivalent to win rate); for forecasting it is a correct sign prediction. It gains meaning only alongside payoff asymmetry — the average win versus the average loss.
Failure modes: A 55% hit rate is meaningless without the payoff ratio — it can be highly profitable or ruinous depending on the size of wins versus losses. Small samples make the rate statistically meaningless, and transaction costs flip marginal hit rates from green to red.
Misconceptions: Hit rate is not a skill certificate: market makers and trend followers profit with hit rates on opposite sides of 50% because their payoff asymmetries differ; only hit rate combined with payoff structure determines expectancy.
Example: A forecaster right on direction 58 of 100 days has a 58% hit rate; a trading system profitable on 42 of 100 trades has a 42% hit rate — viable if winners average twice the losers.
Hit rate = (number of hits) / (number of trials), e.g. winning trades / total closed trades.

Equity curve reviewed

The time series of a trading account's or strategy's value — cumulative realized profit/loss plus open equity, net of deposits and withdrawals — plotted over time. It is the primary visual record of performance, from which drawdown, time under water, and related statistics are computed.
Intuition: It is the strategy's cardiogram: trend, smoothness, and depth of dips say more about robustness than any single return number.
Mechanics: Mark the account to market each period and plot cumulative equity; read off slope (return), path volatility, drawdowns (peak-to-trough declines), and recovery times. Some practitioners overlay a moving average on the curve and reduce size when equity falls below it ('equity curve trading').
Failure modes: A smooth equity curve can be manufactured by illiquid marks or by hiding open losses (marking only closed trades), and backtested curves embed overfitting — the live realization is one path from a distribution the backtest does not show. Curve-based size rules can whipsaw at regime boundaries.
Misconceptions: The equity curve is not just a price chart of profits — conventions differ on open equity and cash flows — and two curves with identical endpoints can represent radically different risk through different drawdowns and time under water.
Example: An account starting at $100k that reaches $130k, falls to $110k, and recovers to $140k shows a 15.4% mid-path drawdown on its equity curve — invisible if you only compare start and end values.
E_t = E_0 + sum_{i=1..t} PnL_i (+ deposits - withdrawals); drawdown at t = (max_{s<=t} E_s - E_t) / max_{s<=t} E_s.

Underwater curve reviewed

The time series of percentage (or absolute) drawdown from the running equity peak, plotted as a persistently non-positive curve that touches zero only at new equity highs. It is the drawdown view of the equity curve, showing the depth and duration of every loss episode at a glance.
Intuition: Flip the equity curve's perspective: instead of how high you've climbed, the underwater curve shows how far below the surface you've spent your time.
Mechanics: At each period compute (running peak - current equity) / running peak and plot it; the depth (maximum drawdown), width (time under water / drawdown duration), and area of the resulting episodes summarize the strategy's pain profile better than the equity curve alone.
Failure modes: It inherits every flaw of drawdown statistics: sample dependence, censoring of unrecovered episodes, and flattering by smoothed marks; and it hides the return side — two strategies with identical underwater curves can have very different compound returns.
Misconceptions: A brief, shallow underwater curve does not prove a safe strategy — it may simply never have met a regime shift — and a curve not back at zero by the sample end means the worst episode is still running, not that it ended.
Example: A fund whose equity peaked 14 months ago and sits 8% below that peak shows an underwater curve pinned at -8% for the whole episode, making the recovery burden (an 8.7% gain is needed) explicit.
UW_t = ( E_t - max_{s<=t} E_s ) / max_{s<=t} E_s <= 0, where E_t is equity at time t.
