    Head and shoulders #

    A classical technical-analysis reversal pattern consisting of three successive peaks in which the middle peak (the head) is the highest and the two outside peaks (the shoulders) are lower and roughly equal, with the intervening reaction lows connected by a neckline. The pattern is treated as complete — and the prior uptrend tentatively reversed — only when price breaks below the neckline. It is an analytical framework for reading supply/demand shifts, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Buyers make one final push to a new high (the head), but the next rally (the right shoulder) can no longer reach it — a sign that demand is exhausting. When price then breaks the neckline, sellers have taken control.
    Mechanics: Identify an established uptrend, then three peaks with the middle one highest; draw the neckline through the two reaction lows (it may slope up, down, or be horizontal). Confirmation is a decisive close below the neckline, ideally on expanding volume; a return move to the neckline (support turned resistance) is common. The conventional price target projects the head-to-neckline distance downward from the break point.
    Failure modes: The pattern fails when the neckline never breaks (the right shoulder just becomes a higher low in a continuing uptrend) or when the break is a false breakout followed by a rally back above the right-shoulder high. Subjective peak selection and sloppy necklines make many 'patterns' artifacts of the beholder.
    Misconceptions: That the three peaks alone signal a top — without a neckline break there is no confirmed reversal, and perfectly symmetrical shoulders are not required. It is also wrongly treated as a guaranteed sell signal rather than a probabilistic hypothesis.
    Example: A stock rallies to $20.50 (left shoulder), pulls back to $17.50, rallies to a new high and falls back to $17.50 (head), then rallies only to $20 (right shoulder). A close below the $17.50 neckline completes the pattern and projects a target near $14.50.
    Price target = N - (H - N), where H = price at the top of the head and N = neckline price at the breakout point.
    Aliases: Head and shoulders top, H&S top

    Inverse head and shoulders #

    The bullish mirror of the head-and-shoulders top: three successive troughs after a downtrend in which the middle trough (the head) is the deepest and the two outside troughs (the shoulders) are shallower, with the intervening reaction highs forming a neckline. The pattern is treated as complete only when price breaks above the neckline, ideally with a clear volume expansion, marking a tentative bullish reversal. Like all chart patterns it is a heuristic framework, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Sellers drive price to a final capitulation low (the head), but the next decline (the right shoulder) cannot reach it — supply is drying up. A break above neckline resistance shows buyers have seized control.
    Mechanics: Identify an established downtrend, then three troughs with the middle one deepest; draw the neckline through the two reaction highs. Confirmation requires a convincing close above the neckline; volume plays a larger role than in the top version — expanding volume on the advance and breakout is considered close to mandatory. The price target projects the head-to-neckline distance upward from the break.
    Failure modes: Fails when price stalls at the neckline and the downtrend resumes, or when a low-volume breakout quickly fails and price falls back below the right-shoulder low. Because bottoms form slowly, the pattern is frequently 'called' long before it is complete.
    Misconceptions: That three troughs alone confirm a bottom — the pattern is incomplete until neckline resistance breaks with volume. Perfect shoulder symmetry is often demanded but is explicitly not required.
    Example: A stock declines to $50 (left shoulder), capitulates to $48 (head), rebounds to $61, then dips only to $57 (right shoulder). A high-volume close above the $61 neckline completes the pattern with a measured target near $74.
    Price target = N + (N - H), where H = price at the bottom of the head and N = neckline price at the breakout point.
    Aliases: Head and shoulders bottom, Inverse H&S, Reverse head and shoulders

    Double top #

    A classical bearish reversal pattern in which price in an established uptrend forms two peaks at roughly the same level separated by a moderate trough, and the pattern completes only when price breaks below the intervening trough (support). It is interpreted as two failed attempts to overcome the same supply zone. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis about trend change, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: The market tests the same high twice and fails both times, showing that sellers consistently overwhelm buyers at that level; breaking the low between the peaks confirms the sellers' victory.
    Mechanics: Require a prior uptrend of months, two peaks within roughly 3% of each other separated by at least about a month, and an intervening decline of roughly 10–20%. Confirmation is a decisive close below the trough low, preferably on expanding volume; broken support often turns into resistance on a retest. The conventional target subtracts the peak-to-trough height from the support break.
    Failure modes: The most common failure is anticipating the pattern: until the trough support breaks, the two peaks are just resistance in an ongoing uptrend, and price frequently breaks to new highs instead. Peaks too close together (days, not weeks) usually represent ordinary resistance rather than a durable reversal.
    Misconceptions: That two equal highs by themselves mark a top — the reversal is unconfirmed until support breaks. Also, a double top on a bar chart is unrelated to the bullish 'double top breakout' on point-and-figure charts.
    Example: A stock rallies to $36.80, declines ~15% to a trough near $30.50, rallies back to $36.80, then falls and closes below $30.50 on rising volume — completing the double top with a target near $24.50.
    Price target = S - (P - T), where P = peak price, T = intervening trough price, and S = support (trough) level at the break.
    Aliases: Double top reversal, M top

    Double bottom #

    A classical bullish reversal pattern in which price in an established downtrend forms two troughs at roughly the same level separated by a moderate rebound, and the pattern completes only when price breaks above the intervening reaction high (resistance). It is read as two successful defenses of the same demand zone. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Sellers push price down to the same low twice and cannot force it lower; when the subsequent rally clears the peak between the two lows, buyers have taken control.
    Mechanics: Require a prior downtrend of months, two troughs within roughly 3% of each other separated by at least about four weeks, and an intervening advance of roughly 10–20%. Confirmation is a decisive close above the intervening reaction high with expanding volume; broken resistance often becomes support on a retest. The conventional target adds the trough-to-peak height to the resistance break.
    Failure modes: Fails when resistance at the intervening peak holds and the downtrend resumes to new lows, or when the breakout occurs on weak volume and quickly collapses back into the range. Bottoms form slowly, so premature entries before confirmation are the dominant error.
    Misconceptions: That two equal lows alone confirm a bottom — the pattern is void until resistance breaks. Volume matters more than in a double top: a breakout without volume expansion is suspect.
    Example: A stock falls to $30, rebounds to $37.50, falls back to $30 two months later, then gaps above $37.50 on heavy volume — completing the double bottom with a target near $45.
    Price target = R + (R - T), where T = trough price and R = resistance (intervening reaction high) at the breakout.
    Aliases: Double bottom reversal, W bottom

    Triple top #

    A classical bearish reversal pattern in which price in an uptrend tests the same resistance level three times, forming three roughly equal, well-spaced highs, and completes only when price breaks below the pattern's support (the lowest of the intervening lows). It extends the double-top logic: three rejections of the same supply zone. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Three failed attempts to push through the same ceiling exhaust the buyers; once the floor between those attempts gives way, there are no buyers left to hold the level.
    Mechanics: Require a prior uptrend, three comparable highs (not exactly equal but reasonably close) marking clear turning points, typically forming over three to six months, with volume usually declining through the pattern. Confirmation is a break below the lowest intervening low, reinforced by volume expansion; the support break often turns into resistance on a retest. The target subtracts the pattern height from the support break.
    Failure modes: Until support breaks, the pattern is indistinguishable from a neutral rectangle or even a bullish ascending triangle, and an upside breakout through the triple resistance produces a powerful continuation instead. Rare, textbook-perfect triple tops tempt over-fitting to imperfect real charts.
    Misconceptions: That three equal highs are by themselves bearish — three equal highs also appear in rectangles and ascending triangles, so the pattern should be treated as neutral until the downside break occurs.
    Example: A stock in an uptrend fails three times near $23 over four months, then breaks below the $19.80 support from the May low on expanding volume, projecting a decline toward $16.60.
    Price target = S - (H - S), where H = average high of the three peaks and S = the support level (lowest intervening low) at the break.
    Aliases: Triple top reversal

    Triple bottom #

    A classical bullish reversal pattern in which price in a downtrend tests the same support level three times, forming three roughly equal, well-spaced lows, and completes only when price breaks above the pattern's resistance (the highest of the intervening highs). It is the mirror of the triple top: three successful defenses of the same demand zone. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Three times sellers try to force price through the same floor and fail; when the ceiling between those floors finally breaks, the accumulated buying pressure is released.
    Mechanics: Require a prior downtrend and three comparable lows marking significant turning points, typically over three to six months, with overall volume declining as the pattern develops. Confirmation is a break above the highest intervening high with volume expansion; broken resistance often becomes support on a retest. The target adds the pattern height to the resistance break, though for very long formations the measured target is considered less reliable.
    Failure modes: Until resistance breaks, three equal lows also fit a neutral rectangle or a bearish descending triangle, and a downside break of support continues the downtrend instead. Long formations can make the measured price target ineffective.
    Misconceptions: That holding support three times proves accumulation — the ability to hold support is encouraging, but the pattern is neutral until the resistance breakout confirms it.
    Example: A stock forms three roughly equal lows near $11 over 13 months, then breaks resistance at $22.50 and closes above it for five consecutive weeks, confirming a major base.
    Price target = R + (R - L), where L = average low of the three troughs and R = the resistance level (highest intervening high) at the breakout.
    Aliases: Triple bottom reversal

    Ascending triangle #

    A classical consolidation pattern bounded above by a horizontal resistance line drawn through two or more comparable highs and below by a rising trend line drawn through successively higher lows. It carries a bullish bias — interpreted as accumulation against a fixed supply level — and usually resolves as a continuation of the prior uptrend, though it occasionally forms as a bottom reversal. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Sellers defend a fixed ceiling while buyers keep stepping in at higher and higher prices; the rising floor shows buying pressure building until the ceiling finally gives way.
    Mechanics: Require at least two reaction highs at similar levels for the horizontal line and at least two successively higher reaction lows for the ascending line (a new low equal to or below the prior low invalidates the pattern). Volume typically contracts during formation and should expand on the upside breakout; broken resistance often turns into support on a return move. The target projects the pattern's widest height up from the breakout.
    Failure modes: Breakouts can fail or reverse ('busted' patterns), especially when volume does not expand or when the pattern forms very late in a mature uptrend. A downside break of the rising trend line converts the 'bullish' pattern into a failure and often a fast decline.
    Misconceptions: That the bullish bias justifies buying inside the pattern before the breakout — the bias is only a tendency, and the pattern is not actionable until resistance actually breaks.
    Example: A stock bumps against $24 three times over five months while its lows rise from $14 to $20; it then breaks $24 on heavy volume, projecting a measured move toward $34.
    Price target = B + (H - L), where B = breakout (horizontal resistance) price, and H - L = the widest vertical distance of the triangle.
    Aliases: Right-angle triangle (ascending), Rising triangle

    Descending triangle #

    A classical consolidation pattern bounded below by a horizontal support line drawn through two or more comparable lows and above by a descending trend line drawn through successively lower highs. It carries a bearish bias — interpreted as distribution against a fixed demand level — and usually resolves as a continuation of the prior downtrend, though it occasionally forms as a top reversal. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Buyers defend a fixed floor while sellers accept lower and lower prices on each rally; the descending ceiling shows supply pressing down until the floor finally collapses.
    Mechanics: Require at least two comparable reaction lows for the horizontal line and at least two successively lower reaction highs for the descending line (a new high equal to or above the prior high invalidates the pattern). Volume usually contracts during formation and ideally expands on the downside break; broken support often turns into resistance on a retest. The target subtracts the pattern's widest height from the support break.
    Failure modes: Bulkowski's statistics show breakouts are actually upward about 53% of the time, so the textbook bearish bias is weak in practice; throwbacks and pullbacks are common and can trap breakout traders. A break of the upper trend line busts the pattern upward.
    Misconceptions: That a descending triangle guarantees a downward resolution — historically the breakout direction is close to a coin flip, and confirmation (a close outside a trendline) matters more than the assumed bias.
    Example: A stock finds support at $45 three times while its rally highs step down from $54; it then gaps below $45 on expanded volume, projecting a measured decline toward $36.
    Price target = B - (H - L), where B = breakout (horizontal support) price, and H - L = the widest vertical distance of the triangle.
    Aliases: Right-angle triangle (descending), Falling triangle

    Symmetrical triangle #

    A classical consolidation pattern in which at least two lower highs and two higher lows produce converging trend lines, forming a contracting trading range. It is directionally neutral until the breakout: most often it resolves as a continuation of the prevailing trend, but a meaningful minority mark reversals. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Buyers and sellers reach into each other with shrinking swings, like a coiling spring; the stored-up indecision is released in whichever direction price finally escapes the converging lines.
    Mechanics: Require a minimum of four turning points (two lower highs, two higher lows), ideally six; volume should diminish as the range contracts. The most reliable breakouts occur roughly halfway to three-quarters of the distance from base to apex, on a closing basis, ideally with volume expansion and possibly a 3% price or 3-day time filter; the apex later acts as support/resistance. Targets are estimated by projecting the widest part of the triangle from the breakout or by drawing a parallel trend line.
    Failure modes: Breakouts very close to the apex tend to be weak and meaningless, and premature or false breakouts are common — Edwards and Magee estimated roughly 25% of symmetrical triangles mark reversals rather than continuations. Guessing the breakout direction in advance is the classic mistake.
    Misconceptions: That the triangle predicts the breakout direction — by construction it is neutral, and only the actual break (preferably with volume) assigns direction. Also, a contraction shorter than about three weeks is conventionally classified as a pennant, not a triangle.
    Example: After a year-long downtrend, a stock coils for five months between falling highs and rising lows, then breaks down through the lower line with accelerating volume; the 10.5-point height projects a decline toward $19 from the $29.50 break.
    Price target = B +/- (H - L), where B = breakout price and H - L = the widest vertical distance of the triangle (sign follows breakout direction).
    Aliases: Coil, Contracting triangle

    Rising wedge #

    A technical-analysis pattern in which price makes higher highs and higher lows but both bounding trend lines slope upward and converge, compressing the advance. When it appears in an uptrend it is read as a bearish reversal warning (momentum fading into the apex); in a downtrend it is read as a bearish continuation (a corrective rally). It is an analytical hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Price is still rising, but each push up is smaller than the last — buyers are paying more for less progress, and the advance typically resolves downward through the lower line.
    Mechanics: Draw two upward-sloping, converging trend lines connecting at least two (preferably three) highs and lows; volume usually declines as the wedge forms. The signal is a break of the lower trend line; traders may enter short on the break with a stop above the wedge. Unlike the symmetrical triangle, both lines slope in the same direction, which is what gives the wedge its directional bias.
    Failure modes: In strong bull trends a rising wedge can keep grinding higher far longer than expected, producing repeated premature short entries; the 'breakdown' can also be a brief shakeout followed by an upside resolution. Convergence is subjective — different trend-line anchors produce different 'wedges' on the same chart.
    Misconceptions: That a rising wedge must always break down — it is a tendency, and the same shape in a downtrend is a continuation pattern whose 'signal' only activates on the lower-line break. It is also often confused with an ascending triangle, whose top line is horizontal rather than rising.
    Example: A stock climbs from $40 to $55 over two months with each successive high only marginally above the last and volume shrinking; a close below the rising lower trend line at $52 triggers the bearish reading.
    Aliases: Ascending wedge

    Falling wedge #

    A technical-analysis pattern in which price makes lower highs and lower lows but both bounding trend lines slope downward and converge, compressing the decline. It is conventionally read as bullish: the converging lines show the sell-off losing momentum, with resolution expected through a break above the upper trend line — as a reversal after a downtrend or as a continuation pause within an uptrend. It is an analytical hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Sellers keep pushing price down, but each new low is harder to achieve — selling pressure is exhausting, and the spring typically releases upward through the upper line.
    Mechanics: Draw two downward-sloping, converging trend lines through at least two highs and two lows; volume typically contracts during formation. The trigger is a break above the upper trend line, ideally with volume expansion; the wedge's convergence allows a relatively close stop-loss below the pattern, which is why traders value its reward-to-risk geometry.
    Failure modes: In persistent bear markets a falling wedge can resolve downward (a downside break of the lower line continues the decline), and in rotating, trendless markets the pattern loses efficacy. As with all trend-line patterns, anchor selection is subjective.
    Misconceptions: That the falling wedge's bullish label means buy immediately — the signal is only the upside break of the upper line; buying inside the falling range is catching a falling knife. It is also frequently confused with a descending triangle, whose bottom line is horizontal.
    Example: A stock slides from $60 to $48 with each new low only slightly under the prior one and volume drying up; a close above the falling upper trend line at $51 marks the bullish resolution.
    Aliases: Descending wedge

    Bull flag #

    A short-term continuation pattern consisting of a sharp near-vertical advance (the flagpole) followed by a brief, tight counter-trend consolidation in a small parallel channel sloping downward (the flag), typically lasting five to twenty bars. It is read as a pause that refreshes the uptrend, with continuation expected on a breakout above the flag's upper line. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: After a sudden surge, early buyers take profits in an orderly drift lower while new buyers absorb the supply; when the profit-taking is done, the trend resumes, often with similar force.
    Mechanics: Identify the pole (sharp advance on rising volume) and the flag (a narrow parallel-channel pullback that should not retrace beyond roughly the midpoint of the pole, on declining volume). Entry is conventionally after a close above the flag's upper parallel line, with a stop below the flag's lower line. A common target adds the flagpole's height to the breakout point.
    Failure modes: False breakouts are common when the flag drifts too deep (retracing more than half the pole) or too long, mutating into a full reversal; low-volume breakouts frequently fail. In choppy, trendless markets 'flags' appear constantly and mean little.
    Misconceptions: That the flag's downward drift signals weakness — a controlled, low-volume pullback is precisely what defines the pattern; heavy-volume declines invalidate it. Also, flags have parallel channel lines; converging lines make it a pennant or wedge, not a flag.
    Example: A stock jumps from $40 to $65 in days, then drifts down in a tight channel between $55 and $51 for two weeks on shrinking volume; a close above $55 projects a target near $80.
    Price target = B + (T - F), where B = breakout price above the flag's upper line, T = top of the flagpole, and F = base of the flagpole.
    Aliases: Bullish flag, Flag (bullish)

    Bear flag #

    A short-term continuation pattern consisting of a sharp near-vertical decline (the flagpole) followed by a brief, tight counter-trend consolidation in a small parallel channel sloping upward (the flag), typically lasting five to twenty bars. It is read as a pause within a downtrend, with continuation expected on a breakdown below the flag's lower line. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: After a sudden plunge, bargain hunters produce a weak, grinding bounce that never recovers much ground; when that feeble buying is exhausted, the decline resumes.
    Mechanics: Identify the pole (sharp decline) and the flag (a narrow upward-sloping parallel channel). Volume behavior differs from the bull flag: because down-moves are fear-driven, volume often stays elevated through the consolidation rather than contracting, and the downside breakout may be less volume-dependent. Entry is conventionally after a close below the flag's lower parallel line, with a stop above the flag; a common target subtracts the pole's height from the breakdown point.
    Failure modes: Bear flags fail when the 'flag' is actually accumulation — a breakout above the flag's upper line busts the pattern and can fuel a sharp short squeeze. In range-bound markets, flag-like pauses have no trend to continue.
    Misconceptions: That bear-flag consolidations should show falling volume like bull flags — bearish consolidations often hold level volume, so volume contraction is not required. Also, converging (not parallel) lines make the formation a pennant or rising wedge.
    Example: A stock drops from $65 to $40 on heavy volume, then grinds up in a tight channel from $44 to $48 over two weeks; a close below $44 projects a measured target near $19.
    Price target = B - (F - T), where B = breakdown price below the flag's lower line, F = top of the flagpole (start of decline), and T = bottom of the flagpole.
    Aliases: Bearish flag, Flag (bearish)

    Bull pennant #

    A short-term continuation pattern consisting of a sharp advance (the flagpole) followed by a brief consolidation bounded by converging trend lines forming a small symmetrical triangle (the pennant), typically lasting one to three weeks. It is read as a pause of market indecision within an uptrend, with continuation expected on a breakout above the pennant's upper line on increased volume. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: After a sudden rally the market catches its breath in ever-tighter swings; the triangle-shaped breather ends when buyers overwhelm sellers again and price shoots out of the apex.
    Mechanics: Require a strong pole on large volume, then a converging triangular consolidation on weakening volume, and finally a breakout with a large volume increase. Traders commonly enter just above the pennant's upper trend line, place the stop at the pennant's lowest point, and set a target by adding the flagpole's height to the breakout price. Confirmation with indicators such as RSI is standard practice.
    Failure modes: Entering before the breakout (anticipating) is the classic failure — consolidation can resolve downward through the lower line. Low participation (weak volume) during the pennant, or external news shocks, frequently break the expected continuation.
    Misconceptions: That pennants and flags are the same pattern — pennants have converging lines (a small symmetrical triangle), flags have parallel lines; duration also differs (pennants typically one to three weeks, longer contractions are triangles).
    Example: A stock rallies from $5 to $10, consolidates in a tightening triangle around $8.50–$9.00, then breaks out at $9.00 on a volume surge; the flagpole method projects a target near $14.
    Price target = B + P, where B = breakout price above the pennant's upper trend line and P = flagpole height (pole top minus pole base).
    Aliases: Bullish pennant
    Related:

    Bear pennant #

    A short-term continuation pattern consisting of a sharp decline (the flagpole) followed by a brief consolidation bounded by converging trend lines forming a small symmetrical triangle (the pennant), typically lasting one to three weeks. It is read as a pause within a downtrend, with continuation expected on a breakdown below the pennant's lower line on increased volume. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: After a sudden drop, buyers and sellers reach a shrinking stand-off in a tiny triangle; when the stand-off resolves, the stored selling pressure usually drives the next leg down.
    Mechanics: Require a strong downward pole on heavy volume, a converging triangular consolidation on weakening volume, then a downside breakout with volume expansion. Traders enter just below the pennant's lower trend line, place the stop at the pennant's highest point (a close back inside invalidates the pattern), and project the flagpole's height downward from the break for a target.
    Failure modes: Pennants fail when consolidation breaks upward instead — an upside resolution turns the pattern into a short squeeze; premature entries during the triangle, low-volume formations, and market-wide news events all override the expected continuation.
    Misconceptions: That the small triangle after a drop must resolve downward — pennants are only 'bear' patterns in expectation, and some act as reversals; the break itself, not the label, assigns direction.
    Example: A stock plunges from $20 to $12 in a week, then coils between $12.50 and $14 for ten days; a high-volume break below $12.50 projects a target near $4.50.
    Price target = B - P, where B = breakdown price below the pennant's lower trend line and P = flagpole height (pole top minus pole bottom).

    Rectangle pattern #

    A classical consolidation pattern in which price oscillates between a horizontal resistance line (at least two comparable highs) and a horizontal support line (at least two comparable lows), forming a trading range. It is directionally neutral: usually a continuation pause within the prevailing trend, but it can also mark significant tops and bottoms, and only the breakout assigns direction. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Bulls buy every dip to the same floor and bears sell every rally to the same ceiling; the range persists until one side runs out of ammunition and price escapes the box.
    Mechanics: Draw parallel horizontal lines through at least two comparable highs and two comparable lows; ranges shorter than about three weeks are conventionally flags instead, while three-plus months is typical. Volume behavior is irregular (unlike triangles), but which direction attracts the most volume can hint at the resolution. Confirmation is a closing break of a boundary (optionally with a 3% price or 3-day filter and volume expansion); the broken boundary often flips polarity on a retest, and the target projects the rectangle's height from the breakout.
    Failure modes: False breakouts out of well-established ranges are notorious — the longer and more obvious the range, the more traders position for the break and the more rewarding a fake-out. Range-trading (buying support, selling resistance) also fails catastrophically on the final breakout that never returns.
    Misconceptions: That a rectangle is bullish or bearish based on the prior trend — it is explicitly neutral until the break. Also that support/resistance lines must be touched at exactly equal prices; reasonably close touches suffice.
    Example: After rallying to the low $40s, a stock churns between $30 and $40 for five months; a high-volume close above $40 projects a measured move of the $10 height toward $50, and broken $40 resistance later acts as support.
    Price target = B +/- (R - S), where B = breakout price, R = resistance line, S = support line (sign follows breakout direction).
    Aliases: Trading range, Consolidation zone, Congestion area, Box range

    Rounding bottom #

    A long-term bullish reversal pattern in which a decline gradually flattens into a shallow U- or bowl-shaped low and then curves back upward, best seen on weekly charts. It represents a slow transfer from distribution to accumulation — sentiment shifting from bearish to bullish at a steady pace — and is confirmed when price breaks above the reaction high at the start of the pattern. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Instead of a violent V-shaped reversal, selling pressure fades week by week and buying interest returns just as gradually, tracing a bowl — patient accumulation replacing capitulation.
    Mechanics: The decline into the low and the advance out of it should take roughly comparable time; the low should be rounded over weeks, not a sharp spike. Ideally volume also traces a saucer: high during the initial decline, minimal through the flat middle, rising on the advance. Confirmation is a breakout above the pattern's left-edge reaction high with volume expansion; duration (months to years) indicates significance.
    Failure modes: Because it forms over months, the pattern invites countless premature calls — most 'rounding bottoms' in progress are just flat, trendless drift that eventually resolves downward. A 'broken saucer' (price spiraling away before completing the bowl) is a recognized failure variant.
    Misconceptions: That there is a reliable measured price target — conventionally there is none, though some analysts project the depth of the U. Also that the two sides must be symmetrical; capturing the gradual sentiment shift matters more than geometric perfection.
    Example: A stock declines from $17 to $11.22 over months, bases in a flat saucer near $12, then advances with steadily rising volume and breaks above the $17 left-edge high — confirming the long-term reversal.
    Aliases: Saucer bottom, Saucer, Bowl, U-shaped bottom

    Rounding top #

    A long-term bearish reversal pattern, the mirror of the rounding bottom, in which an advance gradually flattens into an inverted-U or dome shape and then curves downward as sentiment shifts slowly from bullish to bearish. It is interpreted as a distribution process — informed holders selling gradually without disturbing the price — and is confirmed when price breaks down from the dome's base. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Buyers lose the power to push price higher but sellers exit slowly, so the chart rolls over in a gentle dome rather than a spike; once the artificial support of patient distribution ends, price falls away.
    Mechanics: Price rounds over after an extended uptrend, with volume typically highest at both ends of the pattern and lowest in the middle (an inverted volume saucer). The pattern is confirmed when price falls from the edge of the dome, often with a volume pickup; rounding tops usually take less time to form than rounding bottoms. There is no standard measured target; some analysts project the depth of the inverted U.
    Failure modes: Gradual domes frequently resolve upward — a renewed breakout through the dome's high turns the 'distribution' into a continuation base, so acting before the downside break is the main error. The pattern's rarity and long formation time make clean examples scarce.
    Misconceptions: That a rounding top needs a sharp breakdown to be valid — the defining feature is the gradual rollover; confirmation is simply price leaving the dome's edge. It is also wrongly assumed to take as long as a rounding bottom; tops typically form faster.
    Example: After a multi-year uptrend, a stock's monthly closes arc over a dome between $80 and $95 for a year, volume swelling at both ends; when price slides off the right edge of the dome below $80, the distribution pattern is considered confirmed.
    Aliases: Inverted saucer, Inverse saucer, Dome top

    Cup and handle #

    A bullish continuation pattern popularized by William O'Neil in 'How to Make Money in Stocks' (1988), consisting of a U-shaped consolidation (the cup) followed by a smaller downward-drifting pullback (the handle) on the right side, completed by a breakout above the handle's resistance. The cup resembles a rounding bottom within an existing uptrend. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: After a strong rally the stock digests its gains in a wide bowl; the small final dip (the handle) shakes out the last weak holders before the uptrend resumes through the old high.
    Mechanics: Require a prior uptrend a few months old; the cup should be U-shaped (a sharp V is disqualifying) and ideally retrace no more than one-third to one-half of the prior advance. The handle forms in the upper half of the cup, retracing up to roughly one-third of the cup's advance, over one to several weeks. The trigger is a breakout above handle resistance with a substantial volume increase; the target projects the cup's depth from the breakout point.
    Failure modes: Handles that drift too deep (retracing more than half the cup) or form in the cup's lower half signal weak demand and high failure rates; low-volume breakouts frequently fail. In bear markets the breakout often becomes a bull trap at the old high.
    Misconceptions: That any V-shaped dip followed by a pullback qualifies — O'Neil's specification demands a rounded U cup, a shallow handle in the upper half, and volume expansion on the breakout. Also that the pattern works without a prior uptrend; it is a continuation pattern by construction.
    Example: A stock runs from $10 to $32.69, rounds out a three-month cup with a 42% retracement, forms a six-week handle, then gaps above handle resistance at $32 on surging volume; the measured target is about $41.
    Price target = B + (R - C), where B = breakout price above handle resistance, R = right peak of the cup, and C = low of the cup.
    Aliases: Cup with handle, Cup-and-handle continuation

    Broadening formation #

    A classical technical-analysis pattern in which successively higher highs and lower lows produce two diverging trend lines, so the trading range widens over time — the opposite of a triangle. Occurring more often at tops (where it is conventionally assigned bearish implications), it is read as a market out of control, with public speculation replacing informed participation. It is an analytical hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Each swing overshoots the last in both directions — buyers chase new highs and then panic to new lows — a signature of emotional, leaderless trading rather than orderly accumulation or distribution.
    Mechanics: Require five minor reversals (three successively higher peaks and two lower troughs, or vice versa), with volume irregular and offering no directional clue. The bearish reading is confirmed only when price breaks below the lower of the two declining troughs and keeps falling. There is no standard measured target; the widening swings themselves define the pattern.
    Failure modes: Because every swing breaks a prior extreme, breakout traders are repeatedly whipsawed in both directions; the 'confirmation' breakdown can itself be just another widening swing that reverses into a new high. Trend-line placement is unusually subjective given the expanding range.
    Misconceptions: That broadening formations are common, reliable topping signals — they are comparatively rare, and the widening structure resolves in either direction often enough that it is best treated as a warning of instability, not a short signal by itself.
    Example: A stock in a late-stage rally peaks at $50, dips to $44, rallies to $53, falls to $41, and spikes to $56 on chaotic volume; when price then breaks decisively below $41, the broadening top is considered confirmed.
    Aliases: Megaphone pattern, Broadening top, Broadening bottom, Expanding triangle, Inverted triangle

    Diamond top #

    A rare classical reversal pattern at the end of an uptrend whose outline resembles a diamond — often tilted — formed by an initial broadening phase (higher peaks and lower valleys) followed by a contracting phase (lower peaks and higher valleys, like a symmetrical triangle). The conventional expectation is a downward breakout reversing the prior rise. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Volatility first explodes outward and then collapses inward, as if the market thrashed itself into exhaustion; the diamond's tip marks where the uptrend's energy runs out.
    Mechanics: Draw trend lines connecting the peaks and valleys: diverging in the first half, converging in the second; price should touch each boundary once or twice and volume trends downward roughly 55–59% of the time. Breakouts are downward about 54% of the time in Bulkowski's sample, confirmed by a close outside a boundary. The measure rule projects the pattern's height (highest peak minus lowest valley) from the breakout; after a quick vertical rise into the diamond, price often returns near the launch level.
    Failure modes: Diamonds are hard to identify correctly (often skewed, with outliers hiding the shape), and Bulkowski ranks upward breakouts from diamond tops last of 39 patterns for performance — expecting continuation up from this 'reversal' pattern is the weakest bet. Pullbacks after the breakout frequently bust the signal.
    Misconceptions: That a diamond top must resolve downward — the downward bias is only about 54%, barely better than chance, so the breakout direction, not the label, should drive action.
    Example: After a near-vertical rise from $20 to $35, a stock's swings widen then narrow into a tilted diamond around $30–$36; a close below the lower-right boundary near $31 triggers the measure rule toward the high $20s, near the rally's launch point.
    Price target = B +/- (P - V), where B = breakout price, P = highest peak in the diamond, and V = lowest valley (sign follows breakout direction).

    Diamond bottom #

    A rare classical reversal pattern at the end of a downtrend whose outline resembles a diamond, formed by an initial broadening phase (higher peaks and lower valleys) followed by a contracting phase (lower peaks and higher valleys). The conventional expectation is an upward breakout reversing the prior decline; in Bulkowski's sample, diamond bottoms with upward breakouts were the best-performing of the 36 patterns he ranked for downward breakouts. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Panic first whips price violently in both directions, then the swings die down into a point — the diamond marks the moment the selling storm blows itself out and buyers quietly take over.
    Mechanics: Draw trend lines connecting peaks and valleys: diverging in the first half, converging in the second, with price touching each boundary once or twice; volume trends downward about 67% of the time. Confirmation is a close outside a boundary, which historically is upward about 74% of the time. The measure rule projects the pattern's height from the breakout; after a quick plunge into the diamond, price often recovers toward (but usually just short of) the launch level.
    Failure modes: The shape is genuinely difficult to spot — skewed diamonds and price outliers cause frequent misidentification — and throwbacks/pullbacks after the breakout hurt performance. The remaining ~26% of downward resolutions can be severe continuations of the downtrend.
    Misconceptions: That every diamond-shaped wiggle is tradeable — without the clear broadening-then-contracting structure and a confirmed close outside the boundary, the 'pattern' is noise.
    Example: After a fast drop from $50 to $38, a stock thrashes between $36 and $44 with widening then narrowing swings; a close above the upper-right boundary near $42 confirms the diamond bottom, and price recovers toward the $48–$50 launch area.
    Price target = B +/- (P - V), where B = breakout price, P = highest peak in the diamond, and V = lowest valley (sign follows breakout direction).

    Measured move #

    A classical price-projection framework in which a trend leg, a corrective retracement, and a second trend leg are expected to be roughly equal in price (and, in the idealized version, in time): the second leg 'measures' the first. It is used both as a continuation pattern (a measured move up/down) and as a target-setting rule. It is a technical-analysis heuristic; empirical samples show the second leg typically falls short of the ideal.
    Intuition: Markets often move in two similar thrusts separated by a pause; once you know the size of the first thrust and where the pause ended, you can estimate how far the second thrust should carry.
    Mechanics: Identify three turning points: leg one from low A to high B, a corrective phase retracing part of that move to C, then a second leg from C toward D. Bulkowski's large-sample statistics: average first-leg rise 36% in 38 days, average retrace 48% in 27 days, average second-leg rise 31% in 33 days, with about 60% meeting the full price target; deeper retraces improve target achievement. The pattern also exists inverted (measured move down).
    Failure modes: The second leg fails to materialize when the corrective phase breaks down into a full reversal (price below C invalidates a measured move up); statistically the second leg undershoots the first by roughly a sixth on average. Overhead resistance or trend lines crossing the projected target frequently cap the move.
    Misconceptions: That the second leg must equal the first in price and time — that equality is the idealized version; observed second legs are shorter on average, so the full measure is a reference, not an expectation.
    Example: A stock rises from $20 (A) to $30 (B), retraces to $26 (C), then begins a second leg; the measured-move target is $26 + $10 = $36, and a drop back below $26 would invalidate the setup.
    Price target = C + (B - A), where A = start (low) of leg 1, B = end (high) of leg 1 / start of the corrective phase, and C = low of the corrective phase; some practitioners scale (B - A) by the historical target-hit rate. For a measured move down: target = C - (A - B) with the corresponding peaks/troughs.
    Aliases: Measured move up, Measured move down, Swing measurement, AB=CD move

    Parabolic advance #

    A chart formation in which an uptrend accelerates so that successive rallies steepen and the price path curves upward like a parabola, often with stair-step consolidations ('bases') beneath a curving trend line. It is interpreted as crowd-driven euphoria whose slope is mathematically unsustainable, so the pattern is used as a bearish-reversal framework: the signal is price breaking back below the curved trend line. It is an analytical hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: When each wave of buying has to be more frantic than the last just to keep the rally going, the move is feeding on itself; once the curve cracks, there is no orderly bid underneath.
    Mechanics: Identify a prevailing bullish trend, locate a series of measured pullbacks, and draw a curving (parabolic) trend line beneath the pullback lows; as the slope steepens, each base forms higher and faster. The trade signal is a close beneath the curved trend line, with a stop above the pattern's upper extreme and targets set from the curve or via Fibonacci retracements. Parabolic moves frequently retrace a large share of the entire advance.
    Failure modes: Strong trends stay 'parabolic' far longer than seems possible, and repeatedly re-steepening curves generate false breakdown signals against powerful momentum; shorting early into euphoria is a classic account-killer. In rotating, trendless markets the curved-line construction has no meaning.
    Misconceptions: That a steep rally must reverse immediately — the pattern only signals exhaustion risk, and the actionable event is the break of the curved trend line, not the steepness itself. It is also unrelated to Wilder's Parabolic SAR indicator despite the shared name.
    Example: A currency pair rises in three accelerating waves, each pullback higher and shallower, tracing a curve beneath the lows; when price closes below the curve at 1.3595, a short is entered with a stop above the 1.3627 high.
    Aliases: Parabolic arc, Parabolic curve, Parabolic run, Blow-off trajectory

    Parabolic decline #

    The inverted analogue of the parabolic advance: a downtrend that accelerates so successive sell-offs steepen and the price path curves downward like a downward-opening parabola, typically driven by panic, forced liquidation, or cascading stops. It is interpreted as a capitulation dynamic whose slope is unsustainable, with a tentative bullish-reversal signal when price breaks back above the curved trend line overhead. It is an analytical hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Each wave of selling panics more holders into selling, so the fall keeps steepening — until everyone who could be forced out is out, and the collapse exhausts itself.
    Mechanics: Identify a prevailing downtrend, connect the rally highs within it with a curving downward trend line, and watch for accelerating, gap-prone legs lower often on climactic volume. The reversal signal is a close back above the curved trend line, with a stop below the pattern's lower extreme; recoveries frequently retrace a large fraction of the decline, mirroring the upward arc's tendency to retrace.
    Failure modes: Falling knives: parabolic declines driven by genuine insolvency or fraud never recover, so 'unsustainable slope' is not a floor. Each re-steepening produces false rebound signals, and stops placed just above the curve are repeatedly run in volatile crashes.
    Misconceptions: That a parabolic fall guarantees a V-shaped recovery — the exhaustion-bounce framing is a hypothesis, and many parabolic declines are the first leg of a longer bear market. It shares only a name with the Parabolic SAR indicator.
    Example: A stock drops in three accelerating legs from $60 to $22, each bounce weaker, tracing a downward curve through the rally highs; when price finally closes back above the curve at $26 on heavy volume, the capitulation-reversal reading is triggered.
    Aliases: Parabolic sell-off, Waterfall decline, Capitulation arc
