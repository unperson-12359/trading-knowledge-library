    Open interest change #

    The day-over-day (or period-over-period) change in open interest — the total number of futures contracts, long or short, in a delivery month or market that have been entered into and not yet liquidated by an offsetting transaction or fulfilled by delivery. Rising open interest indicates new positions being created; falling open interest indicates positions being closed.
    Intuition: Volume counts how many contracts changed hands; the change in open interest tells you whether hands are entering or leaving the market.
    Mechanics: Computed as OI_t - OI_{t-1} from exchange-published open interest. A trade between two new entrants increases OI by one; between two existing holders closing out, it decreases OI by one; between an entrant and an exiter, OI is unchanged. Analysts combine the sign of the OI change with price direction to infer whether money is flowing into or out of a move.
    Failure modes: OI changes say nothing about who is trading or their intent — the same increase can be new speculative longs or new commercial shorts. Around contract roll periods, front-month OI mechanically falls while deferred OI rises, so contract-level changes mislead unless aggregated across the curve.
    Misconceptions: Rising open interest does not mean 'more buyers than sellers' — every futures contract has both a buyer and a seller by construction. It also does not measure volume; high volume with flat OI indicates churn (day trading) rather than new positioning.
    Example: Crude oil OI rises from 1.80 million to 1.85 million contracts (+50,000) while price rallies $2: new money is entering long and short, which analysts read as fresh positioning behind the move rather than short covering.
    ΔOI = OI_t - OI_{t-1}, where OI = total open contracts (each counted once, either on the long or short side).
    Aliases: change in open interest, delta OI

    Volume-open-interest analysis #

    A technical-analysis framework for futures markets that interprets price trends jointly with trading volume and open interest to judge whether a move is supported by new positioning or by liquidation of existing positions. It is an analytical framework or heuristic, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Price tells you where the market went; volume tells you how much business was done; open interest tells you whether traders are staying or leaving — read together, they describe the 'conviction' behind a move.
    Mechanics: The classic readings: price up + volume up + OI up is taken as a strong trend (new money entering); price up + OI down suggests short covering (weaker); price down + OI up suggests new shorts entering; price down + OI down suggests long liquidation. The CFTC glossary notes chartists plot trends of price movements, volume of trading, and open interest together.
    Failure modes: The framework is unfalsifiable in its loose form — the same OI/volume pattern can be rationalized as bullish or bearish after the fact. Roll periods, spread trading, and block/EFP activity distort both volume and OI, and rising electronic market-making has weakened the link between OI changes and directional conviction.
    Misconceptions: High volume does not 'confirm' a trend in any causal sense, and OI cannot show net bullishness because every contract has a long and a short. The rules of thumb come from practitioner lore, not from validated statistical models.
    Example: Wheat rallies 3% on record volume while total open interest climbs 8%: the analyst reads new longs and shorts entering and treats the breakout as position-backed; a similar rally on falling OI would be read as short covering and viewed with more suspicion.
    Aliases: volume and OI analysis, price-volume-OI framework

    Commitments of Traders report #

    A weekly report published by the CFTC providing a breakdown of each Tuesday's open interest for markets in which 20 or more traders hold positions at or above CFTC-established reporting levels. The legacy report separates open interest into commercial, non-commercial, and non-reportable holdings; disaggregated and TFF variants provide finer categories.
    Intuition: It is a weekly census of who is long and who is short in each major futures market — hedgers versus speculators versus small traders.
    Mechanics: Compiled from confidential daily large-trader data collected under the CFTC's market surveillance program, published Fridays reflecting Tuesday positions. Available in futures-only and futures-and-options-combined formats, short and long formats; long-format reports add nonreportable positions, crop-year breakdowns, and concentration ratios for the largest four and eight traders.
    Failure modes: The data are three days stale at publication and classify traders, not trades — a 'commercial' position is not necessarily a hedge, and staff classification involves judgment. Categories can be reclassified over time, and historical disaggregated data were backcast using recent classifications, reducing accuracy further back in time.
    Misconceptions: COT is not a real-time positioning feed, and 'noncommercial net position' is not 'the dumb money' — the category includes sophisticated macro funds. Extreme readings are not mechanical contrarian signals; large speculative positions can persist and grow for months in trending markets.
    Example: The legacy COT for corn shows commercials net short 300,000 contracts and noncommercials net long 220,000; analysts track how these net positions shift week to week as a positioning indicator.
    Aliases: COT report, COT

    Commercial trader #

    In the CFTC's legacy Commitments of Traders report, a reportable trader whose futures positions are commercially motivated — traditionally producers, merchants, processors, and users of the physical commodity who manage business risks by hedging, plus swap dealers offsetting OTC risk in the futures market. In the Disaggregated COT this group is split into Producer/Merchant/Processor/User and Swap Dealers.
    Intuition: Commercials are the industry players using futures to insure their real business, not to bet on price direction.
    Mechanics: Classification is made by CFTC staff from Form 40 disclosures and other information, based on the trader's predominant activity; staff classify traders, not individual trades, so a commercial's positions may include some non-hedge activity. Legacy commercial open interest is published long, short, and (for count purposes) spreading.
    Failure modes: Because classification follows the trader rather than the trade, commercial positions can include speculation and swap-dealer inventory, muddying the 'hedging' interpretation. Multi-functional organizations are placed in one category by predominant activity, so mixed businesses are imperfectly represented.
    Misconceptions: Commercials are not automatically 'smart money' that is always right — they are hedgers whose net short in a bull market simply reflects offsetting long physical exposure. Their net position is a byproduct of risk transfer, not a directional forecast.
    Example: A grain elevator that has bought farmers' corn sells corn futures to lock in the price; in the legacy COT its short futures are reported as commercial positions.
    Aliases: commercials, hedgers (COT context)

    Noncommercial trader #

    In the CFTC's legacy Commitments of Traders report, a reportable trader who does not qualify as commercial — encompassing professional money managers such as CTAs, CPOs, and hedge funds as well as a wide array of other non-commercial (speculative) traders. In the Disaggregated COT this group is split into Managed Money and Other Reportables.
    Intuition: Noncommercials are the market's risk-takers: participants trading futures for profit rather than to hedge a physical business.
    Mechanics: Classification is by CFTC staff judgment using Form 40 and other data, based on predominant activity. The legacy report shows noncommercial open interest long, short, and spreading, where spreading is the computed amount of offsetting long and short positions held by the same trader in different calendar months.
    Failure modes: The category is a residual mix — trend-following CTAs, discretionary macro funds, and other speculators are lumped together, so aggregate 'speculative' positioning can hide offsetting sub-currents. Classification involves judgment and can change over time, breaking continuity in historical series.
    Misconceptions: Noncommercial does not mean retail or uninformed — most are professional managers; nor does a large noncommercial net position reliably mark tops or bottoms, since trends can extend positioning far beyond historical extremes.
    Example: A CTA running a trend-following program builds a net long of 50,000 crude oil contracts; these appear in the legacy COT as noncommercial longs, and in the Disaggregated COT as managed money.
    Aliases: non-commercials, large speculators, speculators (COT context)

    Managed money #

    A trader category in the CFTC's Disaggregated Commitments of Traders report comprising registered commodity trading advisors (CTAs), registered commodity pool operators (CPOs), and unregistered funds identified by the CFTC — traders engaged in managing and conducting organized futures trading on behalf of clients.
    Intuition: It is the 'fund money' line in the COT report: positions run by professional managers trading other people's capital.
    Mechanics: CFTC staff assign reportable traders to the category using Form 40 data and other information, based on predominant activity; hedge funds are included regardless of registration status. Managed money open interest is reported long, short, and spreading, where spreading is computed from offsetting positions in different calendar months of the same or related contracts.
    Failure modes: Classification is at the trader level, so a fund's hedge-like spread books and directional books are aggregated; the spreading column partially offsets but residual judgment remains. Unregistered and offshore funds are identified only where the CFTC can identify them, so coverage is not perfectly complete.
    Misconceptions: Managed money is not synonymous with all speculators — other reportables also speculate; nor is it the same as the legacy noncommercial category, which also included non-fund speculators. Managed-money net length is a positioning fact, not evidence of informed forecasting skill.
    Example: A CPO operating a commodity pool with a large long in gold futures is classified as managed money; its position appears in the Disaggregated COT's managed money long column each week.
    Aliases: money managers (COT), managed money category

    Producer merchant processor user #

    A trader category in the CFTC's Disaggregated Commitments of Traders report: an entity that predominantly engages in the production, processing, packing, or handling of a physical commodity and uses the futures markets to manage or hedge risks associated with those activities.
    Intuition: These are the true commercials of the physical supply chain — the farmers, elevators, crushers, and end-users hedging real inventory and inputs.
    Mechanics: CFTC staff classify reportable traders into the category using Form 40 data and, where appropriate, direct conversations, based on predominant activity. For this category open interest is reported only as long or short — no spreading column — unlike the swap dealer, managed money, and other reportables categories.
    Failure modes: Some PMPU-classified traders also engage in swaps activity, and the CFTC cannot know with certainty that all of a trader's activity is hedging, since staff classify traders, not their trading activity. Multi-service organizations centralized in one trading entity force judgment calls on predominant activity.
    Misconceptions: The category is not limited to farmers and miners — merchants and processors (e.g. grain elevators, soybean crushers, refiners) count too. Their positions should not be read as directional views; a PMPU short usually represents hedged physical inventory.
    Example: A flour miller that buys wheat futures to hedge its forward flour sales is classified as a producer/merchant/processor/user, and its longs appear in that COT column.
    Aliases: PMPU, producer/merchant/processor/user

    Swap dealer category #

    A trader category in the CFTC's Disaggregated Commitments of Traders report: an entity that deals primarily in swaps for a commodity and uses the futures markets to manage or hedge the risk associated with those swaps transactions, whether its counterparties are speculators such as hedge funds or traditional commercial clients.
    Intuition: These are banks and dealers whose futures positions are the warehouse for risk they took on from OTC swap clients — the futures leg is the hedge, not the bet.
    Mechanics: The category was created in 2009 on the recommendation of the CFTC's 2008 Staff Report on Commodity Swap Dealers & Index Traders, splitting swap dealers out of the legacy commercial category. Swap dealer open interest is reported long, short, and spreading; commodity index business typically appears here because dealers hedge index swaps with futures.
    Failure modes: Some swap-dealer-classified traders also conduct physical-commodity commercial activity, blurring the line with the PMPU category; classification reflects predominant activity and can change. The category is related to, but not identical with, the commodity index trader category in the CIT supplement — direct index investors using futures fall under managed money or other reportables instead.
    Misconceptions: A large swap-dealer long is not a speculative bet — it typically hedges short exposure from client index swaps. The category also does not map to the legal registration definition of 'swap dealer' under Dodd-Frank (which carries a de minimis notional threshold); it is a COT reporting classification.
    Example: An investment bank that sells a commodity index swap to a pension fund buys the underlying basket of futures to hedge; its futures longs are reported in the Disaggregated COT under swap dealers.
    Aliases: swap dealers (COT), swap dealer classification
