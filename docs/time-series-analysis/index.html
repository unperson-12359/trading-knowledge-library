    Time series #

    A sequence of observations on a variable recorded in time order, typically at equally spaced intervals, in which data points may have internal structure such as autocorrelation, trend, or seasonal variation that must be accounted for when modeling. In trading, prices, returns, volumes, and indicators are all time series.
    Intuition: Unlike a random sample, the order of observations matters: yesterday's price is informative about today's, so time series methods model the dependence across time rather than treating points as independent draws.
    Mechanics: A univariate time series is a set of scalar observations {x_t} indexed by time t. Analysis proceeds by characterizing trend, seasonality, autocorrelation, and volatility structure, then fitting models (e.g., ARMA, state-space) that exploit that dependence for description, inference, or forecasting.
    Failure modes: Standard i.i.d. statistical procedures (tests, standard errors) break down when applied to autocorrelated or trending series, producing spurious significance. Non-equally-spaced or missing data complicate many standard methods.
    Misconceptions: That any sequence of numbers is a valid time series for analysis regardless of sampling frequency or missing values; and that time ordering can be ignored when computing summary statistics or running regressions.
    Example: Daily closing prices of the S&P 500 from 2015-2025 form a time series; the strong correlation between consecutive closes violates the independence assumption of ordinary statistical methods.
    A univariate time series is written x_1, x_2, ..., x_T where t indexes time; time is an implicit variable even though only the values are recorded.
    Aliases: time-series data, temporal data

    Stationarity #

    The property of a stochastic process whose statistical behavior does not change over time. A strictly stationary process has all joint distributions invariant to time shifts; in practice stationarity usually means a flat-looking series with no trend, constant variance, a constant autocorrelation structure, and no periodic fluctuations.
    Intuition: A stationary series behaves the same in 2020 as in 2010: it fluctuates around a stable level with stable variability, so its past behavior is a usable guide to its future behavior.
    Mechanics: Stationarity is assessed from run-sequence plots, ACF behavior, and formal unit-root tests (e.g., augmented Dickey-Fuller). Non-stationary series are transformed by differencing (Y_t = Z_t - Z_{t-1}), detrending (modeling residuals from a fitted trend), or variance-stabilizing transforms such as the logarithm.
    Failure modes: Applying stationary methods (ARMA, ordinary regression) to non-stationary data yields spurious regressions with misleadingly high R-squared and significance. Structural breaks can make a locally stationary model fail even when tests pass.
    Misconceptions: That a stationary series must have no variation (it only needs time-invariant statistical properties), and that detrending and differencing are interchangeable (they address different types of non-stationarity).
    Example: Daily S&P 500 returns are approximately stationary, fluctuating around a near-constant mean; the price level is non-stationary and is typically differenced (into returns) before fitting an ARMA model.
    Strict stationarity: for all h and k, the joint distribution of (X_t1, ..., X_tk) equals that of (X_{t1+h}, ..., X_{tk+h}). Differencing operator: Y_t = Z_t - Z_{t-1}.
    Aliases: stationary process

    Weak stationarity #

    A form of stationarity in which only the first two moments of a process are required to be time-invariant: the mean and variance are constant and the autocovariance between two points depends only on the lag between them, not on their location in time. This is the assumption underlying ARMA modeling and most time-series inference.
    Intuition: Weak stationarity demands only that the series' average level, spread, and lag-pattern of correlation stay the same over time - a much weaker requirement than the full probability distribution being unchanging.
    Mechanics: Formally: E[X_t] = mu (constant), Var(X_t) = sigma^2 (finite and constant), and Cov(X_t, X_{t+h}) = gamma(h) depends only on lag h. Gaussian processes that are weakly stationary are also strictly stationary; heavy-tailed processes can be weakly stationary without being strictly so.
    Failure modes: Weak stationarity says nothing about higher moments or the full distribution, so volatility clustering or regime changes can persist in a covariance-stationary-looking series and break models that assume more. Financial returns often exhibit time-varying variance, violating it.
    Misconceptions: That weak stationarity implies the series is normally distributed or has constant conditional volatility; only the unconditional mean, variance, and autocovariances are constrained.
    Example: A GARCH-modeled return series can be weakly stationary (constant unconditional mean and variance) even though its conditional variance changes every day.
    E[X_t] = mu; Var(X_t) = sigma^2 < infinity; Cov(X_t, X_{t+h}) = gamma(h) for all t, h.
    Aliases: covariance stationarity, second-order stationarity, wide-sense stationarity

    Unit root #

    A property of a time series whose autoregressive representation has a root equal to one, making it difference-stationary rather than level-stationary: shocks have permanent effects and the series follows a stochastic trend. A unit-root process becomes stationary only after differencing.
    Intuition: A series with a unit root has no tendency to return to any fixed level; every shock shifts it permanently, like a drunken walk that wanders without an anchor.
    Mechanics: Detected with unit-root tests such as the augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test, which regresses the change in the series on its lagged level and lagged changes; the null hypothesis is that a unit root exists (series is non-stationary). Rejection supports stationarity. MacKinnon critical values or p-values are used for significance.
    Failure modes: Unit-root tests have low power against near-stationary alternatives and against trend-stationary processes with structural breaks, frequently failing to reject non-stationarity even when it is false (and vice versa). Spurious regressions arise when unit-root series are regressed on each other without cointegration checks.
    Misconceptions: That failing to reject the ADF null proves a unit root (the test only fails to find evidence against it), and that a trending series must have a unit root (deterministic trends are stationary around the trend).
    Example: Testing log S&P 500 prices with the ADF test typically fails to reject the unit-root null, while testing daily returns rejects it, consistent with prices being I(1) and returns I(0).
    ADF regression: Delta y_t = alpha + beta*y_{t-1} + sum_{i=1..k} gamma_i*Delta y_{t-i} + e_t; null hypothesis beta = 0 (unit root present) versus beta < 0 (stationary).
    Aliases: unit root process, integrated of order one, I(1)

    Random walk #

    A stochastic process in which each value equals the previous value plus an unpredictable increment (white noise), so the best forecast of tomorrow's value is today's. In finance, the random-walk hypothesis holds that price changes are unpredictable because new information is incorporated into prices immediately.
    Intuition: Each step is a fresh coin flip added to where you stand now; knowing the whole history of steps tells you nothing about the next one.
    Mechanics: X_t = X_{t-1} + epsilon_t with epsilon_t white noise (zero mean, constant variance, uncorrelated). The process is a unit-root process: its variance grows with time and it never reverts to a mean. A random walk with drift adds a constant c per period: X_t = c + X_{t-1} + epsilon_t.
    Failure modes: Real prices deviate from a pure random walk through autocorrelated returns at short horizons, volatility clustering, and longer-horizon predictability documented in the asset-pricing literature; treating prices as an exact random walk misses these features.
    Misconceptions: That the random-walk hypothesis says prices move randomly for no reason (it says price changes reflect unpredictable news, not that prices are arbitrary), and that it rules out all predictability over all horizons (evidence mainly concerns short horizons).
    Example: Fama's work beginning in the 1960s found stock prices extremely difficult to predict in the short run, consistent with price changes behaving approximately like random-walk increments.
    X_t = X_{t-1} + epsilon_t, epsilon_t ~ WN(0, sigma^2); with drift: X_t = c + X_{t-1} + epsilon_t. Then Var(X_t) grows linearly in t.
    Aliases: random walk hypothesis, random walk process

    Drift #

    The deterministic, systematic component of a stochastic process - the average rate at which the process is expected to move per unit of time, distinct from its random fluctuations. In a geometric Brownian motion or random walk with drift, it is the constant expected change per period.
    Intuition: Drift is the steady current in a river; the waves are random, but the current carries you in one direction on average.
    Mechanics: In a random walk with drift X_t = c + X_{t-1} + epsilon_t, c is the drift and the process trends upward (or downward) by c per period on average. In continuous time, dS_t = mu*S_t*dt + sigma*S_t*dW_t has drift mu*S_t, the expected instantaneous return. Drift is estimated as a sample mean of increments and is hard to estimate precisely because estimation error shrinks only with the length of the time span, not the sampling frequency.
    Failure modes: Drift estimates over short samples are extremely imprecise: decades of daily data still leave wide confidence intervals on annual expected returns. Assuming a constant drift fails when expected returns are time-varying or regime-dependent.
    Misconceptions: That more frequent sampling (e.g., minute data) improves drift estimates - it does not, because drift uncertainty depends on the total time span; and that a visible trend in a short sample establishes a nonzero drift.
    Example: U.S. equity prices are often modeled as a random walk with positive drift of roughly 6-8% per year, but estimating that drift from a 20-year sample carries a standard error of several percentage points.
    Random walk with drift: X_t = c + X_{t-1} + epsilon_t, so E[X_t - X_{t-1}] = c. GBM: dS_t = mu*S_t*dt + sigma*S_t*dW_t, with mu the drift (expected return).
    Aliases: drift term, trend drift

    Autocorrelation #

    The correlation of a time series with itself at different time lags: a measure of how strongly an observation is linearly related to observations h periods earlier or later. Significant nonzero autocorrelation means the series is not random white noise.
    Intuition: Autocorrelation asks: does a high value today tend to be followed by high values tomorrow (positive) or by low values (negative), or is there no relationship at all?
    Mechanics: The sample autocorrelation at lag h is R_h = C_h / C_0, where C_h = (1/N)*sum_{t=1}^{N-h}(Y_t - mean)(Y_{t+h} - mean) is the sample autocovariance and C_0 the sample variance. Under randomness, autocorrelations should lie within bands of about +/-z_{1-alpha/2}/sqrt(N); systematic patterns signal structure and are used to identify AR/MA model orders.
    Failure modes: Absence of autocorrelation does not imply randomness: nonlinear dependence (e.g., volatility clustering in squared returns) survives zero linear autocorrelation. In non-stationary series, sample autocorrelations are spuriously large and slow-decaying.
    Misconceptions: That zero autocorrelation proves independence (uncorrelated is not independent), and that significant daily-return autocorrelation in stock indexes is directly exploitable profit after transaction costs (usually it is not).
    Example: The autocorrelation plot of a strongly autoregressive series shows large positive R_1 decaying gradually; the same plot for white noise shows all R_h near zero within the 95% bands.
    R_h = C_h / C_0, with C_h = (1/N) * sum_{t=1}^{N-h} (Y_t - Ybar)(Y_{t+h} - Ybar); R_h lies in [-1, 1].
    Aliases: serial correlation, lagged correlation

    Partial autocorrelation #

    The correlation between a time series value and its value h periods earlier after removing the linear effect of all intervening lags (1 through h-1). It isolates the direct lag-h relationship that the ordinary autocorrelation confounds with indirect chains of correlation.
    Intuition: If today's value correlates with last week's only because both depend on yesterday, the partial autocorrelation at lag 7 removes that indirect path and shows whether a genuine direct link remains.
    Mechanics: Computed as the coefficient on the h-th lag in a regression of X_t on X_{t-1}, ..., X_{t-h}, or equivalently via Yule-Walker equations, Levinson-Durbin recursion, or Burg's estimator. For an AR(p) process the partial autocorrelation cuts off after lag p, making it a key tool for identifying autoregressive order.
    Failure modes: Sample estimates differ across estimation methods (Yule-Walker, OLS, Burg) especially in short samples; misspecified lag selection can make cutoff identification ambiguous. Non-stationary data invalidate the interpretation.
    Misconceptions: That partial autocorrelation at lag h is just the autocorrelation at lag h 'cleaned up' numerically - it answers a different question (direct vs total dependence) and can differ in sign and magnitude.
    Example: For an AR(2) process, the sample PACF shows significant spikes at lags 1 and 2 and near-zero values beyond lag 2, identifying the AR order.
    pacf(h) = coefficient phi_hh in the regression X_t = phi_h1*X_{t-1} + ... + phi_hh*X_{t-h} + e_t.
    Aliases: PACF coefficient, partial serial correlation

    Autocorrelation function #

    The full set of autocorrelations of a time series viewed as a function of the lag h, usually plotted as a correlogram with confidence bands. The ACF's shape (exponential decay, cutoff, oscillation) characterizes the dependence structure and guides model identification.
    Intuition: Instead of asking whether the series depends on its past at one specific lag, the ACF scans all lags at once, revealing the memory profile of the series.
    Mechanics: The theoretical ACF of a stationary process is rho(h) = gamma(h)/gamma(0); the sample ACF plots R_h = C_h/C_0 against h = 1, 2, ... with approximate 95% bands of +/-1.96/sqrt(N) under the null of white noise. MA(q) processes show an ACF that cuts off after lag q; AR processes show decaying ACFs.
    Failure modes: For non-stationary series the sample ACF decays very slowly, mimicking strong persistence and misleading identification. With many lags examined, some spuriously exceed the confidence bands by chance (roughly 5% under the null).
    Misconceptions: That any ACF spike outside the bands proves exploitable predictability; at 20 lags about one spike is expected by chance, and statistical significance is not economic significance after costs.
    Example: The ACF of squared daily stock returns decays slowly over many lags, revealing long memory in volatility even though raw returns show almost no autocorrelation.
    rho(h) = Cov(X_t, X_{t+h}) / Var(X_t); sample estimate R_h = C_h / C_0 with C_h = (1/N)*sum_{t=1}^{N-h}(Y_t - Ybar)(Y_{t+h} - Ybar).
    Aliases: ACF, correlogram

    Partial autocorrelation function #

    The partial autocorrelations of a time series viewed as a function of lag h, plotted with confidence bands. Whereas the ACF measures total dependence at each lag, the PACF measures only the direct dependence net of intermediate lags, and its cutoff after lag p is the classic signature of an AR(p) process.
    Intuition: The PACF is a lag-by-lag scan asking: after accounting for everything in between, does the value h steps back still tell me anything new about today?
    Mechanics: Estimated at each lag h as the final coefficient of an order-h autoregression (Yule-Walker, OLS, Levinson-Durbin, or Burg methods). Used jointly with the ACF for Box-Jenkins model identification: AR(p) shows PACF cutoff at p with decaying ACF; MA(q) shows ACF cutoff at q with decaying PACF; ARMA shows decay in both.
    Failure modes: Cutoff identification is judgmental in real data where estimates are noisy and series are short or seasonal; over-reliance on visual cutoffs leads to over- or under-specified models. Non-stationarity produces slowly decaying, uninformative PACFs.
    Misconceptions: That the PACF is a redundant duplicate of the ACF; the two answer different questions and are used together because they have complementary cutoff/decay signatures.
    Example: A monthly sales series with a PACF spike at lag 1 and lag 12 only, and an ACF that decays, suggests an AR structure with a seasonal lag-12 term.
    PACF(h) = phi_hh, the coefficient on X_{t-h} in X_t = sum_{j=1..h} phi_hj*X_{t-j} + e_t, computed recursively from the autocovariances.
    Aliases: PACF, partial correlogram

    White noise #

    A time series of uncorrelated random variables with zero mean and constant finite variance - a process with no linear dependence structure at any lag. White noise is the building block (shock sequence) of ARMA-type models and the benchmark against which randomness is tested.
    Intuition: White noise is pure unpredictable static: every value is a fresh, unrelated draw, so no pattern in the past helps predict the future.
    Mechanics: A white-noise sequence {epsilon_t} satisfies E[epsilon_t] = 0, Var(epsilon_t) = sigma^2, and Cov(epsilon_t, epsilon_{t+h}) = 0 for h != 0. Its ACF is zero at all lags (sample values fall inside the +/-1.96/sqrt(N) bands). Gaussian white noise adds independence and normality; weaker white noise allows uncorrelated but dependent increments.
    Failure modes: Uncorrelatedness rules out only linear predictability: squared white-noise-like financial returns are strongly autocorrelated (volatility clustering), so a series can be white noise yet have predictable variance. Tests for white noise can also fail to detect weak dependence in short samples.
    Misconceptions: That white noise must be normally distributed (it need only be uncorrelated with constant variance), and that a model whose residuals look like white noise is necessarily a good forecasting model (it only means no linear structure remains).
    Example: Residuals from a well-specified ARMA model of daily returns should behave approximately like white noise: their autocorrelation plot shows no spikes outside the confidence bands.
    epsilon_t ~ WN(0, sigma^2): E[epsilon_t] = 0, Var(epsilon_t) = sigma^2, Cov(epsilon_t, epsilon_{t+h}) = 0 for all h != 0.
    Aliases: white noise process, WN

    Innovation #

    The unpredictable new information entering a time series at each period - the part of the current value that cannot be forecast from the series' own past. In ARMA and state-space models, innovations are the white-noise input sequence that drives the process.
    Intuition: The innovation is the surprise in each new observation: the gap between what actually happened and the best prediction you could have made a period earlier.
    Mechanics: In an MA model the current value is a weighted sum of current and past innovations; in an AR model the innovation A_t is the white-noise term added to the predictable autoregressive part. Model estimation treats fitted one-step-ahead errors as innovation estimates, and a correctly specified model leaves innovations that resemble white noise.
    Failure modes: Estimated innovations inherit model misspecification: if the model is wrong, the 'innovations' retain structure and are not genuine surprises. Non-Gaussian innovation distributions (fat tails in finance) break Gaussian-likelihood-based inference.
    Misconceptions: That innovation and residual are identical concepts (residuals are sample-based estimates of innovations from a fitted model), and that innovations must be independent rather than merely unpredictable in the linear sense.
    Example: If an AR(1) model predicts today's return as 0.3 times yesterday's, the difference between the realized return and that prediction is today's innovation.
    For AR(p): X_t = delta + phi_1*X_{t-1} + ... + phi_p*X_{t-p} + A_t, where A_t is the white-noise innovation. Formally, the innovation is nu_t = X_t - E[X_t | X_{t-1}, X_{t-2}, ...].
    Aliases: shock, disturbance, one-step-ahead prediction error

    AR model #

    A time-series model in which the current value is expressed as a linear combination of its own p previous values plus a white-noise innovation. It is a linear regression of the series on its own lags and is stationary when the roots of its characteristic polynomial lie outside the unit circle.
    Intuition: An AR model says today is explained by momentum or mean-reversion from the recent past: how much of yesterday (and the days before) carries into today.
    Mechanics: An AR(p) model X_t = delta + phi_1*X_{t-1} + ... + phi_p*X_{t-p} + A_t is fit by linear least squares or maximum likelihood; the order p is identified from the PACF cutoff and information criteria. Its ACF decays gradually while its PACF cuts off after lag p. Stationarity requires the coefficients to satisfy constraints (e.g., |phi_1| < 1 for AR(1)).
    Failure modes: Pure AR structure cannot capture shock-driven dynamics that need MA terms; wrong order selection underfits (residual autocorrelation) or overfits (poor out-of-sample forecasts). AR coefficients estimated on trending or non-stationary data are biased toward spurious persistence.
    Misconceptions: That a high R-squared in an AR regression of a price level indicates forecasting power - levels regressions on near-unit-root data are misleading; returns-based AR models for stocks show little exploitable linear predictability.
    Example: An AR(2) fitted to monthly industrial production uses the two previous months' values to forecast the next, with the PACF showing significant spikes at lags 1 and 2.
    X_t = delta + phi_1*X_{t-1} + ... + phi_p*X_{t-p} + A_t, where delta = (1 - sum phi_i)*mu with mu the process mean, and A_t white noise.
    Aliases: autoregressive model, AR(p)

    MA model #

    A time-series model in which the current value is a constant plus a linear combination of the current and q past white-noise innovations. Despite the name, it is not a smoothing average of the series but a regression on unobserved past shocks, and it is always stationary.
    Intuition: An MA model says shocks linger for exactly q periods and then vanish: today's value reflects today's surprise plus fading echoes of the last q surprises.
    Mechanics: An MA(q) model X_t = mu + A_t - theta_1*A_{t-1} - ... - theta_q*A_{t-q} cannot be fit by ordinary least squares because the A terms are unobserved, so iterative nonlinear (maximum likelihood) estimation is used. Its ACF cuts off after lag q while its PACF decays, the mirror image of AR behavior.
    Failure modes: Estimation is numerically harder than AR because innovations are latent; non-invertible parameterizations make forecasts unstable. MA structure cannot capture momentum-like dependence on lagged levels.
    Misconceptions: That an MA model is the same as a moving-average smoothing of prices used in technical analysis; the MA(q) time-series model weights past shocks, not past observed values.
    Example: An MA(1) for daily returns says today's return depends on today's news shock plus a fraction of yesterday's shock, so the ACF is nonzero only at lag 1.
    X_t = mu + A_t - theta_1*A_{t-1} - theta_2*A_{t-2} - ... - theta_q*A_{t-q}, with A_t white-noise innovations and q the model order.
    Aliases: moving average model, MA(q)

    ARMA model #

    A time-series model combining an autoregressive part of order p and a moving-average part of order q, expressing a stationary series as a function of its own lagged values and lagged innovations. Box and Jenkins popularized a systematic identification-estimation-validation methodology for ARMA models.
    Intuition: ARMA captures both ways the past echoes into the present: through the series' own lagged levels and through lingering shocks.
    Mechanics: ARMA(p,q): X_t = delta + phi_1*X_{t-1} + ... + phi_p*X_{t-p} + A_t - theta_1*A_{t-1} - ... - theta_q*A_{t-q}. By the Wold decomposition any weakly stationary process can be approximated by an ARMA model. Fitting requires a stationary series (difference first if needed, giving ARIMA), moderately long samples (roughly 50-100+ observations), and maximum likelihood estimation; ACF/PACF patterns guide the choice of p and q.
    Failure modes: ARMA assumes linearity, stationarity, and time-invariant parameters; structural breaks, regime changes, and volatility clustering in financial data violate these, so fitted models deteriorate out of sample. Parameter redundancy (near-common AR/MA factors) causes identification problems.
    Misconceptions: That a high in-sample ARMA fit implies the series is predictable going forward; in-sample fit does not establish out-of-sample forecast skill, and near-zero-return predictability in asset prices is the empirical norm.
    Example: An ARMA(1,1) fitted to a stationary macro series uses last period's value and last period's shock to forecast, identified by decaying patterns in both the ACF and PACF.
    X_t = delta + sum_{i=1..p} phi_i*X_{t-i} + A_t - sum_{j=1..q} theta_j*A_{t-j}, with A_t white noise.
    Aliases: ARMA(p,q), Box-Jenkins model

    ARIMA model #

    An integrated autoregressive moving-average model, ARIMA(p,d,q), that extends the ARMA model to non-stationary series by differencing the data d times and then fitting autoregressive (order p) and moving-average (order q) terms to the differenced series. It is the core of the Box-Jenkins identification-estimation-validation methodology.
    Intuition: Many real series wander with a trend or unit root, so you difference them until they look stationary, model the differences with an ARMA model, and sum forecasts back up to the original scale.
    Mechanics: Identification uses the ACF and PACF of the (possibly differenced) series to choose p, d, q; parameters are estimated by (conditional) maximum likelihood; residuals are checked for remaining autocorrelation. Forecasts of the differenced series are integrated (cumulatively summed) to produce level forecasts.
    Failure modes: The differencing choice is fragile: under-differencing leaves a unit root and spurious inference, while over-differencing induces artificial moving-average structure and inflates forecast variance. ARIMA also assumes a stable linear process, so structural breaks degrade forecasts.
    Misconceptions: That ARIMA models trending or seasonal series directly: the 'I' handles only stochastic trends via differencing, and periodic structure needs seasonal terms or prior deseasonalizing. A good in-sample fit also does not imply genuine predictive content.
    Example: Log prices of an equity index are close to a random walk, so an ARIMA(0,1,1) models the change in log price as a moving average of past forecast errors and integrates the forecasts back to price levels.
    phi(B)(1-B)^d X_t = c + theta(B) A_t, where B is the backshift operator, phi(B) = 1 - phi_1 B - ... - phi_p B^p the AR polynomial, theta(B) = 1 - theta_1 B - ... - theta_q B^q the MA polynomial, and A_t white noise.
    Aliases: ARIMA(p,d,q), Box-Jenkins model

    Seasonal ARIMA #

    A multiplicative extension of the ARIMA model that adds seasonal autoregressive, seasonal differencing, and seasonal moving-average terms at the seasonal period s, written SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_s, to model series with stable repeating periodic patterns.
    Intuition: Monthly sales or quarterly earnings resemble not only the adjacent period but the same period a year ago, so the model regresses on lags spaced by the season length as well as on short lags.
    Mechanics: In addition to the non-seasonal operators phi(B), theta(B) and differencing (1-B)^d, the model includes seasonal polynomials Phi(B^s), Theta(B^s) and seasonal differencing (1-B^s)^D, usually combined multiplicatively. Identification examines ACF spikes at the seasonal lags s, 2s, ... alongside the short lags.
    Failure modes: The parameter count grows quickly with seasonal terms, inviting overfitting on short samples; several full seasonal cycles are needed for identification. If the seasonal pattern itself shifts (calendar changes, regime change), the fixed-period terms misfit.
    Misconceptions: That seasonal ARIMA discovers arbitrary periodicities: s must be specified a priori from the data frequency, and the model assumes the seasonal period and shape are stable over the sample.
    Example: Monthly airline passengers spike every December; SARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_12 differences once and seasonally, then models residuals with both a one-month and a twelve-month moving-average term.
    phi(B) Phi(B^s) (1-B)^d (1-B^s)^D X_t = theta(B) Theta(B^s) A_t, where s is the seasonal period and (P,D,Q) the seasonal AR, differencing, and MA orders.
    Aliases: SARIMA, SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)s, multiplicative seasonal ARIMA

    Vector autoregression #

    A multivariate time-series model in which each variable of a system is regressed on its own lags and the lags of every other variable, treating all variables as jointly endogenous. Sims (1980) introduced it as an alternative to heavily restricted simultaneous-equation macro models.
    Intuition: Instead of deciding in advance which variable drives which, let every variable potentially influence every other with lags and let the data reveal the dynamic interactions.
    Mechanics: A VAR(p) for a k-vector y_t stacks k equations, each estimated by OLS on the same regressors (a constant and p lags of all variables). Analysis proceeds through Granger-causality tests, impulse-response functions, and forecast-error variance decompositions; structural interpretation requires additional identifying restrictions.
    Failure modes: The parameter count grows quadratically in the number of variables, so VARs overfit quickly in short samples. Reduced-form residuals are correlated across equations, so impulse responses depend on the chosen identification scheme or variable ordering.
    Misconceptions: That a VAR reveals structural causal relationships: without identifying restrictions it is a reduced-form summary of dynamic correlations, and Granger-causal ordering is not economic causation.
    Example: A bivariate VAR on 10-year yields and inflation traces how a one-standard-deviation inflation shock propagates into yields over the following quarters through the impulse-response function.
    y_t = c + A_1 y_{t-1} + ... + A_p y_{t-p} + u_t, where y_t is a k-vector, each A_i a k x k coefficient matrix, and u_t vector white noise with covariance matrix Sigma_u.
    Aliases: VAR, VAR(p)

    Cointegration #

    A property of two or more individually non-stationary (e.g., I(1)) series for which some linear combination of them is stationary, indicating a stable long-run equilibrium relationship even though each series wanders. Engle and Granger (1987) formalized the concept together with its representation, estimation, and testing.
    Intuition: Two drunkards on a leash: each price wanders unpredictably, but the spread between them is tethered and keeps snapping back, so the spread is forecastable even though the levels are not.
    Mechanics: In the Engle-Granger two-step procedure, one I(1) series is regressed on the other(s) by OLS and the residuals are tested for stationarity (augmented Dickey-Fuller); if the residuals are I(0), the series are cointegrated and the regression estimates the long-run vector. Johansen's likelihood procedure generalizes this to multiple cointegrating vectors within a VAR.
    Failure modes: Standard OLS on non-cointegrated I(1) series yields spurious regressions with inflated R-squared and t-statistics. Cointegration tests have low power and are sensitive to structural breaks, lag length, and sample period.
    Misconceptions: That high correlation between two trending series implies a meaningful relationship: correlated random walks are typically spurious, and only a stationary residual spread evidences a genuine equilibrium.
    Example: Spot and futures prices of crude oil each behave like unit-root processes, but their spread (the basis) is stationary, so pairs strategies model the basis rather than either level.
    If x_t and y_t are each I(1) and there exists beta such that z_t = y_t - beta x_t is I(0), then y_t and x_t are cointegrated with cointegrating vector (1, -beta).
    Aliases: cointegrated series, long-run equilibrium relationship

    Error-correction model #

    A dynamic model for cointegrated series in which short-run changes respond to lagged changes plus an error-correction term measuring the deviation from the long-run equilibrium, so the system adjusts back toward equilibrium over time. Its validity follows from the Granger representation theorem of Engle and Granger (1987).
    Intuition: The cointegrating spread acts like a rubber band: when last period's gap from equilibrium was wide, today's changes pull the series back toward it, at a speed set by the adjustment coefficient.
    Mechanics: Estimate the long-run cointegrating vector, form the equilibrium error z_{t-1} = y_{t-1} - beta x_{t-1}, and regress Delta y_t on lagged changes of both series plus alpha z_{t-1}; a negative, significant alpha confirms error correction. In the vector setting (VECM) each equation carries its own loading on the equilibrium errors.
    Failure modes: Everything hinges on the estimated cointegrating vector: if it is misspecified or unstable, the error-correction term injects noise rather than signal. Adjustment may be asymmetric or threshold-type in practice, which the linear ECM misses.
    Misconceptions: That the error-correction term by itself shows which variable does the adjusting: the loading coefficients must be estimated (weak exogeneity tested), and interpreting alpha as causality carries the usual reduced-form cautions.
    Example: Modeling consumption changes as Delta c_t = -0.2 (c_{t-1} - 0.9 y_{t-1}) + short-run dynamics implies consumption closes about 20% of its gap from the long-run income relationship each quarter.
    Delta y_t = alpha (y_{t-1} - beta x_{t-1}) + sum_i gamma_i Delta y_{t-i} + sum_j delta_j Delta x_{t-j} + epsilon_t, where alpha < 0 is the speed of adjustment and (y - beta x) the equilibrium error.
    Aliases: ECM, VECM, vector error-correction model

    Granger causality #

    A statistical precedence concept: a series x Granger-causes y if past values of x improve the forecast of y beyond what past values of y alone provide, tested by F-tests on lagged coefficients in an autoregression or VAR. Introduced by Granger (1969).
    Intuition: If knowing yesterday's thunder helps predict today's rain beyond what yesterday's rain tells you, thunder Granger-causes rain: it is incremental predictive content, not metaphysical cause.
    Mechanics: Regress y_t on p of its own lags (restricted model), then add p lags of x_t (unrestricted model); an F-test of the joint null that all x-lag coefficients are zero rejects Granger non-causality. The test runs in both directions and generalizes in a VAR to block-exogeneity Wald tests.
    Failure modes: Results are sensitive to lag length, sample period, and omitted variables: an omitted common driver manufactures apparent causality. With integrated or cointegrated data, standard F-test distributions are invalid without Toda-Yamamoto or VECM adjustments.
    Misconceptions: That Granger causality is true causality: it is only incremental predictability within the chosen information set, and enlarging that set can reverse the conclusion.
    Example: Testing whether money-supply growth Granger-causes GDP growth amounts to adding lagged money growth to an autoregression of GDP growth and jointly testing its coefficients.
    In y_t = a_0 + sum_{i=1}^p a_i y_{t-i} + sum_{j=1}^p b_j x_{t-j} + e_t, x Granger-causes y if the b_j are jointly non-zero (F-test of H_0: b_1 = ... = b_p = 0).
    Aliases: Granger causality test, Granger-Sims causality

    Heteroskedasticity #

    A violation of the constant-error-variance assumption in regression in which the conditional variance of the disturbance varies across observations, typically as a function of the regressors or of time. OLS coefficients remain unbiased under heteroskedasticity but are no longer efficient, and conventional standard errors are biased.
    Intuition: The size of the model's mistakes is not constant: errors may balloon for large values of a regressor or in turbulent periods, so treating every observation as equally precise is wrong.
    Mechanics: Detected by plotting squared residuals against fitted values or regressors and by formal tests such as Breusch-Pagan (regress squared residuals on the regressors and test joint significance) or White's test. Remedies include heteroskedasticity-consistent (White/Huber) standard errors, generalized least squares, or explicit variance models.
    Failure modes: Ignoring heteroskedasticity invalidates t- and F-tests and confidence intervals even though point estimates stay unbiased; in finance, volatility clustering makes homoskedastic inference badly overconfident.
    Misconceptions: That heteroskedasticity biases the coefficient estimates: it does not; it corrupts inference (standard errors) and efficiency, not the unbiasedness of OLS.
    Example: A regression of household consumption on income shows residual spread widening with income; a Breusch-Pagan test on squared residuals rejects constant variance, so White standard errors are reported instead.
    Var(epsilon_i | x_i) = sigma_i^2 != sigma^2; in the Breusch-Pagan test, e_i^2 is regressed on x_i and nR^2 of that regression is asymptotically chi-squared under homoskedasticity.
    Aliases: heteroscedasticity, non-constant variance

    Conditional heteroskedasticity #

    Time-varying variance of a time series in which the variance of the innovation, conditional on past information, changes predictably over time, typically clustering so that large changes are followed by large changes. This is the defining feature modeled by the ARCH family introduced by Engle (1982).
    Intuition: Volatility comes in waves: after a big market move, the near future is likely to be volatile too, so the risk forecast should update with recent shocks instead of being one fixed number.
    Mechanics: The innovation is written epsilon_t = sigma_t z_t with z_t i.i.d. and sigma_t^2 a function of past information (past squared innovations in ARCH, plus past variances in GARCH). Conditional variance is then forecastable even when the conditional mean is not, and it is estimated by maximum likelihood jointly with the mean equation.
    Failure modes: Conditional-variance models assume a stable data-generating process; structural breaks in unconditional volatility are absorbed into spuriously high persistence estimates. Forecasts revert to the unconditional variance within a modest horizon.
    Misconceptions: That volatility clustering contradicts unpredictability of returns: returns can be nearly unforecastable in level while their squares are strongly autocorrelated.
    Example: For S&P 500 daily returns, the ACF of returns is near zero while the ACF of squared returns is large and slowly decaying, evidence of conditional heteroskedasticity that motivates GARCH-type models.
    epsilon_t = sigma_t z_t with z_t ~ i.i.d.(0,1); Var(epsilon_t | F_{t-1}) = sigma_t^2 = f(epsilon_{t-1}^2, epsilon_{t-2}^2, ..., sigma_{t-1}^2, ...).
    Aliases: time-varying volatility, volatility clustering

    ARCH model #

    Autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity: a model in which the conditional variance of the current innovation is a linear function of past squared innovations, so volatility is serially correlated and forecastable. Engle (1982) introduced it to model the time-varying variance of inflation.
    Intuition: Treat variance like an autoregression on squared shocks: if yesterday's surprise was big, today's expected variance is big, which produces the volatility clustering seen in financial returns.
    Mechanics: ARCH(q) sets sigma_t^2 = omega + sum_{i=1}^q alpha_i epsilon_{t-i}^2 with omega > 0 and alpha_i >= 0, fitted by maximum likelihood jointly with a mean equation (e.g., ARMA). Covariance stationarity of the process requires the sum of the alpha_i to be less than one.
    Failure modes: A large q is often needed to capture long volatility memory, making estimation noisy, and symmetric ARCH cannot represent the leverage effect whereby negative shocks raise volatility more than positive ones. Structural breaks inflate apparent persistence.
    Misconceptions: That ARCH models the conditional mean: it models the conditional variance of the error, while the mean equation (often zero or ARMA) is specified separately.
    Example: Engle's original application modeled the variance of UK inflation as depending on squared forecast errors from earlier quarters, separating periods of high and low inflation uncertainty.
    epsilon_t = sigma_t z_t, z_t ~ i.i.d.(0,1); sigma_t^2 = omega + alpha_1 epsilon_{t-1}^2 + ... + alpha_q epsilon_{t-q}^2, with omega > 0, alpha_i >= 0, and sum alpha_i < 1 for covariance stationarity.
    Aliases: ARCH(q), autoregressive conditional heteroscedasticity

    GARCH model #

    Generalized ARCH: a model in which the conditional variance depends on both past squared innovations and past conditional variances, parsimoniously capturing the slowly decaying volatility persistence of financial returns. Introduced by Bollerslev (1986).
    Intuition: Variance today is a weighted average of a long-run level, yesterday's shock, and yesterday's variance estimate: a smoothing recursion that reacts to shocks but relaxes back to normal gradually.
    Mechanics: GARCH(p,q) sets sigma_t^2 = omega + sum_{i=1}^q alpha_i epsilon_{t-i}^2 + sum_{j=1}^p beta_j sigma_{t-j}^2; a GARCH(1,1) approximates a high-order ARCH with three parameters and is estimated by (quasi-)maximum likelihood. Covariance stationarity requires the persistence sum alpha + beta to be below 1, though values near 1 are typical for daily asset returns.
    Failure modes: Symmetric GARCH misses the asymmetric response of volatility to negative shocks (addressed by EGARCH and GJR-GARCH), and structural breaks are absorbed as near-unit persistence. Quasi-MLE standard errors are fragile if the innovation distribution is misspecified.
    Misconceptions: That a GARCH forecast of high volatility implies a predictable direction for returns: GARCH forecasts variance only and says nothing about the sign of returns.
    Example: Fitting GARCH(1,1) to daily equity returns typically yields alpha around 0.08 and beta around 0.90, so a volatility shock decays with a half-life of roughly 6-7 trading days.
    sigma_t^2 = omega + alpha epsilon_{t-1}^2 + beta sigma_{t-1}^2 with omega > 0, alpha >= 0, beta >= 0, alpha + beta < 1; the unconditional variance is omega / (1 - alpha - beta).
    Aliases: GARCH(p,q), generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity
