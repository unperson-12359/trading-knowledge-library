    Monte Carlo simulation reviewed #

    The use of repeated random sampling to approximate quantities that are difficult to compute analytically, such as expectations, distributions of estimators, option prices, or the sampling variability of a trading rule, by generating many pseudo-random realizations and averaging. Named and systematized by Metropolis and Ulam (1949).
    Intuition: If you cannot solve the probability problem on paper, play it out thousands of times with a random number generator and use the empirical frequencies as the answer.
    Mechanics: Draw pseudo-random samples from the assumed model, compute the quantity of interest on each draw, and average; the Monte Carlo error falls as 1/sqrt(N), so accuracy is controlled by the replication count. Variance-reduction techniques (antithetic variates, control variates, importance sampling) improve efficiency.
    Failure modes: Answers are only as good as the model being simulated: Monte Carlo propagates rather than fixes wrong distributional assumptions, such as Gaussian draws understating tail risk. Rare-event estimates converge slowly without importance sampling.
    Misconceptions: That a more sophisticated generator removes model risk: simulation explores the implications of assumed dynamics but does not validate them against reality.
    Example: Estimating a path-dependent option price: simulate 100,000 price paths under the model, compute the discounted payoff on each, and average, with a standard error read from the spread of payoffs.
    E[g(X)] approximately (1/N) sum_{i=1}^N g(X_i) with X_i drawn from the target distribution; the standard error of the estimate is sd(g(X))/sqrt(N).
    Aliases: Monte Carlo method, stochastic simulation

    Bootstrap reviewed #

    A resampling method that approximates the sampling distribution of a statistic by repeatedly resampling the observed data with replacement and recomputing the statistic, using the empirical distribution as a stand-in for the unknown population. Introduced by Efron (1979).
    Intuition: Pretend your sample is the whole population: draw many new samples from it with replacement, watch how much the statistic wiggles across them, and read off standard errors and confidence intervals without distributional formulas.
    Mechanics: From n observations, draw B bootstrap samples of size n with replacement, recompute the statistic on each, and use the spread of the bootstrap replicates for standard errors, bias estimates, and percentile or BCa confidence intervals. The method is consistent for smooth statistics under i.i.d. sampling.
    Failure modes: The i.i.d. bootstrap fails for dependent data, where it destroys serial structure and block variants are required, and for non-smooth or extreme statistics such as sample minima. It also fails when the sample poorly represents the population tails.
    Misconceptions: That the bootstrap creates new information: it reuses only what is in the sample, so with small or unrepresentative samples its intervals are no better than the data allow.
    Example: Estimating a confidence interval for a strategy's Sharpe ratio: resample the 500 daily returns 10,000 times with replacement, recompute the Sharpe each time, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.
    SE_boot = sqrt( (1/(B-1)) sum_b (theta*_b - mean(theta*))^2 ); the percentile interval uses the alpha/2 and 1 - alpha/2 quantiles of the replicates theta*_b.
    Aliases: bootstrap resampling, nonparametric bootstrap

    Block bootstrap reviewed #

    A bootstrap variant for dependent data that resamples contiguous blocks of observations rather than individual points, preserving short-run serial dependence within each block while still generating artificial samples. Developed by Kunsch (1989) as the moving-blocks bootstrap.
    Intuition: Resampling single returns would shuffle away the volatility clustering and momentum you need to preserve, so you resample whole chunks of consecutive days and let dependence live inside each chunk.
    Mechanics: Divide the series into overlapping (moving-blocks) or non-overlapping blocks of length l, resample the blocks with replacement, concatenate them into pseudo-series of length n, and recompute the statistic on each. Block length trades bias (too short breaks dependence) against variance (too long leaves few distinct blocks), with l typically growing as n^{1/3}.
    Failure modes: Results are sensitive to block length, and dependence spanning more than the block length is still severed at block boundaries, biasing statistics that depend on long memory.
    Misconceptions: That any block length works once blocks are used: too-short blocks reproduce the i.i.d. bootstrap's failure on dependent data, and the choice is a genuine tuning problem.
    Example: Testing a trend-following rule's t-statistic: generate moving-block bootstrap return series with l = 20 days, rerun the rule on each, and compare the real t-statistic to the bootstrap distribution.
    For x_1,...,x_n, blocks B_i = (x_i, ..., x_{i+l-1}); a bootstrap sample concatenates ceiling(n/l) blocks drawn uniformly with replacement.
    Aliases: moving block bootstrap, MBB

    Permutation test reviewed #

    An exact, distribution-free significance test that computes the p-value by recomputing the test statistic over all (or many random) rearrangements of the data labels consistent with the null hypothesis, generating the null distribution from the data themselves. Systematized by Pitman (1937) following Fisher's randomization idea.
    Intuition: If the treatment made no difference, the labels are meaningless: shuffle them thousands of times, recompute the statistic each time, and see how extreme the real statistic is among the shuffled ones.
    Mechanics: Compute the statistic T on the observed assignment, permute group labels or pairing signs B times (or exhaustively), recompute T under each permutation, and take the p-value as the proportion of permuted statistics at least as extreme as the observed one. Validity requires exchangeability under the null, not any parametric distribution.
    Failure modes: Exchangeability fails under dependence: naive permutations of autocorrelated series destroy serial structure and give invalid p-values, requiring restricted or block permutations. Exhaustive enumeration is computationally feasible only for small samples.
    Misconceptions: That permutation tests are assumption-free: they trade distributional assumptions for the structural assumption of exchangeability, itself violated by cross-group heteroskedasticity and time dependence.
    Example: Testing whether a signal's long-short spread is real: permute the signal-to-date alignment 5,000 times in blocks, rebuild the spread each time, and check whether the real spread exceeds 99% of permuted versions.
    p = (1 + #{b : T_b >= T_obs}) / (B + 1) over B permutations of the data under the null of exchangeability.
    Aliases: randomization test, re-randomization test

    Multiple testing problem reviewed #

    The inflation of false-positive rates when many hypotheses are tested simultaneously: at a per-test level alpha, the probability of at least one false rejection across m tests grows toward 1 as m increases, so unadjusted testing reliably produces spurious discoveries.
    Intuition: A 5% test cries wolf one time in twenty by design; test a hundred strategies and you should expect about five wolves even if every strategy is worthless.
    Mechanics: The familywise error rate (probability of any false rejection) is controlled by Bonferroni (alpha/m) or Holm's step-down procedure; the false discovery rate is controlled by the Benjamini-Hochberg step-up procedure. In finance, Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) argue that the factor literature requires t-statistic thresholds well above 2 for exactly this reason.
    Failure modes: FWER control is conservative with many or correlated tests and destroys power, while FDR control assumes independence or positive dependence and needs the Benjamini-Yekutieli correction under arbitrary dependence.
    Misconceptions: That a single strong p-value is immune to multiplicity: what matters is how many hypotheses were examined to produce it, and p = 0.01 found among 200 tests is unremarkable under the null.
    Example: Scanning 300 calendar anomalies at alpha = 0.05 yields about 15 significant effects under pure chance; Bonferroni demands p < 0.00017 before declaring any of them real.
    FWER = P(at least one false rejection); Bonferroni rejects H_i when p_i <= alpha/m, giving FWER <= alpha.
    Aliases: multiplicity problem, multiple comparisons problem

    False discovery rate reviewed #

    The expected proportion of false rejections among all rejections in a multiple-testing procedure, FDR = E[V/R] (defined as 0 when R = 0), where V counts false positives among R rejections. Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) proposed controlling it as a less conservative alternative to familywise error rates.
    Intuition: Instead of demanding zero chance of any false positive, accept that a small controlled fraction of your discoveries may be false, buying back the power to find real effects among many tests.
    Mechanics: The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure orders the p-values p_(1) <= ... <= p_(m), finds the largest k with p_(k) <= (k/m) q, and rejects hypotheses 1 through k; this controls the FDR at level q under independence (and under positive dependence).
    Failure modes: BH control can fail under strong arbitrary dependence among test statistics, requiring the much more conservative Benjamini-Yekutieli adjustment, and the FDR bounds a rate, not which specific rejections are false.
    Misconceptions: That FDR control at 5% gives each rejected hypothesis a 5% chance of being false: it bounds the average proportion of false rejections across the whole rejection set.
    Example: Screening 1,000 candidate alpha signals at FDR q = 0.10, the BH procedure rejects 40, and in expectation no more than about 4 of those are false positives.
    FDR = E[V / max(R, 1)]; BH rejects H_(1),...,H_(k) where k = max{ i : p_(i) <= (i/m) q }.
    Aliases: FDR, Benjamini-Hochberg procedure

    Data snooping reviewed #

    The bias arising when the same data are used repeatedly to select and evaluate models or strategies, so the best-performing specification among those searched is optimistically biased in-sample even if none has genuine predictive ability. White's (2000) reality check formalized testing whether the best rule in a searched universe truly beats a benchmark.
    Intuition: If you let the data pick your strategy and then judge it on the same data, you are grading an exam the student wrote for themselves: the winner's edge is partly luck harvested by the search.
    Mechanics: Prevention uses untouched holdout data, walk-forward evaluation, and pre-registered specifications. Correction uses White's reality check and the superior predictive ability test, which compare the best searched rule to its null distribution via resampling, or deflated performance metrics that adjust for the number of trials.
    Failure modes: Holdout data lose their protection once consulted repeatedly during development (holdout snooping), and collective backtests by many researchers on public data create community-wide snooping that no single study can correct.
    Misconceptions: That a large in-sample t-statistic rules out snooping: the statistic is unadjusted for the search that produced it, and significance must be judged against the best-of-many null, not the single-test null.
    Example: White's reality check applied to universes of technical trading rules asks whether the best rule's apparent profit exceeds what the luckiest rule would achieve under the null of no predictive ability.
    Reality-check null: max over k of E[f_k] <= 0 across the searched universe of rules, tested by bootstrapping the maximum of the recentered performance statistics.
    Aliases: data mining bias, specification search bias

    Look-ahead bias reviewed #

    A backtesting error in which a simulated decision uses information that would not have been available at the simulated decision time, such as future prices, revised fundamentals, or full-sample statistics, artificially inflating measured performance.
    Intuition: It is trading with tomorrow's newspaper: any rule looks brilliant if at each historical date it quietly consults data that only became known later.
    Mechanics: Common forms include signals computed on a bar's close but executed at that same close, use of revised rather than point-in-time fundamentals, universes defined with hindsight, and features normalized with full-sample means and variances. Prevention requires point-in-time data, lagging signal computation relative to execution, and walk-forward rather than full-sample preprocessing.
    Failure modes: Subtle leaks, index membership, delisting returns, feature scaling, and cross-validation folds that interleave past and future, survive casual review and almost always bias results upward, making weak strategies appear strong.
    Misconceptions: That look-ahead bias requires explicitly using future prices: standard i.i.d. cross-validation on autocorrelated financial data leaks future information through overlapping folds just as surely.
    Example: A moving-average crossover computed with a centered window uses prices after the signal date; switching to a trailing window with next-open execution removes several percent of phantom annual return.
    Aliases: look-ahead, forward-looking bias

    Survivorship bias reviewed #

    The distortion of sample statistics caused by conditioning on survival: analyzing only the funds, stocks, or strategies that persisted to the end of the sample while dropping those that failed or delisted, thereby overstating average performance and understating risk. Quantified for mutual funds by Brown, Goetzmann, Ibbotson and Ross (1992).
    Intuition: Judging bomber safety by the holes in the planes that returned ignores the ones that never came back; the failures carry information the survivors cannot show.
    Mechanics: Dead and delisted entities disappear from standard databases, so average returns computed on survivors exclude the losses of the vanished, biasing mean performance and persistence estimates upward. Correction requires point-in-time universes that include delistings and dead funds with their full, often negative, terminal returns.
    Failure modes: The bias grows with attrition rates and return dispersion: databases with high dropout can overstate mean returns by several percentage points a year, and index backtests using today's constituents inherit it silently.
    Misconceptions: That adding currently known dead funds fixes it: the universe must be defined as of each historical date (point-in-time), not as of today minus remembered closures.
    Example: Brown et al. showed that apparent persistence in mutual fund rankings weakened or reversed once funds that had exited the sample were included in the analysis.
    Aliases: survival bias

    Selection bias reviewed #

    Systematic error introduced when the data selected for analysis are not representative of the target population because selection is related to the variables under study, so associations measured in the selected sample differ from those in the population.
    Intuition: You only observe the winners of some filtering process, whoever applied, survived, volunteered, or was listed, so what you measure reflects the filter as much as the phenomenon.
    Mechanics: It arises through non-random sampling, self-selection, conditioning on a collider or an outcome, attrition, and truncation. In econometrics, Heckman's two-step correction models the selection equation explicitly; in backtesting, defining the test universe with hindsight produces selection bias alongside survivorship bias.
    Failure modes: No within-sample statistical technique can repair selection on unobservables, because the bias concerns which data exist rather than how they are analyzed; only design changes (randomization, point-in-time sampling) or explicit selection models address it.
    Misconceptions: That a very large sample protects against selection bias: the bias does not shrink with n, and a huge biased sample converges precisely to the wrong answer.
    Example: Evaluating a signal only on stocks that passed a liquidity screen conditions on a variable related to returns, so the measured performance does not generalize to the full universe.
    Aliases: sample selection bias, sampling bias

    Publication bias reviewed #

    The distortion of the published research record caused by preferential publication of statistically significant or spectacular results over null or unremarkable ones, so the literature overstates effect sizes and the fraction of true effects. In finance it interacts with the 'factor zoo' documented by Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016).
    Intuition: Journals and authors both prefer headline results, so the file drawer fills with failures and the literature shows only the winners, like reading only the strategies that survived.
    Mechanics: Multiple-testing incentives plus significance-filtered publication inflate reported t-statistics and Sharpe ratios across hundreds of published return predictors. Diagnostics include funnel-plot asymmetry and p-curve analysis; mitigations include pre-registration, replication requirements, and raised significance thresholds that account for the searched universe.
    Failure modes: Meta-analyses that ingest the published record inherit its inflation, and the out-of-sample decay of published anomalies partly reflects the bias being revealed rather than arbitrage alone.
    Misconceptions: That peer review filters out false positives: review does not adjust for the many unreported tests behind each reported result, so publication itself is the selection mechanism.
    Example: Harvey, Liu and Zhu argue that given the volume of factor mining, a new factor should need a t-statistic above roughly 3.0 rather than the conventional 2.0.
    Aliases: file-drawer problem, reporting bias

    Overfitting reviewed #

    The production of a model that corresponds too closely to the particular training data, fitting noise as if it were structure, so it performs well in-sample but fails to generalize to new data. It arises whenever the selection criterion (training performance) diverges from the evaluation criterion (out-of-sample performance).
    Intuition: The model memorizes the textbook's answers instead of learning the subject: perfect on past questions, helpless on new ones.
    Mechanics: Risk grows with model complexity relative to data, weak signal-to-noise, and repeated reuse of the same data for tuning. Countermeasures include holdout test sets and cross-validation, regularization (ridge, lasso), early stopping, complexity penalties (AIC/BIC), and in trading walk-forward testing with trial-adjusted performance metrics.
    Failure modes: In-sample fit statistics (R-squared, backtest Sharpe) are upward-biased estimates of real performance, with the bias largest exactly where the search was most intense, and validation sets consulted repeatedly become training data.
    Misconceptions: That overfitting requires huge models: three parameters mined across thousands of specification choices overfit as surely as a million-parameter network, because overfitting lives in the selection process, not just model size.
    Example: A degree-20 polynomial threads through all 20 noisy training points with zero error yet predicts wildly off-trend on the next observation.
    Aliases: overtraining, over-fitting

    Underfitting reviewed #

    The failure of a model to capture the systematic structure present in the data, because it is too simple, too constrained, or trained too little, resulting in high bias and poor fit on both training and new data.
    Intuition: Drawing a straight line through a curve: the model is not wrong about the noise, it never even saw the signal.
    Mechanics: Diagnosed when training and validation errors are both high and close together (high bias), in contrast to the wide gap between low training and high validation error that marks overfitting. Remedies include richer model classes, additional relevant features, weaker regularization, or longer training.
    Failure modes: Underfit models push real effects into the error term, biasing coefficients and reading genuine structure as noise; in trading, a linear model of a genuinely nonlinear payoff concludes there is no signal.
    Misconceptions: That a simpler model is always safer: beyond some point simplicity destroys signal, and the bias-variance tradeoff penalizes both extremes.
    Example: Fitting a constant expected return to a strongly trending series leaves residuals that are themselves highly autocorrelated, the signature of the unmodeled trend.
    Aliases: high bias, under-fitting
