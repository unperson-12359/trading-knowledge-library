    Time-series momentum #

    A strategy that takes long positions in assets whose own trailing return is positive and short positions where it is negative - each asset against its own history rather than against peers - typically with volatility-scaled sizing. Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen (2012) document significant time-series momentum across 58 liquid futures in equity indexes, currencies, commodities, and bonds, persisting about a year and partially reversing thereafter.
    Intuition: Do not ask whether gold beat stocks; ask whether gold beat gold - if its own past year is up, be long, if down, be short, and size each bet so every market contributes equal risk.
    Mechanics: For each instrument, take the sign of the past 12-month excess return as the direction (long if positive, short if negative), size positions inversely to ex-ante volatility (estimated from exponentially weighted squared daily returns with a 60-day center of mass), and hold one month, averaging returns across cohorts. The diversified factor has little exposure to standard asset-pricing factors and performs best during extreme markets.
    Failure modes: Whipsawing ranges reverse the sign of trailing returns repeatedly, and sharp V-shaped reversals hit the strategy exactly when it is maximally positioned for the prior trend; crowding among trend-following funds compresses the effect out of sample.
    Misconceptions: That it is the same thing as cross-sectional momentum; time-series momentum uses an asset's own past return with no winner/loser ranking, and empirically it largely subsumes the cross-sectional stock effect while remaining a distinct construction.
    Example: Crude oil's trailing 12-month excess return is +18% with ex-ante volatility of 30%: go long sized at 40%/30% = 1.33 volatility units; natural gas at 60% volatility gets half that size, so both positions contribute equal risk.
    Per-instrument TSMOM return = sign(r_{t-12..t-1}) x (40% / sigma_{t-1}) x r_t, with sigma^2 = 261 x sum_i (1-delta) delta^i (r_{t-1-i} - r_bar)^2.
    Aliases: TSMOM, absolute momentum, trend momentum

    Source: Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen, 'Time Series Momentum', Journal of Financial Economics 104(2), 2012, 228-250 (author-hosted PDF, NYU Stern) — Sections 1-4

    Post-earnings announcement drift #

    The documented tendency of stock prices to continue moving in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeks after the announcement, and the strategy of trading that continuation. Bernard and Thomas (1989) showed cumulative abnormal returns drift with the sign of the surprise for roughly 60 days, concluding the pattern is consistent with a delayed price response rather than risk mismeasurement.
    Intuition: The market under-reacts to earnings: the announcement-day jump is only part of the move, so buying after good news - late, after everyone saw the print - historically still captured the slow digestion that followed.
    Mechanics: Rank announcements by standardized unexpected earnings or the announcement-window abnormal return; buy the highest-surprise decile and short the lowest, holding for the following weeks to a quarter. The effect is stronger for small companies and coexists with elevated announcement-period volatility.
    Failure modes: The anomaly weakens as it is arbitraged and can invert in high-attention, high-liquidity names where reaction is instant; trading costs and the need to act immediately after each announcement consume much of the gross drift.
    Misconceptions: That PEAD guarantees upside after every earnings beat; it is a statistical tendency across large samples of announcements with wide dispersion - individual prints reverse violently all the time.
    Example: Buy the decile of stocks with the largest positive earnings surprises the day after reporting and short the most negative decile; Bernard-Thomas's sample shows the long-short portfolio continuing to earn abnormal returns over the following ~60 days.
    SUE = (EPS_actual - EPS_expected) / sigma(EPS forecast errors); portfolios sorted on SUE show cumulative abnormal return drift over the subsequent ~60 trading days.
    Aliases: PEAD, earnings momentum (drift)

    Short squeeze trading #

    Trading that exploits the feedback loop in heavily shorted stocks, where rising prices force short sellers to buy shares to cover, pushing prices higher still and forcing further covering. Squeeze traders position long in high-short-interest names ahead of or during such cascades - a volatility event trade, not a valuation strategy.
    Intuition: Every short seller is a future guaranteed buyer; when the price rises enough that their losses force buying, the squeeze feeds on itself as the available float shrinks exactly when demand explodes.
    Mechanics: Screen for high short interest (percentage of float, days-to-cover), borrow-fee rates, and crowding; enter long on the triggering momentum and exit as covering exhausts. The SEC's 2021 staff report documents the GameStop episode: large short interest, surging individual-investor volume, price moves that forced covering, and resulting broker trading restrictions.
    Failure modes: Timing is everything: high short interest can persist for months while the stock bleeds lower, and late entries after the squeeze peak catch the collapse when covering ends and price returns toward fundamentals; trading halts and broker restrictions, as in January 2021, can trap positions entirely.
    Misconceptions: That a short squeeze reflects improving fundamentals; the SEC staff report attributes the GameStop price action to volume, sentiment, and covering dynamics rather than any change in the company's earnings prospects.
    Example: GameStop ran from $17.25 to a pre-market peak above $500 in January 2021 as shorts covered into rising prices, with cumulative short-seller losses estimated at about $5 billion and several brokers restricting purchases at the peak.
    Days to cover = short interest / average daily volume; squeeze risk rises with short interest as a percentage of float and with borrow cost.
    Aliases: squeeze trading (short side), short-covering rally trade

    Source: U.S. SEC, Staff Report on Equity and Options Market Structure Conditions in Early 2021 — Sections 3.2, 3.4 (GME equities trading; short selling and covering)

    Long squeeze trading #

    Trading around the downside mirror of a short squeeze: sharp price declines that force leveraged or stop-protected long holders to sell, whose selling pressures more longs into selling and accelerates the drop. The trade either shorts into the cascade or buys the capitulation once forced selling exhausts.
    Intuition: Crowded longs with stops at the same levels are dominoes: the first forced sales hit the next stops, and a normal dip becomes an air pocket - the squeeze trader profits from the mechanics of others' exits.
    Mechanics: Identify crowded long positioning (high leverage, clustered stop levels, recent parabolic gains); short the breakdown through the stop cluster, or wait for the volume-spike capitulation and buy the reversion. Long squeezes are typically shorter and shallower than short squeezes because falling prices eventually meet value buyers.
    Failure modes: Dips driven by long liquidations are indistinguishable from the start of genuine downtrends until afterward; buying capitulation in a real repricing is catching a falling knife, and shorting the breakdown risks immediate reversion once the shallow stop pool is cleared.
    Misconceptions: That every sharp down-move is a long squeeze to be bought; the term describes a specific forced-selling feedback loop, and without evidence of clustered leverage there is no mechanical reason to expect a bounce.
    Example: After a vertical run, a stock breaks a level dense with retail stops and drops 4% in minutes on huge volume with no news; the squeeze buyer waits for the volume climax, enters, and exits on the 2% snapback as forced selling dries up.
    Aliases: long liquidation cascade, capitulation trading

    Pairs convergence trade #

    The core pairs-trading position: when the spread between two historically co-moving securities diverges beyond a threshold, short the outperformer and buy the underperformer in expectation that the spread converges back to its historical relation. Gatev, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst (2006) formalized the rule, matching pairs by minimum distance between normalized price series and trading divergences beyond two standard deviations.
    Intuition: Two near-substitute stocks cannot stay mispriced relative to each other forever; the convergence trade sells whichever got too expensive and buys whichever got too cheap, hedging out the market's direction.
    Mechanics: Form pairs by minimum distance over a 12-month formation period; open when the normalized-price spread exceeds two historical standard deviations, and close on convergence, at the next formation date, or if the pair ceases to trade. Positions are dollar-neutral across the two legs, and a portfolio diversifies across many pairs.
    Failure modes: Convergence fails when the divergence is informational - a takeover rumor, fraud, or fundamental break re-prices one leg permanently and the spread widens through every reversion threshold; Gatev et al. themselves document declining profitability as the strategy crowded.
    Misconceptions: That matched pairs make the trade market-neutral and therefore safe; neutrality holds only for the common factor - idiosyncratic divergence risk is the whole position, and it is fat-tailed.
    Example: Two beverage stocks' normalized prices separate by 2.5 standard deviations; short the leader and buy the laggard dollar-neutral, then unwind for a profit when the spread closes to 0.3 standard deviations three weeks later.
    Open when |spread of normalized prices| > 2 sigma (historical); P&L approx. (converged spread - entry spread) per dollar-neutral unit.
    Aliases: pairs mean-reversion trade, convergence stat-arb

    Pairs divergence trade #

    The entry logic of a pairs trade: positioning as the spread between two linked securities diverges to an extreme, on the expectation that the divergence has run its course and the spread will close. In the Gatev-Goetzmann-Rouwenhorst framework, divergence beyond two historical standard deviations is precisely the event that triggers the dollar-neutral position.
    Intuition: Divergence is the signal, not the enemy: you wait for the rubber band between two stocks to stretch beyond historical tolerance, then take the position that profits as it snaps back.
    Mechanics: During the trading period, monitor the normalized-price spread of a formed pair; a divergence beyond +/-2 historical standard deviations opens the dollar-neutral long/short. Risk management defines a maximum adverse divergence (stop), because spreads can widen arbitrarily before closing - or never close.
    Failure modes: The central risk is divergence that never closes: structural breaks turn the entry signal into a trap, and each unit added to a widening spread increases exposure exactly as the probability of a permanent break rises.
    Misconceptions: That larger divergence means a better trade; beyond some point, extreme divergence correlates with pair breakdown (for example, an impending acquisition of one leg), so the biggest apparent opportunities carry the highest breakdown risk.
    Example: A pair's spread widens from 0 to -2.2 sigma over two weeks on no news; the trader buys the lagging leg and shorts the leader, with a stop at -3.5 sigma in case the relationship has broken.
    Trigger: |s_t| > 2 sigma_s, where s is the normalized price spread; position = short the high leg, long the low leg, in equal dollar amounts.
    Aliases: divergence entry (pairs), pairs spread opening trade
