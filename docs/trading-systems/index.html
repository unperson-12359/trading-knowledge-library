    Scalping #

    An ultra-short-horizon trading style in which a trader opens and closes positions within seconds to minutes to capture very small price movements, often exploiting the bid-ask spread or momentary micro-imbalances in highly liquid instruments. It is the shortest-horizon form of day trading and relies on high trade frequency, tight risk limits, and low per-trade costs.
    Intuition: Instead of trying to catch a big move, the scalper skims many tiny moves, accepting a few ticks of profit per trade and repeating this dozens or hundreds of times a day.
    Mechanics: Positions are established and liquidated within minutes or seconds, using direct-access execution, level-2/order-book data, and strict stop discipline. Profit per trade is typically a fraction of a percent, so commissions, fees, and slippage must be minimized; small losses are cut immediately because a single large loss can erase dozens of wins.
    Failure modes: Transaction costs and slippage can exceed the small gross edge; during volatility spikes or thin liquidity, stops are jumped and small losses become large ones.
    Misconceptions: That scalping is easy money because each trade is small and quick; in practice most retail scalpers lose after costs, and the SEC warns that day traders typically suffer severe financial losses. Scalping is also confused with the illegal practice of 'scalping' by advisers who tout a stock after secretly buying it.
    Example: A trader buys 2,000 shares of a liquid large-cap stock at $50.01 and sells them seconds later at $50.04, capturing about $60 gross before fees, repeating the process many times per session.
    Aliases: spread trading (historical)

    Day trading #

    A speculative trading approach in which a trader buys and sells (or sells and buys) the same financial instrument within the same trading day, closing all positions before the session ends to avoid overnight gap risk. Under FINRA rules, a 'day trade' is the purchase and sale of the same security on the same day in a margin account, and customers executing four or more day trades in five business days are designated pattern day traders subject to special margin requirements.
    Intuition: Day traders try to profit from intraday price swings, riding a stock's momentum for seconds to hours, and never hold positions overnight.
    Mechanics: Positions are opened and closed during a single session, often on margin and with direct-access platforms; true day traders hold nothing overnight. In U.S. margin accounts, pattern day traders must maintain at least $25,000 in equity and are subject to day-trading buying-power limits.
    Failure modes: High leverage, commissions, and spread costs cause most retail day traders to lose money; academic studies of day traders find that the large majority are unprofitable after fees.
    Misconceptions: That day trading is a form of investing or a reliable income source; the SEC states that day traders do not 'invest', typically suffer severe losses in their first months, and should never risk money needed for living expenses or retirement.
    Example: A trader buys 500 shares of XYZ at $41.20 at 10:00 a.m. after a breakout, sells them at $41.75 by 11:30 a.m., and holds no position into the close.
    Aliases: intraday trading

    Swing trading #

    A speculative trading style in which positions are held for one or more days up to several weeks to profit from short- to medium-term price 'swings'. Holding periods sit between day trading (closed intraday) and position trading or buy-and-hold (months to years), and entries/exits are typically driven by technical analysis, sometimes combined with fundamentals.
    Intuition: The swing trader tries to catch the middle chunk of a move that plays out over days, without needing to buy the exact bottom or sell the exact top.
    Mechanics: Traders identify setups (trend continuation, pullbacks to support, breakouts) on daily or intraday charts, enter with predefined stops and targets, and hold overnight, accepting gap risk in exchange for capturing multi-day moves. Rule-based swing systems encode entry/exit as objective technical rules.
    Failure modes: Overnight and weekend gaps can blow through stops; in choppy, trendless markets swing signals whipsaw and repeated small losses accumulate.
    Misconceptions: That swing trading reliably times tops and bottoms, or that widely followed rule-based setups keep their edge; competition and regime change erode simple swing rules.
    Example: A trader buys a stock pulling back to its rising 20-day average at $88, places a stop at $85 and a target at $96, and exits eight trading days later.

    Position trading #

    A trading style in which positions are held for weeks to months (or longer) to capture major price trends, with decisions driven mainly by macro trends, fundamentals, or long-horizon technical analysis. The term is sometimes used interchangeably with buy and hold, though position traders may actively exit when the underlying thesis or long-term trend ends.
    Intuition: Position traders ignore daily noise and try to own an asset for the big part of a long move, reviewing the thesis periodically rather than watching the tape.
    Mechanics: Entries are based on long-term trend signals (e.g., weekly charts, macro or fundamental theses); stops are wide and positions are reviewed weekly or monthly rather than intraday. Transaction costs are low because turnover is low, but capital is exposed to drawdowns over long holding periods.
    Failure modes: Wide stops mean large per-trade losses when a long-term thesis is wrong; secular reversals or value traps can hold capital in losing positions for years.
    Misconceptions: That position trading is identical to passive buy-and-hold indexing; position traders actively select securities and exit when trends or theses fail, whereas buy and hold implies no market timing at all.
    Example: A trader goes long an equity index ETF after a weekly golden cross, holds for nine months through interim corrections, and exits when the weekly trend breaks.
    Aliases: long-term trading

    Trend following #

    A systematic trading approach that takes long positions in instruments whose own recent returns have been positive and short positions in those whose recent returns have been negative, on the hypothesis that price trends persist. It is an empirical strategy family, not a proven predictive law; academic work documents that a security's own past returns positively predict its near-term future returns across many futures markets, with partial reversal at longer horizons.
    Intuition: Rather than predicting turning points, trend followers assume a market that has been going up will keep going up for a while, and they ride it until the trend reverses.
    Mechanics: Signals are computed from past returns or price series (e.g., sign of the trailing 12-month excess return, moving-average filters, channel breakouts); positions are often scaled by inverse volatility and diversified across equity index, bond, currency, and commodity futures. Positions are exited or flipped when the trend signal reverses.
    Failure modes: In range-bound or mean-reverting markets trend signals whipsaw, producing strings of small losses; trend strategies also suffer sharp drawdowns at abrupt trend reversals (e.g., V-shaped recoveries).
    Misconceptions: That trend following predicts where prices will go; it is purely reactive to what prices have done and its documented edge is a statistical regularity that can weaken as it is crowded, not a guarantee.
    Example: A CTA goes long S&P 500 futures because the 12-month return is positive, sizes the position to 10% annualized volatility, and flips short a year later after the trailing return turns negative.
    Position sign s_t = sign(r_{t-12,t}), where r_{t-12,t} is the instrument's excess return over the past 12 months (Moskowitz, Ooi, Pedersen 2012); position size is commonly scaled as s_t / sigma_t with sigma_t an ex-ante volatility estimate.
    Aliases: trend trading, managed futures trend following

    Mean reversion #

    The hypothesis, and the family of strategies built on it, that asset prices or returns contain a transitory component that tends to revert toward a long-run mean, so that extreme moves are at least partially undone. Empirically, stock returns show positive serial correlation at short horizons and negative autocorrelation at long horizons, consistent with a slowly decaying transitory price component, though the effect is statistical and contested, not a law.
    Intuition: What stretches too far from its usual level tends to snap back; mean-reversion traders buy what has fallen too far and sell what has risen too far.
    Mechanics: Traders measure deviation from an estimated mean (moving average, fair-value model, or historical spread) and take positions expecting reversion, exiting when the series returns toward its mean. Statistically, mean reversion is tested with variance-ratio tests or modeled with mean-reverting processes such as Ornstein-Uhlenbeck.
    Failure modes: When the underlying equilibrium shifts (structural breaks, bankruptcies, regime change), 'cheap' assets keep getting cheaper; betting against strong trends produces large losses, and estimated reversion speeds are unstable out of sample.
    Misconceptions: That prices must revert to a historical mean on a predictable schedule, or that mean reversion contradicts momentum; empirically both coexist at different horizons, and apparent reversion can reflect time-varying required returns rather than mispricing.
    Example: A stock drops 12% in two weeks on no fundamental news while its sector is flat; a mean-reversion trader buys, expecting the gap versus its 60-day average to close, and exits after an 8% rebound.
    Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model: dX_t = theta * (mu - X_t) dt + sigma dW_t, where mu is the long-run mean, theta the speed of reversion, and sigma the volatility; variance-ratio test: VR(q) = Var(r_t^q) / (q * Var(r_t)), with VR < 1 indicating mean reversion (Poterba and Summers 1988).
    Aliases: reversal strategy, contrarian trading

    Momentum trading #

    A family of strategies that buy assets with strong recent performance and sell or short assets with weak recent performance, based on the empirically documented pattern that securities outperforming their peers over the past 3 to 12 months tend to keep outperforming over the following month. Momentum is a well-studied cross-sectional anomaly, not a guaranteed law, and is distinct from time-series momentum, which uses an asset's own past return rather than its rank versus peers.
    Intuition: Winners tend to keep winning for a while, so the momentum trader buys what is already strong and avoids or shorts what is weak.
    Mechanics: Universe securities are ranked on trailing (typically 3- to 12-month) returns, skipping the most recent month to avoid short-term reversal; the portfolio goes long the top decile/quintile and short the bottom, rebalanced periodically. Retail implementations simply buy instruments breaking to new highs or showing high relative strength.
    Failure modes: Momentum crashes occur at sharp market reversals (e.g., 2009), when past losers rebound violently and the short leg surges; crowding and transaction costs also erode the gross anomaly.
    Misconceptions: That momentum means simply chasing whatever is up; systematic momentum is a ranked, hedged, periodically rebalanced portfolio construction, and its long-run returns come with occasional severe crashes.
    Example: Each month a quant ranks 500 stocks on their trailing 6-month return, buys the top 30, shorts the bottom 30, and rebalances monthly.
    Momentum score M_i = R_i(t-12, t-1), the cumulative return of asset i from 12 months to 1 month before formation; portfolios are formed by sorting on M (Jegadeesh and Titman 1993).
    Aliases: relative strength trading, momentum investing

    Breakout trading #

    A technical-analysis trading style that enters a position when price passes through and holds beyond a defined support or resistance level, on the hypothesis that such breakouts mark the early stage of a new trend. It is an analytical framework, not a proven predictive law; broken resistance is often treated as new support and vice versa.
    Intuition: When price finally escapes the ceiling or floor where it has been stuck, breakout traders jump in, betting the escape is the start of a real move.
    Mechanics: Traders mark horizontal support/resistance from prior swing highs and lows, then place buy stops above resistance or sell stops below support, often confirming with volume. Stops are placed back inside the broken level (a breakout that immediately re-enters the range is treated as failed).
    Failure modes: False breakouts are common, especially in low-volume or range-bound conditions: price pokes through the level, triggers entries, then reverses back into the range, stopping out breakout traders repeatedly.
    Misconceptions: That a breakout predicts continued movement with high probability; support/resistance levels are heuristic constructs, and the frequency of failed breakouts means risk control, not prediction, drives any edge.
    Example: A stock has failed three times near $60; a trader places a buy stop at $60.25 with volume confirmation and a protective stop at $58.50 when filled.
    Typical long trigger: enter if P_t > R, where R = max(High_{t-N}, ..., High_{t-1}) over a chosen lookback N; stop placed at or below R after entry.

    Range trading #

    A trading style applied to non-trending markets in which price repeatedly rises off a support level and falls off a resistance level; the trader buys near support and sells or shorts near resistance, expecting the oscillation to persist. It is a technical-analysis heuristic whose validity ends when the range breaks.
    Intuition: If a stock keeps bouncing between the same floor and ceiling, trade the bounce: buy low in the band, sell high in the band.
    Mechanics: Support and resistance are identified from repeated reaction highs/lows; entries are taken near the boundaries with stops just outside the range and targets near the opposite boundary. Oscillators (e.g., stochastic, RSI) are often used to time entries within the range.
    Failure modes: The strategy is destroyed by range breakouts: a position entered at the 'wrong' boundary just before a genuine breakout loses continuously, and repeated range profits can be erased by one failed range.
    Misconceptions: That ranges are stable structures; what looks like a range is often just a pause in a trend, and the edges are fuzzy zones rather than precise prices.
    Example: EUR/USD oscillates between 1.0800 and 1.1000 for two months; a range trader buys near 1.0820 with a stop at 1.0770 and a target at 1.0970.
    Aliases: range-bound trading, channel trading

    Pairs trading #

    A market-neutral strategy that matches two securities whose prices historically move together, then goes long the underperformer and short the outperformer when their prices diverge, profiting if the spread between them reconverges. Gatev, Goetzmann, and Rouwenhorst (2006) formalized the canonical version: stocks are paired by minimum distance between normalized historical prices and traded with a simple divergence/convergence rule.
    Intuition: If two similar companies' prices drift apart, bet they will come back together: buy the laggard, short the leader, and you profit on the gap closing regardless of market direction.
    Mechanics: Pairs are formed from a historical estimation window (e.g., 12 months of normalized prices or a cointegration/hedge-ratio regression); a trade opens when the spread widens beyond a threshold (e.g., two standard deviations) and closes on convergence or at the end of a fixed horizon. Dollar-neutral sizing makes the position roughly insensitive to market moves.
    Failure modes: When the historical co-movement breaks for fundamental reasons (e.g., one firm is deteriorating), the spread widens instead of converging; pairs profits also decayed after the strategy became crowded in the 2000s.
    Misconceptions: That a diverging pair must reconverge because it always did before; co-movement is a statistical association that can break permanently, so divergence can signal a real relative-value change rather than a temporary mispricing.
    Example: Coca-Cola and PepsiCo normalized prices diverge by 2.5 standard deviations; the trader shorts the outperformer and buys the underperformer in equal dollar amounts, exiting when the spread returns to its mean.
    Spread S_t = ln P_A,t - beta * ln P_B,t, where beta is the hedge ratio from regressing A's log price on B's; trade when z_t = (S_t - mu_S) / sigma_S exceeds an entry band (commonly +/-2), exit when z_t crosses 0.
    Aliases: pairs trade, relative-value arbitrage

    Statistical arbitrage #

    A family of quantitative, typically market-neutral strategies that exploit statistical mispricings among large portfolios of related securities, using short-horizon mean reversion and factor models rather than the strict riskless mispricing of classical arbitrage. Pairs trading is the canonical example; profits are expected values over many small trades, not risk-free arbitrage.
    Intuition: Instead of one guaranteed mispricing, stat arb places hundreds of small, hedged bets that each have a slight statistical edge, so the law of large numbers does the work.
    Mechanics: Models estimate expected short-horizon returns from residuals after removing factor exposures; portfolios are built long undervalued and short overvalued securities, neutralized to market and common factors, and turned over quickly. Gatev et al. (2006) link pairs-trading profits to temporary mispricing of close substitutes with a common return factor.
    Failure modes: Model risk and regime shifts: estimated relationships degrade exactly when correlations spike in crises, and deleveraging events (e.g., August 2007 quant unwind) cause many stat-arb books to lose simultaneously.
    Misconceptions: That 'arbitrage' here means riskless profit; statistical arbitrage is a set of probabilistic bets that can and do produce extended losses.
    Example: A fund residuals-neutralizes 2,000 U.S. stocks against sector and style factors, shorts stocks 2 sigma above their model price, buys those 2 sigma below, and rebalances daily.
    Aliases: stat arb, statistical relative-value trading
    Related:

    Convertible arbitrage #

    A market-neutral hedge-fund strategy involving the simultaneous purchase of a convertible security (usually a convertible bond) and the short sale of the same issuer's common stock, designed to exploit inefficient pricing of the convertible relative to the underlying equity. The short stock position is sized to the convertible's equity sensitivity (delta), and the position is dynamically re-hedged.
    Intuition: Buy the convertible bond, short the stock against it: if the bond is cheap relative to the stock-plus-option it embeds, you harvest the difference while staying roughly immune to small stock moves.
    Mechanics: The arbitrageur buys the convertible and shorts a delta-neutral ratio of the underlying shares, capturing the bond's coupon plus the cheapness of the embedded equity option; as the stock moves, the hedge ratio is rebalanced (dynamic delta hedging), and this re-hedging of a long-gamma position adds to returns.
    Failure modes: Credit events hurt both legs at once (e.g., the 2005 GM downgrade: long convertibles lost on credit while short equity rallied); liquidity gaps can also make convertible prices fall faster than the stock hedge gains, as in 1987.
    Misconceptions: That the trade is riskless because it is hedged; it carries credit risk, liquidity risk, and model risk in the delta estimate, and returns depend heavily on cheap financing and borrow availability for the short leg.
    Example: A fund buys $10 million face of a convertible trading below theoretical value and shorts 60,000 shares of the issuer (the delta hedge), earning the coupon and gamma P&L as it re-hedges.
    Shares shorted = delta_cv x conversion ratio x number of bonds, where delta_cv is the sensitivity of the convertible's price to the underlying stock price.
    Aliases: convert arb

    Fixed-income arbitrage #

    A group of market-neutral strategies that exploit pricing differences between related fixed-income securities or contracts, such as swap-spread, yield-curve, mortgage, and cash-futures basis trades. Positions pair long and short exposures in economically similar instruments and bet on the convergence of small yield or price discrepancies, usually with substantial leverage.
    Intuition: Two bonds or rates that should be priced almost identically sometimes are not; fixed-income arbs buy the cheap one, short the rich one, and wait for the tiny gap to close.
    Mechanics: Traders identify rich/cheap points using yield-curve models, financing via repo; typical trades include long Treasury/short swap (swap-spread), butterfly structures on the curve, long MBS hedged with Treasuries or swaps, and long cash bond/short futures (basis). Returns per trade are small, so leverage is high.
    Failure modes: High leverage on small spreads means liquidity and financing shocks force losses: the LTCM collapse (1998) and the March 2020 Treasury basis unwind show convergence trades diverging further before converging, triggering margin spirals.
    Misconceptions: That convergence is guaranteed within a tolerable horizon; 'riskless' relative-value trades can stay mispriced longer than a levered trader can stay solvent.
    Example: A desk buys the cheapest-to-deliver 10-year Treasury, shorts the matched futures contract, finances the bond in repo, and plans to hold the position as the basis converges toward delivery.
    Swap spread = yield to maturity of the Treasury bond minus the fixed rate of the matched-maturity interest-rate swap (sign conventions vary by market).
    Aliases: fixed-income relative value

    Volatility arbitrage #

    A class of option-based relative-value strategies that trade the difference between an option's implied volatility and a forecast of the underlying's future realized volatility, implemented through a delta-neutral portfolio of options and the underlying. The trader treats volatility, rather than price, as the unit of relative value: buying options delta-hedged is a bet that realized volatility will exceed implied, and selling them is the opposite bet.
    Intuition: If the market is pricing options as if the stock will move 10% but you think it will move 20%, you can buy the options, hedge out the direction, and profit from the extra movement itself.
    Mechanics: Forecast realized volatility (e.g., from historical or high-frequency returns), compare with implied volatility backed out of market option prices, then buy underpriced or sell overpriced options and keep the book delta-neutral by trading the underlying. Profit is realized through the continuous re-hedging (gamma scalping) of the position over the option's life.
    Failure modes: It is not true arbitrage: the forecast can be wrong, and volatility spikes correlated across markets (black-swan events) cause losses across a whole book, as with LTCM's short-volatility positions.
    Misconceptions: That delta-hedging removes all risk; a delta-neutral book still carries gamma, vega, theta, and jump risk, and 'buying vol cheap' loses steadily through theta if the realized move never comes.
    Example: A trader buys S&P 500 straddles at 12% implied volatility while forecasting 18% realized, delta-hedging daily; if realized vol comes in near 18%, the re-hedging gains exceed the theta paid.
    Option value C = f(S, sigma, ...); implied volatility sigma_imp solves C_market = f(S, sigma_imp, ...). Trade long vol if sigma_imp < sigma_forecast, short vol if sigma_imp > sigma_forecast.
    Aliases: vol arb

    Dispersion trading #

    An options relative-value strategy that trades the spread between the implied volatility of an index and the implied volatilities of its constituent stocks. The classic construction is short index options (straddles or variance) against long options on the components - a position that profits when realized correlation comes in below the correlation embedded in index option prices.
    Intuition: Index option implied volatility embeds an average pairwise correlation among components; if the stocks move less in lockstep than implied, the index's realized volatility undershoots the volatility of its parts, rewarding the short-index/long-components book.
    Mechanics: Sell index options and buy options on index constituents in vega- or notional-weighted proportions, then delta-hedge both legs. P&L is driven by the gap between realized component volatilities and realized index volatility, which scales with realized correlation.
    Failure modes: A market crash drives realized correlation toward one, producing large losses for short-correlation books; the position also carries net short-volatility exposure, so implied-volatility spikes hurt even without a correlation shock.
    Misconceptions: That dispersion trading is a pure, hedged correlation bet; imperfect baskets and residual vega and skew exposures mean it is short crash risk, not riskless arbitrage.
    Example: A trader sells a 3-month at-the-money index straddle at 18% implied vol and buys straddles on the 20 largest components averaging 28% implied vol, delta-hedged daily; if realized correlation is 0.30 instead of the implied 0.55, index realized vol prints near 14% and the short index leg gains more than the long legs lose.
    sigma_index^2 approx sum(w_i^2 sigma_i^2) + 2 sum_{i<j}(w_i w_j rho_ij sigma_i sigma_j): index variance as a function of component weights w, volatilities sigma, and average correlation rho; dispersion trades short implied rho against expected realized rho.
    Aliases: correlation trading (short correlation), index-dispersion trade
    Related:

    Source: Deng, 'Volatility Dispersion Trading' (SSRN working paper) — Abstract

    Gamma scalping #

    A dynamic-hedging options strategy in which a trader holds a long-gamma options position (such as a straddle) and repeatedly rebalances an offsetting position in the underlying to keep the book delta-neutral, harvesting small gains from realized price oscillations. It monetizes the difference between realized volatility and the implied volatility paid for the options.
    Intuition: Being long gamma means your delta grows when the underlying rises and shrinks when it falls, so re-hedging mechanically forces you to sell high and buy low; if the underlying wiggles more than the implied volatility you paid for, the scalps can outearn the theta decay.
    Mechanics: Buy calls (short underlying) or puts (long underlying) in delta-matched size, then re-hedge as delta changes: sell the underlying as it rises and buy it as it falls. The book is net long gamma and short theta, so it profits only if realized volatility exceeds implied volatility net of hedging costs.
    Failure modes: In quiet markets theta decay bleeds the position faster than scalps accumulate, and frequent re-hedging racks up commissions and slippage that can erase the theoretical volatility edge.
    Misconceptions: That gamma scalping is a directional strategy; it is explicitly non-directional and loses when the underlying trends smoothly without oscillation or sits still.
    Example: Buy 10 calls with delta 0.29 and gamma 0.005 for $12 each and short 290 shares; the stock rises $5, call delta becomes 0.32, so short 30 more shares at $480 and buy them back at $475 - a roughly $150 scalp set against daily theta decay.
    Approximate hedging P&L = 0.5 x Gamma x S^2 x (sigma_realized^2 - sigma_implied^2) x t, minus transaction costs, where Gamma is position gamma and S the underlying price.
    Aliases: gamma hedging, delta-neutral scalping

    Delta-neutral trading #

    A portfolio construction approach that offsets positive and negative deltas so the combined position has near-zero sensitivity to small moves in the underlying's price. Directional risk is neutralized so that other exposures - volatility, time decay, or carry - drive the P&L.
    Intuition: If an option behaves like 40 shares, holding 40 offsetting shares cancels first-order price risk; what remains is the curvature (gamma), the clock (theta), and the volatility exposure (vega).
    Mechanics: Compute the net delta of all legs (option deltas summed per contract times multiplier; stock counts one per share) and trade the underlying to bring net delta to zero, rebalancing as prices, time, and implied volatility shift the Greeks.
    Failure modes: Delta neutrality holds only for small moves: large gaps or volatility spikes change delta faster than rebalancing can track, and a perfectly delta-neutral book can still lose heavily through vega, theta, and funding costs.
    Misconceptions: That delta-neutral means risk-free; it only neutralizes first-order directional exposure, leaving convexity, volatility, and liquidity risks fully in place.
    Example: Long 10 puts with delta -0.31 plus long 310 shares gives net delta near zero; after a $1.24 drop the put delta moves to -0.43, so the trader buys 120 more shares to re-neutralize.
    Delta_portfolio = sum(n_i x Delta_i) = 0, where n_i is the number of contracts or shares and Delta_i the per-unit delta of each leg.
    Aliases: delta-neutral hedging, delta hedging

    Market making #

    A liquidity-provision activity in which a firm continuously quotes two-sided bid and ask prices, earning the spread (and any rebates) on turnover while managing the inventory risk of positions accumulated from filling customer orders. In U.S. equities, registered and off-exchange market makers also internalize a large share of retail order flow under Regulation NMS execution and disclosure rules.
    Intuition: The market maker is a dealer, not an investor: it tries to buy at the bid and sell at the offer thousands of times a day, and its enemy is holding inventory when prices move against it.
    Mechanics: Post limit orders on both sides around a fair-value estimate, adjust quotes as prices and inventory change, and hedge or internalize the resulting flow. The SEC's 2021 staff report describes how a small number of off-exchange market makers execute most individual-investor orders, with payment for order flow creating conflicts governed by best-execution and Rule 605/606 disclosure regimes.
    Failure modes: Adverse selection - being run over by better-informed or faster flow - and inventory losses in one-way markets are the core failure modes; the January 2021 meme-stock episode forced extreme inventory and margin stress on off-exchange market makers.
    Misconceptions: That market making is riskless spread capture; losses on individual trades are routine and must be covered by aggregate spread income, and the economics depend on speed, rebates, and flow access unavailable to retail traders.
    Example: A market maker quotes a stock $50.00 bid / $50.02 offered, 1,000 shares up; it buys 500 at the bid and sells 500 at the offer for $10 gross, then hedges residual inventory when a news release moves fair value to $49.90.
    Aliases: dealing, liquidity provision

    Grid trading #

    A systematic order-placement technique that lays a ladder of buy and sell orders at fixed price intervals above and below a reference price, harvesting oscillation or trend mechanically without forecasting direction. With-the-trend grids add exposure as price runs; against-the-trend (range) grids buy lower rungs and sell higher rungs to monetize mean reversion.
    Intuition: Instead of predicting where price will go, the grid turns the price axis into a conveyor belt: each step down triggers a buy and each step back up triggers a sell at the next rung, locking the spacing in as gross profit per completed cycle.
    Mechanics: Choose a base price, an interval (e.g., 10 pips), and a number of rungs, then place resting orders at each level. Range grids place buys below and sells above the base; trend grids do the reverse. Risk control requires a hard stop beyond the outermost rung, because position size grows against an adverse trend.
    Failure modes: A sustained trend through the grid accumulates a large losing position (range grid) or forces profit give-back (trend grid); without a stop, a one-way move can exceed the sum of all harvested oscillation profits many times over.
    Misconceptions: That grid trading is a low-risk money machine because it wins frequently; the many small wins are paid for with an unbounded tail loss when price leaves the grid and never returns.
    Example: With EURUSD at 1.1450, place buys every 10 pips down to 1.1390 and sells every 10 pips up to 1.1510 with a stop at 1.1530; each completed down-up oscillation earns 10 pips per unit, while a run to 1.1530 stops the grid for a 270-pip worst case.
    Gross profit per completed grid cycle approx. grid interval x size per rung; with n rungs at spacing s around base P, orders sit at P +/- k*s for k = 1..n.
    Aliases: grid strategy, grid bot trading

    Dollar-cost averaging #

    An accumulation strategy that invests a fixed dollar amount into an asset at regular intervals regardless of price, so that more units are bought when prices are low and fewer when prices are high. It replaces market-timing decisions with a mechanical purchase schedule.
    Intuition: A fixed $500 buys twice as many shares at $10 as at $20, so the average cost per share ends up below the average price over the period - the schedule does the buying-low work automatically.
    Mechanics: Divide the planned investment into equal tranches and deploy them on a fixed calendar (weekly, monthly, quarterly). Regulator education stresses that DCA changes when and how money is invested; it does not prevent losses or guarantee returns.
    Failure modes: In a steadily rising market DCA underperforms investing the lump sum up front because cash sits idle, and in a secular decline it systematically buys into losses; frequent small purchases can also carry proportionally high fees.
    Misconceptions: That DCA is a timing strategy that beats the market or removes risk; its benefit is behavioral discipline and timing-risk smoothing, and it still loses money in sustained downturns.
    Example: Investing $250 weekly into an ETF priced at $99, $96, $97, and $100 produces an average share cost of about $98.00, versus $99.00 for a week-one lump-sum purchase (CIRO illustration).
    Average cost per share = n x A / sum(A / P_i) = n / sum(1 / P_i), the harmonic mean of purchase prices P_i for fixed amount A per period.
    Aliases: DCA, constant dollar plan

    Value averaging #

    An accumulation strategy, introduced by Michael Edleson in a 1988 AAII Journal article, in which the investor targets a fixed increase in the portfolio's total value each period (the value path) and invests or sells whatever amount is required to hit that target. Purchases are larger after declines and smaller - or negative - after rallies.
    Intuition: Dollar-cost averaging fixes the dollars you invest; value averaging fixes the growth of your portfolio's value, so the market itself dictates each installment - more when it is cheap, less, or even a sale, when it is rich.
    Mechanics: Set a target value for each period (e.g., initial amount plus $500 per month). At each rebalancing, compute the gap between the current holdings value and the target, then buy the shortfall or sell the excess. Required cash flows are unbounded, so reserves must be planned.
    Failure modes: Deep bear markets demand ever-larger purchases that can exceed available cash, and in taxable accounts the forced sales trigger capital-gains tax; the strategy also requires more monitoring and transactions than DCA.
    Misconceptions: That value averaging is strictly superior to DCA; its lower average cost comes from greater aggressiveness (larger investments after losses), which is a risk choice rather than a free lunch.
    Example: With a $500 monthly value path, a rally that lifts holdings $840 above target prompts a sale of about 7.3 shares ($340) instead of a purchase; a drop from $49.74 to $45.50 forces a $1,182 investment (AAII worked example).
    Investment at period t = V_target(t) - (shares held x price_t), with V_target(t) = V_0 + t x deltaV for a linear value path.
    Aliases: VA, Edleson value averaging

    Pyramiding #

    A position-building tactic that adds to a winning trade in predefined increments as price moves in the position's favor, so that full size is carried only after the trade has proven itself. In trend systems the adds are mechanical - the Turtle rule adds one unit every 0.5N (half the average true range) above the previous fill.
    Intuition: Start small, let the market pay for confirmation, then press: each add is effectively financed by open profit, while stops trail so that the worst case on the full pyramid approximates the risk of the first unit.
    Mechanics: Define the initial unit, the add interval, and the maximum unit count before entry. Turtle construction: enter one unit at the breakout, add one unit per 0.5N advance up to four units, and raise stops on earlier units so total position risk stays near 2% of equity.
    Failure modes: Late adds sit close to the reversal point, so a sharp give-back can turn a comfortably profitable trade into a scratch or a loss on the final units; in choppy, mean-reverting markets pyramids repeatedly stop out near the top of the range.
    Misconceptions: That pyramiding is the same as averaging down; pyramiding adds only to winners, while averaging down adds to losers - the two have opposite risk dynamics.
    Example: With N = 2.50 and a 55-day breakout at 310.00, buy units at 310.00, 311.25, 312.50, and 313.75, trailing the stop to 2N below the most recent fill (Turtle worked example).
    Add price(k) = fill(k-1) + 0.5 x N for longs, where N is the 20-day exponential average true range; maximum four units in the Turtle system.
    Aliases: adding to winners, anti-martingale sizing

    Averaging down #

    Adding to an existing long position as its price falls, which lowers the average cost per share and reduces the rebound needed to break even. It is a position-sizing practice rather than a complete strategy, most defensible within a valuation framework and strict risk limits.
    Intuition: Buying 100 shares at $50 and 100 more at $40 puts the cost basis at $45, so the position needs only a 12.5% recovery from $40 instead of a 25% one - but the investor now owns twice as much of a falling asset.
    Mechanics: Purchase additional shares at successively lower prices per a predetermined plan (fixed increments or valuation triggers). The new average cost is the share-weighted mean of purchase prices, and position size and portfolio concentration grow with each add.
    Failure modes: If the decline reflects deteriorating fundamentals rather than noise, averaging down concentrates the portfolio in a loser - the classic falling-knife path to outsized single-name losses.
    Misconceptions: That a lower average cost means lower risk; the break-even math improves but total capital at risk rises with every add, and the practice is often used to avoid admitting a mistake rather than as a rational plan.
    Example: 100 shares at $60 plus 100 more at $40 gives a $50 basis: the position needs only a 25% rise from $40 to profit instead of 50% - but $14,000 is now exposed to one stock.
    Average cost = sum(q_i x p_i) / sum(q_i) over all purchases i.
    Aliases: buying the dip, adding to a loser

    Averaging up #

    Buying additional shares of an existing position at successively higher prices, raising the average cost basis while increasing exposure to a rising asset. It is the mirror of averaging down and the discrete-time cousin of pyramiding into strength.
    Intuition: Leaning into winners trades a worse average price for a bigger position in a confirmed trend - you pay up precisely because the market is validating the thesis.
    Mechanics: Add tranches at predetermined higher price points or on technical triggers such as moving-average breaks or momentum readings. Each add raises the cost basis, so the position becomes increasingly sensitive to a reversal from the peak.
    Failure modes: Buying into late-stage momentum risks adding just before exhaustion; a sharp reversal puts the later, higher-priced tranches underwater immediately, and rising concentration compounds the loss.
    Misconceptions: That averaging up is always reckless; disciplined versions predetermine add points and total size, whereas the undisciplined version is simply chasing. It is also distinct from dollar-cost averaging, which is price-agnostic rather than momentum-following.
    Example: Buying XYZ at $20 and then equal amounts at $24, $28, and $32 yields a $26 basis; a drop to $25 loses money on the whole position even though the first tranche is still up 25%.
    Average cost = sum(q_i x p_i) / sum(q_i); equal buys at $20, $24, $28, and $32 give a $26 basis.
    Aliases: adding to strength, leaning into winners

    Scaling in #

    Building a position in multiple tranches at different prices instead of committing full size at once - either into weakness (lowering average cost) or into strength (adding as the trade proves itself). It spreads execution and timing risk and lets the trader confirm the thesis before full deployment.
    Intuition: Splitting a 1,000-share buy into four 250-share steps at descending prices turns one all-or-nothing decision into a graduated one, at the cost of sometimes never reaching full size.
    Mechanics: Set a target price and total size, then place partial orders at intervals, stopping when price reverses or full size is reached. Scaling in also reduces market impact and helps hide large orders; many traders start small and add only as the trade moves in their favor.
    Failure modes: Scaling into weakness means deliberately growing a losing position; if the thesis is wrong, the trader reaches maximum size at the point of maximum loss.
    Misconceptions: That scaling in always improves the entry; if price runs without filling the remaining rungs, the trader is underinvested in their best ideas - opportunity cost is the price of the smoother entry.
    Example: An investor wanting 1,000 shares of a $20 stock buys 250 shares each at $20.00, $19.90, $19.80, and $19.70, averaging $19.85 instead of $20.00 (Investopedia worked example).
    Average entry = sum(q_i x p_i) / sum(q_i); four 250-share tranches at $20.00, $19.90, $19.80, $19.70 average $19.85.
    Aliases: scale in, graduated entry
