    Scaling out #

    Closing a position in partial tranches at successively more favorable prices rather than exiting all at once, banking profits progressively while leaving a residual position to capture further movement.
    Intuition: Selling a third at each target converts an all-or-nothing exit into a ladder: some profit is locked in, and the remaining runner is effectively financed by realized gains.
    Mechanics: Predefine partial-exit levels or targets; as each is reached, close a fraction of the position and commonly move the stop on the remainder to break-even or better, so the residual position carries little remaining downside relative to the entry.
    Failure modes: In strong trends scaling out caps participation in the biggest moves - the residual runner earns a fraction of what full size would have; in choppy markets premature partials plus tightened stops can exit the whole position at the worst moments.
    Misconceptions: That scaling out is always the prudent choice; its main documented benefit is psychological and risk-smoothing, and fixed full exits can outperform staggered ones when trends persist.
    Example: Holding 900 shares from a $50 entry, sell 300 at $52 and 300 at $54, move the stop to $50, and let the last 300 run - $1,200 is banked while upside exposure remains.
    Remaining size after k exits = Q_0 - sum(q_i); realized P&L = sum(q_i x (p_i - p_entry)) over exited tranches i.
    Aliases: scale out, partial profit-taking

    Core-satellite approach #

    A portfolio-construction framework that places the majority of assets in a low-cost, passively managed core tracking broad market indexes, surrounded by smaller actively managed satellite positions intended to add alpha or diversification. It blends passive efficiency with bounded active risk.
    Intuition: Lock in the market return on most of the money and confine your bets to a satellite sleeve, where manager skill or concentrated themes can only help or hurt at the margin.
    Mechanics: Allocate the core to index funds or ETFs (e.g., an S&P 500 tracker) and the satellites to active funds, sectors, or strategies (high-yield bonds, biotech, commodities), then rebalance to hold the split. The core minimizes costs and portfolio beta while satellites carry the tracking error.
    Failure modes: Poor satellite selection or an excessive satellite weight erodes the cost and risk benefits, effectively recreating an expensive active portfolio; persistent active underperformance drags total return below the cheap index the core was meant to secure.
    Misconceptions: That the framework itself generates outperformance; it is an organizational discipline - the satellites still must earn their fees, and most active managers do not beat their benchmarks net of costs.
    Example: 50% in an S&P 500 index fund as the core, with 10% in an actively managed high-yield bond fund, 20% in a biotechnology fund, and 20% in a commodities fund as satellites (Investopedia illustration).
    Aliases: core-satellite investing, core and explore
    Related:
    • Buy and hold
    • Index investing
    • Asset allocation
    • Alpha
    Source: Investopedia, 'Core-Satellite Investing Strategy' — Building Your Core-Satellite Portfolio

    Buy and hold #

    A passive investment strategy of purchasing securities - typically diversified equity funds - and holding them for years regardless of short-term price fluctuations, forgoing timing decisions in favor of long-run participation in asset growth. Positions are selected once and traded only for rebalancing, contributions, or withdrawals.
    Intuition: If you cannot reliably predict next month's price, own productive assets and let compounding and time in the market do the work; every round trip avoided is costs and taxes not paid.
    Mechanics: Acquire the chosen assets and hold through bull and bear markets; capital-gains taxes are deferred until sale, and turnover and transaction costs approach zero. Indexed implementations hold constituents as long as they remain in the index, with annual turnover often under 5%.
    Failure modes: Secular bear markets or buying at extreme valuations can produce a decade or more of flat real returns, and behavioral failure - panic selling at the bottom - converts a temporary drawdown into a permanent loss.
    Misconceptions: That buy and hold means never reassessing; the strategy concerns trading frequency, not due diligence - holdings still require periodic review for thesis or allocation drift, and long-run equity outperformance is a historical pattern, not a law.
    Example: 100 shares of Apple bought at $18 in January 2008 and held to January 2019 (then $157) returned nearly 900% over 11 years without a single trade (Investopedia example).
    Aliases: long-term passive investing, buy-and-hold investing

    Carry trade #

    A strategy of funding in a low-interest-rate currency (or asset) and investing in a high-interest-rate one to earn the interest differential, in violation of the uncovered-interest-parity benchmark that predicts offsetting depreciation. Its return profile is short-volatility: steady differential income punctuated by crash risk on the investment currency.
    Intuition: Borrow yen at 0.5%, buy Australian dollars at 4.5%, and pocket the four-point spread - as long as AUD does not slide; the trade earns until everyone unwinds at once, which is why it goes up by the stairs and down by the elevator.
    Mechanics: Sell or borrow the funding currency and buy the investment currency via spot, forwards, or futures, holding the differential as carry. Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen (2009) document that high interest differentials predict negative skewness: crashes cluster with funding-liquidity squeezes and VIX spikes, when speculators unwind positions.
    Failure modes: Funding-liquidity spirals force synchronized unwinds - investment currencies crash precisely when speculators must delever (e.g., the October 1998 dollar-yen collapse); rising global risk aversion (VIX, TED spread) is the canonical kill switch.
    Misconceptions: That the carry differential is riskless arbitrage; the average profit coexists with negative skewness, so the return is compensation for bearing crash risk rather than a free lunch.
    Example: A position long AUD funded in USD earned about the 0.6% quarterly differential plus 0.3% FX excess return in the 1986-2006 sample, while carrying average daily-return skewness around -0.32 (Brunnermeier-Nagel-Pedersen summary statistics).
    Carry return z(t+1) = (i*_t - i_t) - delta_s(t+1), where i* - i is the interest differential and delta_s the depreciation of the investment currency; profit requires the differential to exceed depreciation.
    Aliases: currency carry, forward-bias trade
    Related:

    Source: Brunnermeier, Nagel & Pedersen, 'Carry Trades and Currency Crashes', NBER Macroeconomics Annual 2008, vol. 23 — Sections I, III-IV (pp. 313-347)

    Basis trade #

    A relative-value strategy that trades the basis - the spread between an asset's cash (spot) price and its futures price - by going long the cheap leg and short the rich leg, expecting convergence as the futures contract approaches delivery. The position is neutral to the asset's outright price and earns or loses the change in the spread.
    Intuition: A futures contract and the deliverable asset are the same thing at expiry, so any gap between them today beyond financing and carry is a harvestable mispricing: buy the cheap one, sell the dear one, and wait for them to meet.
    Mechanics: Compute fair value (spot plus cost of carry minus income such as dividends) and compare it with the futures price; if futures are rich, buy the asset and short the futures (long the basis), financing the cash leg, then hold to convergence or roll. Index-arbitrage desks apply the same logic to index futures versus the constituent basket.
    Failure modes: The basis can widen before it converges, forcing margin and financing stress on the leveraged position - the archetypal limit-to-arbitrage failure; funding-cost spikes and forced unwinds (March 2020 Treasuries) can turn a convergent trade into a large realized loss.
    Misconceptions: That basis trades are riskless arbitrage; convergence is only assured at delivery, and the leverage used to make thin spreads economical makes interim widening potentially fatal.
    Example: With the S&P future trading 8 points above fair value, a desk buys the 500-stock basket and shorts the future; as expiry approaches, the two converge and the desk captures the 8 points less financing and execution costs.
    Basis = futures price - spot price; fair value = cash x (1 + r x x/360) - dividends, so the tradeable edge approx. actual basis minus theoretical basis.
    Aliases: basis trading, cash-and-carry (one direction)

    Cash-futures basis trade #

    The Treasury-market instantiation of the basis trade: arbitrageurs, chiefly hedge funds, buy cash Treasury notes and bonds and short Treasury futures when futures trade above their no-arbitrage price, financing the bond in the repo market so the position requires almost no net cash outlay. Profit comes from cash and futures prices converging by the delivery date.
    Intuition: If the future is priced above what the deliverable bond plus financing costs, you can warehouse the bond, deliver into the future, and lock in the gap - as long as your repo funding survives until delivery.
    Mechanics: Identify the cheapest-to-deliver Treasury, buy it, short the corresponding CBOT futures contract, and finance the bond with overnight or short-term repo while posting futures margin. The Office of Financial Research estimates hedge-fund basis positions peaked around $400-500 billion before March 2020, when margin and repo stress forced partial unwinds.
    Failure modes: Overnight repo creates rollover risk and futures margin creates cash-call risk; in March 2020 rising margins and repo costs forced hedge funds to sell the cash leg into an illiquid market, amplifying Treasury-market stress until the Federal Reserve intervened.
    Misconceptions: That the high leverage makes the trade speculative rather than arbitrage; the position is hedged against rate moves, and its fragility comes from funding structure, not directional exposure.
    Example: From end-2017 to September 2019 hedge-fund Treasury exposure roughly doubled to $2.02 trillion while short futures positions rose $352 billion - the footprint of the long-basis build-up that partially unwound in March 2020 (OFR estimates).
    No-arbitrage condition: P(t,tau) = sum_s B(t,s) c_s + B(t,T) F(t,tau,T) - the cash bond price equals discounted coupons plus the discounted futures invoice price; violations define the tradeable disconnect.
    Aliases: Treasury basis trade
    Related:

    Funding-rate arbitrage #

    A crypto-market cash-and-carry style strategy that pairs a position in a perpetual futures contract with the opposite position in spot to harvest the funding rate - the periodic payment exchanged between perp longs and shorts that tethers the contract to the index price. When funding is positive, the classic trade shorts the perp and holds spot, collecting funding from longs.
    Intuition: Perpetual futures never expire, so exchanges tax the crowded side: when longs pay shorts 0.01% every eight hours, being short the perp and long the coin collects the toll while the two legs' price risk cancels.
    Mechanics: Hold spot (or a margin-equivalent) against an equal-notional short perp; funding payments accrue to the short when the rate is positive (contango) and to the long when negative (backwardation), typically every eight hours. The position is directionally hedged; risks are funding-rate flips, borrow and margin costs, liquidation mechanics, and exchange counterparty risk.
    Failure modes: Funding flips sign in sharp selloffs, turning the income stream into a cost, and adverse wicks can liquidate the leveraged leg before the spot leg can be unwound, breaking the hedge; exchange freezes or defaults are uncompensated tail risks.
    Misconceptions: That it is risk-free yield; the hedge is imperfect under liquidation and margin rules, and high positive funding usually signals crowded leverage that unwinds violently.
    Example: With BTC perp funding at +0.01% per 8 hours, short $100,000 of perp against $100,000 of spot and collect about $30 per day in funding while the legs' price P&L nets to zero - until funding flips negative.
    Funding payment per interval = position notional x funding rate; a $10,000 position at +0.01% pays or receives $1 every 8 hours.
    Aliases: funding-rate carry, perp cash-and-carry
    Related:
    • Basis trade
    • Cash-futures basis trade
    • Perpetual futures
    • Cross-exchange arbitrage

    Cross-exchange arbitrage #

    Buying an asset on the exchange where it is cheaper and simultaneously selling it where it is dearer to capture the price gap between venues. In cryptocurrency markets, Makarov and Schoar (2020) document that such deviations are recurrent and much larger across countries than within them, reflecting capital controls and limits to arbitrage.
    Intuition: The same bitcoin should not be worth $30,000 in Seoul and $29,400 in New York for long - but moving dollars and coins across borders and exchanges is slow and costly, so the gap persists and pays whoever can bridge it.
    Mechanics: Monitor quoted prices across venues, buy on the cheap exchange and sell on the expensive one, then rebalance fiat and asset inventories between venues. Speed, transfer costs, withdrawal limits, and capital controls determine which gaps are actually capturable.
    Failure modes: Transfer and settlement delays mean the legs are not simultaneous in practice - the gap can close while assets are in transit, and withdrawal halts, capital controls, or counterparty failure can strand one leg entirely.
    Misconceptions: That a visible price gap equals capturable profit; the documented gaps persist precisely because frictions - capital controls and slow-moving arbitrage capital - prevent most traders from closing them.
    Example: Bitcoin trades $500 higher on a Korean exchange than a U.S. one; an arbitrageur buys in the U.S. and sells in Korea but captures the spread only after moving won proceeds back through the banking system - days of exposure, not milliseconds.
    Gross edge = P_venueB - P_venueA - (transfer + trading + FX costs); Makarov-Schoar find cross-country deviations co-move and widen during large bitcoin appreciations.
    Aliases: exchange arbitrage, kimchi premium trade (crypto context)
    Related:

    • Funding-rate arbitrage
    • Index arbitrage
    • Limits to arbitrage
    Source: Makarov & Schoar, 'Trading and Arbitrage in Cryptocurrency Markets' (SSRN; published JFE 2020) — Abstract

    Latency arbitrage #

    A high-frequency strategy that exploits the mechanical arbitrage opportunities created when correlated securities' prices update at slightly different times across venues, racing to trade on a stale quote before its owner can update it. Budish, Cramton and Shim (2015) show this arms race is built into the continuous limit order book design itself.
    Intuition: When the E-mini jumps in Chicago, the SPY quote in New York is wrong for a few milliseconds; being first to pick off that stale quote is worth millions a year, so firms spend hundreds of millions on being microseconds faster.
    Mechanics: Colocate near exchange matching engines, consume direct feeds, and send orders against quotes that have not yet incorporated price moves in correlated instruments. BCS document ES-SPY correlation falling to about 0.09 at 10 milliseconds and 0.007 at 1 millisecond, with the median arbitrage duration shrinking from 97ms in 2005 to 7ms in 2011 while profit per opportunity stayed roughly constant.
    Failure modes: The rent is competed away in speed rather than in price: as rivals get faster, capture requires ever-larger infrastructure spending for the same prize, and market-design changes such as frequent batch auctions or speed bumps can eliminate the opportunity set outright.
    Misconceptions: That latency arbitrage involves predicting prices; it exploits purely mechanical, publicly visible mispricings whose existence depends on serial processing in continuous markets, not on information or forecasting skill.
    Example: At 1:51:39.590 pm the E-mini jumps about 2.5 index points while SPY has not yet reacted; the fastest firm buys SPY at the stale offer and unwinds moments later at the corrected price, capturing the spread (BCS Figure 1.1 example).
    Median ES-SPY arbitrage profit approx. 0.08 index points per unit, about 1,000 opportunities per day (roughly $75 million per year) in the BCS 2005-2011 sample.
    Aliases: speed arbitrage, stale-quote sniping
    Related:
    • Scalping
    • Market making
    • Cross-exchange arbitrage
    • High-frequency trading
    Source: Budish, Cramton & Shim, 'The High-Frequency Trading Arms Race: Frequent Batch Auctions as a Market Design Response' (NBER version; published QJE 2015) — Introduction; Sections 4-5

    Index arbitrage #

    A strategy that exploits deviations between the price of an index futures contract (or ETF) and the theoretical fair value of the underlying constituent basket, buying the cheaper side and selling the richer. It is the engine of classic program trading and enforces the law of one price between index derivatives and their components.
    Intuition: The S&P 500 future is just the 500 stocks in a wrapper; if the wrapper trades rich to the contents, sell the wrapper and buy the contents - computers do this in milliseconds, which is why the gap rarely exceeds transaction costs.
    Mechanics: Compute fair value = cash index x (1 + r x x/360) - dividends and compare it with the futures price; beyond a threshold set by round-trip costs, execute the basket and the futures as a unit. Opportunities are fleeting and razor-thin, so the strategy is dominated by institutions with high-speed execution and near-zero costs.
    Failure modes: Execution-leg risk: a 500-stock basket cannot be filled at the quoted snapshot, so slippage can consume the edge; in dislocations (e.g., August 24, 2015) constituent and ETF prices detach violently, and part of the apparent gap is unpriceable, illiquid components.
    Misconceptions: That index arbitrage is accessible to retail traders; the capital, speed, and cost structure needed to profit from sub-0.1% discrepancies are institutional by construction.
    Example: If S&P 500 futures trade 0.2% above fair value, an institution buys all 500 stocks in index weights and shorts the equivalent futures, unwinding both at convergence for the spread minus financing and costs.
    Fair value = cash x {1 + r(x/360)} - dividends; trade triggers when |futures - fair value| > round-trip transaction costs.
    Aliases: index arb, program trading arbitrage
    Related:
    • Basis trade
    • Program trading
    • ETF arbitrage
    • Latency arbitrage
    Source: Investopedia, 'Index Arbitrage' — How Index Arbitrage Works; Real-World Examples

    Calendar arbitrage #

    Trading the price relationship between futures (or options) on the same underlying with different expiration months - the intra-commodity calendar spread - by buying one expiry and selling another to capture changes in the term-structure gap rather than the outright price. Framed as arbitrage, it targets spreads that deviate from carry-implied or historical norms.
    Intuition: March wheat and September wheat are the same wheat at different dates; the gap between them reflects storage, financing, and seasonal demand - trade the gap when it stretches beyond what carry justifies.
    Mechanics: Buy the nearby (or deferred) month and sell the other as a single unit with two legs; P&L equals the change in the inter-month spread. Exchanges grant reduced margins on calendar spreads because systematic market risk largely nets out between the legs.
    Failure modes: Spreads are not bounded by delivery the way cash-futures convergence is: supply shocks, delivery squeezes in the nearby month, or storage-regime changes can run the spread far past fair carry, and liquidity in the deferred leg can be thin.
    Misconceptions: That calendar spreads are slow and safe; nearby-month squeezes can move the spread more violently than the outright market, and the arbitrage framing is relative-value, not riskless.
    Example: Buy March wheat at 526'6 and sell September at 537'6 (spread -11'0); when the spread narrows to -8'0, the 3-cent gain on the 5,000-bushel contract is $150 (Investopedia worked example).
    Spread P&L = (P_near,exit - P_near,entry) - (P_far,exit - P_far,entry); a spread narrowing from -11 to -8 cents on a 5,000-bushel wheat contract earns $150.
    Aliases: calendar spread trading, intra-commodity spread
    Related:

    • Futures curve
    • Roll yield
    • Contango and backwardation
    Source: Investopedia, 'Futures Spread' — Intra-Commodity Calendar Spread; Real-World Example

    News trading #

    A short-horizon style that trades the price impact of scheduled announcements (earnings, central-bank decisions, economic data) and unscheduled events, entering just before or immediately after the release to capture the volatility burst. Most news traders operate intraday, since the exploitable reaction is typically short-lived.
    Intuition: News is the one scheduled source of volatility: everyone knows when the payrolls print lands, nobody knows its content - the game is positioning for, reacting to, or fading the crowd's reaction better than the crowd.
    Mechanics: Prepare scenarios and alerts around the economic calendar; trade the initial directional reaction, the buy-the-rumor-sell-the-news unwind, or the fade once enthusiasm peaks. Stops must accommodate post-release volatility, and spread widening and slippage around releases are structural costs.
    Failure modes: Spreads widen and liquidity vanishes exactly at the release, so theoretical fills are unavailable, and algorithmic reaction times leave discretionary traders structurally late; fading a genuinely regime-changing headline is a standing source of large losses.
    Misconceptions: That knowing the news content is the edge; prices react to the surprise relative to expectations, not the headline itself - a good number can sell the asset off if it was not good enough.
    Example: A trader shorts EUR/USD strength minutes after a payrolls beat that fell short of the whisper number, covering an hour later as the initial dollar spike fades.
    Aliases: trading the news, event-driven day trading
    Related:
    • Earnings trading
    • Fade strategy
    • Gap-and-go strategy
    • Day trading
    Source: Investopedia, 'News Trader' — How News Traders Operate in the Market

    Earnings trading #

    Trading equity positions around quarterly earnings announcements to exploit the announcement-day volatility and the post-announcement price drift documented in the accounting literature. Implementations range from intraday reaction trades to holding surprise winners and losers for the multi-week drift.
    Intuition: Earnings are scheduled surprises: prices jump at the print and then, on average, keep crawling in the direction of the surprise for weeks, as if the market digests the news only gradually.
    Mechanics: Screen for upcoming reports, estimate the surprise (actual versus consensus), and trade the direction of the surprise or the subsequent drift. Bernard and Thomas (1989) document post-earnings-announcement drift: abnormal returns continue in the direction of the surprise for roughly 60 days, a pattern they find consistent with delayed price response rather than risk mismeasurement.
    Failure modes: Announcement windows concentrate gap risk - positions held through the print face binary moves that jump stops - and crowding plus post-publication decay of the drift anomaly compress the available edge.
    Misconceptions: That earnings trading is just guessing the number; systematic versions trade the market's reaction function (surprise to drift), not the fundamental outcome itself.
    Example: A stock reports EPS 15% above consensus and gaps up 6% at the open; a drift-following position held for the following weeks targets the continuation documented beyond the initial jump.
    Surprise proxies include SUE = (EPS_actual - EPS_expected) / sigma(forecast errors); portfolios sorted on SUE show drift in cumulative abnormal returns over the following quarter.
    Aliases: earnings momentum trading, trading earnings announcements
    Related:
    • News trading
    • Post-earnings-announcement drift
    • Gap-and-go strategy
    • Earnings surprise
    Source: Bernard & Thomas, 'Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift: Delayed Price Response or Risk Premium?', Journal of Accounting Research 27 (1989), 1-36 (EconPapers record) — Bibliographic record (DOI 10.2307/2491062)

    Opening-range breakout #

    An intraday strategy that marks the high and low of the session's first minutes (commonly the first 15) and enters in the direction of a break beyond that range, treating the opening battle as the day's reference for momentum. It is a named day-trading system without a canonical academic source.
    Intuition: The first 15 minutes concentrate overnight information and the day's heaviest volume; whichever side wins that skirmish often keeps control, so the range edges become the day's natural trigger levels.
    Mechanics: Mark the opening-range high and low; enter long on a break above the high or short below the low, with stops inside the range or below the breakout candle and targets as multiples of risk or of the range width. The day's high or low is printed in the opening minutes far more often than a random walk would imply.
    Failure modes: False breakouts in mean-reverting sessions stop the trade repeatedly, and on low-conviction range-bound days the OR edges are noise rather than information; slippage at the open is structurally high.
    Misconceptions: That every opening-range break is tradable; practitioners filter by gap context, relative volume, and higher-timeframe trend - the raw breakout is an analytical framework, not a validated edge.
    Example: With a stock's 15-minute opening range at $41.08-$41.65, a break above $41.65 at 9:55 a.m. triggers a long with a stop below $41.08 and a first target near $42.80 (about 2R).
    Long trigger: price > opening-range high; risk unit = OR high minus stop; a common first target is entry + 2 x risk unit.
    Aliases: ORB, opening range breakout (ORB)
    Related:
    • Breakout trading
    • Gap-and-go strategy
    • Day trading
    • News trading
    Source: Investopedia, 'Guide to Using the Opening Range in Trading' — How to Trade Effectively Using the Opening Range

    Gap-and-go strategy #

    A day-trading momentum strategy that buys (or shorts) a stock that gaps at the open on high volume and continues trending in the gap direction without a meaningful pullback, entering on continuation beyond the opening range. It is a named practitioner setup with no canonical source.
    Intuition: A high-volume gap means overnight information repriced the stock and trapped the other side; if price never looks back, the trapped traders' exits and momentum chasers feed a one-way move you ride with tight invalidation.
    Mechanics: Screen for gaps with elevated premarket and opening volume; enter on the break of the opening range in the gap direction, place the stop at the prior session's close or the gap's far edge, and trail the exit with a trend line or the most recent swing low.
    Failure modes: The setup's evil twin is the gap fade: gaps that stall and fill trap momentum entries at the day's extreme, and wide opening spreads plus slippage make realized entries far worse than chart-based backtests suggest.
    Misconceptions: That the gap direction is itself the signal; practitioners require volume confirmation and continuation structure - many gaps fill, and trading every gap is an exercise in chasing.
    Example: A stock gaps up 8% on earnings with triple normal opening volume; the trader buys the break of the first 5-minute high, stops below the prior close, and exits when the rising trend line from the open is breached near midday.
    Aliases: gap and go
    Related:
    • Opening-range breakout
    • Momentum trading
    • News trading
    • Earnings trading
    Source: TradingSim, 'Top 3 Morning Gap Trading Setups' — #1 - Gap and Go Strategy

    Fade strategy #

    A contrarian tactic that trades against the prevailing move - selling into strength or buying into weakness - on the premise that the late stages of a move are driven by slow-reacting traders and prone to reversal. Common applications include fading overextended intraday moves and fading the initial reaction to economic data releases.
    Intuition: If everyone who wanted to buy has already bought, the marginal flow runs out and the move stalls; fading is selling the exhaustion of a crowded move rather than its beginning.
    Mechanics: Identify an extended move (a news spike, vertical run, or stretched deviation from a reference), enter against it with a defined invalidation beyond the extreme, and target reversion toward the pre-move level or a mean. Fading economic releases typically waits for the first reaction to complete before entering against it, with wider stops to survive post-release volatility.
    Failure modes: Trends persist far beyond apparent exhaustion, so fading strong trends produces repeated losses punctuated by occasional large ones when the trader averages into the fade; on regime-changing news the reversal simply never comes.
    Misconceptions: That fading is synonymous with recklessness; disciplined fades use the same invalidation-first construction as any other setup, but it is a regime-dependent framework rather than a validated general edge.
    Example: After a payrolls beat sends USD/JPY up 80 pips in minutes, a fade trader sells the pair once momentum stalls, targeting a retrace of half the spike with a stop above the reaction high.
    Aliases: fading the move, contrarian entry
    Related:
    • Mean reversion
    • Range trading
    • News trading
    • Contrarian investing
    Source: Investopedia, 'Fade' — How Fade Strategies Work in Trading; Using Fading Strategies for Economic News

    Pairs spread z-score strategy #

    A statistical-arbitrage rule that monitors the spread between two historically linked securities, standardizes it as a z-score against its own rolling mean and standard deviation, and enters a market-neutral long/short when the spread stretches beyond a threshold (commonly two standard deviations), exiting at reversion. It is the standardized-distance variant of the Gatev-Goetzmann-Rouwenhorst pairs rule.
    Intuition: If two stocks usually move together, the times they do not are opportunities: when the gap is two standard deviations wide, history says it more often narrows than widens - so you short the leader and buy the laggard.
    Mechanics: Estimate the spread (price ratio or hedge-ratio residual), compute z = (spread - rolling mean) / rolling sigma, open the pair when |z| exceeds the entry threshold, and close when z reverts toward zero or crosses the exit threshold. Gatev et al. (2006) match pairs by minimum distance between normalized price series and trade divergences beyond two historical standard deviations.
    Failure modes: Structural breaks - a takeover, earnings regime change, or delisting - turn a stretched spread into a permanently repriced one while the z-score keeps signaling reversion, and rolling-window estimates degrade in volatility regime shifts.
    Misconceptions: That a 2-sigma threshold makes the trade statistically safe; spread distributions are fat-tailed and non-stationary, so extreme readings cluster exactly when the pair relationship is breaking.
    Example: Two retail stocks' price-ratio spread has mean 1.00 and sigma 0.02; when the spread hits 1.05 (z = 2.5), short the outperformer and buy the underperformer dollar-neutral, exiting when the spread returns to 1.01.
    z_t = (s_t - mu_s) / sigma_s; enter short the spread if z_t > +2, long if z_t < -2, exit near z_t approx. 0.
    Aliases: z-score pairs trading, standardized spread stat-arb

    Channel breakout strategy #

    A trend-entry rule that buys when price exceeds the highest high (or sells when it breaks the lowest low) of a trailing N-period channel, on the premise that a break of an established range marks the start of a sustained move. It is the 'trading range break' rule tested by Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron (1992) and generalized in Donchian-style systems.
    Intuition: A market coiled in a box stores energy; a break beyond the box means buyers or sellers finally overwhelmed the range boundary, and the absence of nearby reference points lets the move run.
    Mechanics: Compute the channel as the rolling N-day high and low; enter long one tick above the N-day high or short one tick below the N-day low, with stops inside the prior range or ATR-based. Brock-Lakonishok-LeBaron tested trading-range-break rules on the Dow from 1897 to 1986, finding buy and sell signals asymmetric in return and volatility relative to random-walk, AR(1), GARCH-M, and EGARCH nulls.
    Failure modes: Sideways, mean-reverting markets generate repeated false breakouts whose accumulated small losses define the strategy's cost; the rule is structurally many small losers against few big winners, so choppy regimes bleed it.
    Misconceptions: That the channel length is the secret; results are far more sensitive to regime, costs, and exit rules than to the exact lookback, and the academic evidence is descriptive of historical data rather than a proven predictive law.
    Example: With a 50-day high at $102, a stop-entry buy triggers at $102.05; the protective stop sits at the 10-day low of $96, risking about 6% of price on the position.
    Long signal: P_t > max(H over t-N..t-1); short signal: P_t < min(L over t-N..t-1).
    Aliases: trading range break, N-day breakout

    Moving-average crossover strategy #

    A trend-following rule that goes long when a short moving average crosses above a longer one and exits or shorts on the reverse cross, using the smoothed momentum of two lookback windows as the signal. It is the canonical moving-average rule in Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron's (1992) tests of simple technical trading rules.
    Intuition: The short average is the market's recent mood, the long average its season; when mood crosses season something has changed - you trade the crossing, accepting that you will be late at both turns.
    Mechanics: Compute a fast and a slow moving average; hold long while the fast average is above the slow one (golden-cross state) and flat or short while below (death-cross state), optionally with a band filter to suppress noise crosses. Brock-Lakonishok-LeBaron tested variable-length moving-average rules (e.g., 1/50, 1/150, 5/150, 1/200, 2/200) on the Dow from 1897 to 1986 with bootstrap comparisons.
    Failure modes: In range-bound markets the crossovers whipsaw, entering at local extremes of each oscillation, and the rule's structural lag means V-shaped reversals cost the full round trip before the signal flips.
    Misconceptions: That a golden cross is a validated buy signal; it is a descriptive trend-following framework whose historical test results are sample- and cost-dependent, not a proven predictive law.
    Example: A 50-day SMA rising through the 200-day SMA at $98 flips the system long; it stays long until the 50-day falls back below the 200-day at $91, capturing the intervening trend less the lag at both ends.
    SMA_N(t) = (1/N) sum_{i=0}^{N-1} P(t-i); signal = sign(SMA_fast - SMA_slow).
    Aliases: MA crossover, golden cross / death cross system, variable-length moving average rule

    Donchian breakout strategy #

    A channel-breakout system built on Donchian channels - the trailing N-day high and low - entering long when price exceeds the channel top and short when it breaks the channel bottom, most famously as the 20-day (System 1) and 55-day (System 2) entries of the Turtle rules. Exits use a shorter opposite channel: 10-day for System 1 and 20-day for System 2.
    Intuition: Richard Donchian's insight was that you need not predict trends, only agree to join every new N-day extreme; most breakouts fail, but the few that run pay for all of them.
    Mechanics: Enter one unit when price trades one tick beyond the 20-day high or low (System 1, skipped if the previous breakout was a winner, with a 55-day failsafe entry) or the 55-day high or low (System 2, all taken). Stop at 2N, add units every 0.5N, and exit on the opposite 10-day (System 1) or 20-day (System 2) extreme.
    Failure modes: Long flat or choppy stretches produce strings of losing false breakouts, and because entries sit at the extreme, slippage and stop-outs cluster at the worst prices; whatever edge exists lives entirely in rare, fat-tailed trends.
    Misconceptions: That the 20/55 parameters are magic; the Turtles themselves emphasized N-based position sizing and consistency over the entry parameters, which any similar channel length approximates.
    Example: Gold's 55-day high is 310.00 with N = 2.50: buy one unit at 310.00, add at 311.25, 312.50, and 313.75, stop the pyramid 2N below the last fill, and exit on a 20-day low (Turtle worked example).
    Upper channel = max(high, last 20 or 55 days); lower channel = min(low, last 20 or 55 days); N = 20-day exponential average of True Range, used for sizing and stops.
    Aliases: Donchian channel breakout, N-day channel breakout

    Turtle trading #

    The complete mechanical trend-following system Richard Dennis and William Eckhardt taught their 'Turtle' trainees in 1983-84: Donchian-channel breakout entries, volatility-normalized position sizing in units of 1% risk per N, 2N stops, adds every 0.5N, and portfolio-level unit limits. Published in full by original Turtle Curtis Faith as 'The Original Turtle Trading Rules' (2003).
    Intuition: Dennis's bet was that trading could be taught as rules: trade many liquid futures markets, risk a fixed volatility fraction per unit, take every breakout, and let a few giant trends pay for dozens of stopped-out false starts.
    Mechanics: N is the 20-day exponential average of True Range; unit size = 1% of account / (N x dollars per point). System 1 enters on 20-day breakouts (skipped after a winning breakout, with a 55-day failsafe), System 2 on 55-day breakouts (all taken). Stops sit 2N from entry (about 2% risk); add one unit per 0.5N up to four; unit caps are 4 per market, 6 in closely correlated markets, 10 in loosely correlated, and 12 per direction; exits are 10-day (System 1) or 20-day (System 2) opposite extremes.
    Failure modes: Trend following fails in long trendless regimes: strings of 2N stop-outs grind equity down, and the published, widely copied rules invite crowded entries and slippage at exactly the breakout points; the system's own rule of cutting account size 20% after each 10% loss exists because losing streaks are the norm.
    Misconceptions: That the entry signals were the secret; Dennis held the rules could be printed in a newspaper because consistency, discipline, and N-based sizing were the real edge - and historical success is not a promise of profitability in today's markets.
    Example: On a $1,000,000 account with heating-oil N = 0.0141 and $42,000 per point, one unit = $10,000 / $592.20, truncated to 16 contracts; a 20-day breakout buys one unit with a stop 2N away, risking about 2% of equity (Turtle rules worked example).
    Unit = (0.01 x account equity) / (N x dollars per point); N_t = (19 x N_{t-1} + TR_t) / 20, where TR is the daily true range.
    Aliases: Turtle system, Turtle rules

    Risk-on risk-off trading #

    A macro positioning framework that rotates between higher-risk assets (equities, cyclicals, commodities, credit) in optimistic risk-on regimes and safe havens (Treasuries, gold, cash, reserve currencies) in fearful risk-off regimes, driven by shifts in aggregate investor risk tolerance. The correlated cross-asset moves in these regimes are the phenomenon the framework trades.
    Intuition: In RORO markets the global portfolio breathes as one organism: when fear rises, everything risky is sold together and everything safe is bought together, so the trade is the regime, not the asset.
    Mechanics: Define regime indicators (equity momentum, credit spreads, volatility indexes, dollar strength) and shift allocations or take paired positions - long equities against short Treasuries in risk-on, the reverse in risk-off. Dedicated products such as RORO ETFs mechanize the rotation between high-risk equities and low-risk Treasuries.
    Failure modes: Regime labels are obvious only in hindsight; choppy transitions whipsaw rotation signals, and structural shifts - such as stock-bond correlation turning positive in inflation regimes - break the canonical risk-on/risk-off pairings.
    Misconceptions: That risk-off means all risky assets fall and all havens rise every day; RORO describes correlation tendencies in aggregate risk appetite, not a daily law, and idiosyncratic news routinely overrides it.
    Example: When weak data and falling earnings guidance flip the indicators risk-off, a rotation fund shifts from 80% equities / 20% Treasuries to 30% / 70%, cutting drawdown if the selloff continues but forfeiting the rebound if it does not.
    Aliases: RORO, risk appetite rotation

    Global macro trading #

    A top-down strategy class that takes long and short positions across equity indexes, government bonds, currencies, and commodities based on macroeconomic and political analysis - interest-rate paths, growth differentials, policy shifts, and geopolitics. It is practiced by global macro hedge funds in discretionary, systematic, and CTA forms.
    Intuition: Instead of asking which stock is cheap, ask which country's whole story is mispriced - then express the view with the most efficient instrument: its currency, its bonds, its equity index, or its commodities.
    Mechanics: Build theses from rates, growth, government policy, and geopolitics, then express them via liquid instruments - FX forwards, sovereign bond futures, equity index futures, and options - often with leverage. Sub-strategies are typically currency-based, interest-rate-based, or equity-index-based; CTA and systematic funds implement model-driven, frequently trend-following versions.
    Failure modes: Macro theses fail on timing: a correct long-run view can bleed carry and mark-to-market losses for years before paying - or never pay - and policy surprises such as central-bank pivots or capital controls gap markets against leveraged books.
    Misconceptions: That global macro is glorified forecasting; practitioners emphasize asymmetric expression (options, defined-risk structures) and risk budgeting over point predictions, and the label covers both discretionary judgment and fully systematic models, which behave very differently.
    Example: A manager bearish on U.S. growth shorts S&P 500 futures and goes long 10-year Treasury futures, while going long the currency of a commodity exporter whose terms of trade are improving.
    Aliases: global macro, macro trading

    Relative strength rotation #

    A cross-sectional allocation strategy that periodically rotates capital into the assets, sectors, or industries with the strongest recent relative performance and out of the weakest, harvesting the momentum effect at the group level. It operationalizes industry momentum, which Moskowitz and Grinblatt (1999) showed accounts for much of individual-stock momentum.
    Intuition: Leadership persists longer than contrarians expect: the sectors that led last quarter tend to keep leading next quarter, so you systematically climb aboard the leaders and abandon the laggards on a fixed schedule.
    Mechanics: Rank candidates (sectors, industries, or asset classes) by trailing 3- to 12-month total return, hold the top ranks - or go long the top group and short the bottom - and re-rank and rebalance monthly or quarterly. Moskowitz-Grinblatt find industry momentum significant even after controlling for size, book-to-market, individual-stock momentum, and microstructure effects.
    Failure modes: Momentum crashes - sharp reversals after crises - rotate leadership violently, so the strategy buys the old leaders just as they mean-revert; high turnover also makes costs and taxes a first-order drag.
    Misconceptions: That relative strength is a chart heuristic; the academic version is a systematic cross-sectional ranking with a documented but decaying, crash-prone return pattern, and the rotation adds nothing if the ranking window is noise.
    Example: Each month, rank 11 sector ETFs by trailing 6-month return, hold the top 3 equal-weighted, and rotate; in a year where energy leads technology by 25 points, the portfolio stays long energy until its rank decays.
    Score_i = R_i(t-12, t-1) (optionally skipping the most recent month); hold the top-k candidates by score.
    Aliases: sector momentum rotation, relative strength investing

    Cross-sectional momentum #

    The anomaly and strategy class of buying securities with the highest recent returns relative to their peers and selling those with the lowest, over 3- to 12-month formation and holding periods. Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) documented that such relative-strength portfolios generated significant abnormal returns not explained by systematic risk or delayed reaction to common factors.
    Intuition: Winners keep winning and losers keep losing for a while: rank the whole market by recent performance, buy the top decile, short the bottom, and let the persistence work over the next quarter.
    Mechanics: Each month, rank stocks by past J-month return (J = 3-12), form decile portfolios, buy the top decile and short the bottom, and hold for K months (K = 3-12) with overlapping monthly cohorts. Jegadeesh-Titman found the strongest strategies earning roughly 1% per month in 1965-1989 data, with part of the first-year abnormal return dissipating over the following two years.
    Failure modes: Momentum crashes - violent reversals concentrated in rebound phases after market declines - can erase years of gains in months, and turnover plus short-side costs are large; the published anomaly has decayed as it became crowded.
    Misconceptions: That it is a free lunch refuting market efficiency; subsequent literature ties the premium to crash risk, behavioral underreaction, and trading costs - it is a risky, capacity-constrained anomaly, not an arbitrage.
    Example: Rank 3,000 stocks by trailing 6-month return, buy the top 300 and short the bottom 300, hold 3 months, and reform monthly; Jegadeesh-Titman's tests show the best such zero-cost portfolios averaging about 1% per month before costs.
    Momentum return = R(top decile by past J-month return) - R(bottom decile), measured over holding period K and averaged across overlapping (J,K) cohorts.
    Aliases: relative strength momentum, winners-minus-losers, J/K momentum
