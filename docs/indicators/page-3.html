    Negative volume index #

    Paul Dysart's cumulative index (1930s; popularized by Norman Fosback in Stock Market Logic) that accumulates percentage price changes only on periods when volume declines, on the theory that informed 'smart money' acts on quiet days while the crowd trades on high-volume days.
    Intuition: Strip out the noisy high-volume days and watch what remains: if price grinds higher on falling-volume days, quiet accumulation is presumed.
    Mechanics: Fosback's version (used by StockCharts): start the index at 1000; when volume falls versus the prior period, apply the period's percentage price change to the index; when volume rises, leave it unchanged. Signals come from NVI relative to its 255-day EMA - above it favors a bull market, below it a bear market.
    Failure modes: Designed for broad market indexes with exchange volume, it behaves erratically on individual stocks; the 255-day EMA signal lags heavily and whipsaws in volatile stretches.
    Misconceptions: Fosback's 96%/53% bull/bear odds were results from a 1941-1975 study, not stable laws; and falling volume is not inherently bullish in every market regime.
    Example: From NVI 1000: volume falls and price rises 0.8%, so NVI = 1008; next day volume rises and price rises 1%, and NVI stays 1008.
    If V_t < V_{t-1}: NVI_t = NVI_{t-1} x (1 + (C_t - C_{t-1})/C_{t-1}); else NVI_t = NVI_{t-1}. Dysart's original accumulated net market advances instead of percentage price change.
    Aliases: NVI
    Related:

    Positive volume index #

    The complement of the Negative Volume Index: Dysart's cumulative index (in Fosback's formulation) that accumulates percentage price changes only on periods when volume increases, tracking what the volume-driven 'crowd' is doing.
    Intuition: High-volume days are when the crowd trades; PVI isolates those days to show the crowd's cumulative net effect on price.
    Mechanics: When today's volume exceeds yesterday's, add the percentage price change (scaled by the index) to the running PVI from a base of 1000; otherwise the index is unchanged. Watched against its 255-period (one-year) moving average: above it confirms rising prices, below it confirms declines.
    Failure modes: Prone to whipsaw crossovers of its moving average, and it can fall while price rises strongly if the biggest-volume days are down days; the crowd-is-wrong premise is a heuristic, not an established fact.
    Misconceptions: Fosback's 67%/21% bear-market odds are period-specific historical results, and PVI above its one-year average 'confirms' rather than predicts a price rise.
    Example: From PVI 1000: volume jumps and price rises 1.2%, so PVI = 1012; next day volume falls and price drops 0.5%, and PVI stays 1012.
    If V_t > V_{t-1}: PVI_t = PVI_{t-1} + ((C_t - C_{t-1})/C_{t-1}) x PVI_{t-1}; else PVI_t = PVI_{t-1}.
    Aliases: PVI

    Volume-weighted average price #

    The average price of a security weighted by volume, computed intraday as cumulative (typical price x volume) divided by cumulative volume from the session open; the benchmark institutions use to judge execution quality and intraday fair value.
    Intuition: It is the day's center of gravity of trading: above VWAP the average buyer is in profit, below it the average seller is.
    Mechanics: For each intraday bar compute the typical price (H+L+C)/3, multiply by volume, and keep running totals of price-volume and volume; VWAP = cumulative TPV / cumulative volume, resetting each session. Price above VWAP reads as intraday strength; the line serves as a trend and mean-reversion reference.
    Failure modes: Being cumulative, its lag grows through the day and early values are erratic on few bars; in its classic single-session form it is undefined for multi-day analysis.
    Misconceptions: VWAP is not a moving average and is not defined for daily or weekly charts; and trading 'back to VWAP' is a heuristic, not a law of price behavior.
    Example: StockCharts' IBM example: over the first 30 one-minute bars prices ranged 126.67-127.36 while VWAP ran 127.09-127.21 through the middle.
    VWAP = sum(TypicalPrice_i x Volume_i) / sum(Volume_i), TypicalPrice = (High + Low + Close)/3, accumulated from the day's open.
    Aliases: VWAP

    Anchored VWAP #

    Volume-weighted average price computed from a user-chosen anchor bar - a significant high or low, earnings release, gap, or other event - rather than the session open, extending the VWAP logic across days and timeframes.
    Intuition: Markets reprice after events; anchoring VWAP to the event measures whether buyers or sellers have won all the volume traded since that moment.
    Mechanics: Same formula as VWAP - sum(TP x V) / sum(V) - but accumulation starts at the chosen anchor bar and runs to the latest bar, on any timeframe. Used as dynamic support/resistance; converging AVWAPs from multiple anchors mark especially watched zones.
    Failure modes: Anchor choice is subjective - different anchors give different lines, inviting confirmation bias; like VWAP it lags increasingly as the anchored window lengthens.
    Misconceptions: There is no objectively 'correct' anchor - the tool measures the post-event volume-weighted consensus, not a hidden fair value.
    Example: StockCharts' TSLA example: the session VWAP from the open was skewed by the first 20 minutes, while an AVWAP anchored at the morning low better tracked midday trade.
    AVWAP = sum(TypicalPrice_i x Volume_i, from anchor to t) / sum(Volume_i, from anchor to t).
    Aliases: AVWAP
    Related:
    • Volume-weighted average price
    • Moving VWAP
    • Volume-weighted moving average

    Moving VWAP #

    A moving average of successive VWAP values over a user-chosen number of periods, producing a volume-weighted average that flows continuously from day to day instead of resetting each session.
    Intuition: Classic VWAP dies at the close; MVWAP keeps a rolling volume-weighted memory, giving longer-horizon traders a VWAP-like reference across days.
    Mechanics: Compute intraday VWAP as usual (cumulative typical price x volume over cumulative volume), then average the last n VWAP values; a 10-period MVWAP starts plotting after 10 VWAP points and slides forward, dropping the oldest.
    Failure modes: Doubly smoothed, it lags more than plain VWAP, and its value depends on how each platform chains daily VWAPs across sessions; there is no single canonical construction.
    Misconceptions: MVWAP has no end-of-day 'final value' significance the way daily VWAP does - it is a rolling average of averages, not an execution benchmark.
    Example: If the last five daily VWAP values are 100, 101, 101.5, 102, 102.5, the 5-period MVWAP = 101.4.
    MVWAP(n) = (1/n) x sum of the last n VWAP values, where each VWAP = sum(TypicalPrice x Volume) / sum(Volume) over its calculation window.
    Aliases: MVWAP
    Related:

    Volume-weighted moving average #

    A moving average in which each price is weighted by its bar's volume - bars traded on heavy volume pull the average toward them, so the line tracks where business was actually done rather than treating all bars equally.
    Intuition: An SMA answers 'average price of the last n bars'; a VWMA answers 'average price weighted by how much traded in each of those bars.'
    Mechanics: Multiply each close by its volume, sum over n periods, and divide by the sum of volume over n periods. Often compared against an equal-length SMA to judge whether volume-backed prices lead or lag the plain average.
    Failure modes: A single extreme-volume bar dominates the window, so the average jumps when it enters and again when it leaves; meaningless on instruments without reliable volume such as spot forex.
    Misconceptions: VWMA is not VWAP: VWAP is cumulative from a session anchor, VWMA is a rolling n-period window - they answer different questions.
    Example: Tulip Indicators' worked example: closes 81.59-83.61 with volumes around 3.8-7.7M give a first 5-period VWMA of 82.33, versus an equal-weighted SMA of 82.63.
    VWMA(n) = sum(C_{t-i} x V_{t-i}, i=0..n-1) / sum(V_{t-i}, i=0..n-1).
    Aliases: VWMA

    Relative volume #

    The ratio of current trading volume to average volume over a look-back (typically 10-20 sessions), often time-of-day adjusted so that, say, 10:30 a.m. volume is compared with prior 10:30 a.m. volumes; a reading of 1.0 means perfectly normal participation.
    Intuition: Raw volume is meaningless without context - five million shares is quiet for a mega-cap and extraordinary for a small-cap; RVOL normalizes it.
    Mechanics: RVOL = current volume / average volume over n sessions; intraday versions divide cumulative volume so far by the average cumulative volume at the same time of day. Traders screen for RVOL of roughly 1.5-2.0 or higher to find 'in play' names.
    Failure modes: High RVOL signals attention, not direction - it accompanies both breakouts and dumps; time-of-day effects (open and close volume surges) distort non-adjusted intraday readings.
    Misconceptions: RVOL is not a buy signal - a spike can mark exhaustion at the end of a move as easily as participation at the start; it measures activity, nothing else.
    Example: A stock averaging 300,000 shares a day that trades 1,000,000 has RVOL ~ 3.3; TradingSim notes SIGA traded 37M shares versus a ~130k average on its May 2022 news day.
    RVOL = V_current / Average(V, n); time-adjusted form: RVOL(t) = CumV(today, up to t) / Average_n(CumV(up to same time t)).
    Aliases: RVOL, Relative Volume (RVOL)
    Related:

    Volume oscillator #

    A MACD-style oscillator applied to volume: the difference between a fast and a slow exponential moving average of volume, usually expressed as a percentage of the slow average (the Percentage Volume Oscillator, defaults 12, 26, 9).
    Intuition: It shows whether participation is expanding or drying up: positive and rising means short-term volume is running above its longer norm.
    Mechanics: PVO = (EMA(V,12) - EMA(V,26)) / EMA(V,26) x 100, with a 9-period EMA signal line and a histogram, paralleling the MACD/PPO structure. Used mainly to confirm breakouts - price breaks on rising or positive PVO carry more weight.
    Failure modes: Volume does not trend smoothly, so PVO is choppy and divergences are unreliable; because both EMAs lag, PVO can confirm a breakout only after the fact.
    Misconceptions: A positive PVO does not say volume is 'high' in any absolute sense - only that the 12-period average exceeds the 26-period; and high volume is neither bullish nor bearish without price context.
    Example: A 12-day volume EMA of 3.0M against a 26-day EMA of 2.5M gives PVO = +20, matching the surge level StockCharts shows confirming ADM's resistance and support breaks.
    PVO = ((EMA(V,12) - EMA(V,26)) / EMA(V,26)) x 100; Signal = EMA(PVO, 9); Histogram = PVO - Signal.
    Aliases: PVO, Percentage Volume Oscillator, Volume Oscillator (VO)

    Chaikin oscillator #

    Marc Chaikin's momentum-of-money-flow oscillator: the MACD formula applied to the Accumulation/Distribution Line - the 3-day EMA of the ADL minus its 10-day EMA - designed to anticipate turns in accumulation and distribution.
    Intuition: It is an indicator of an indicator: instead of watching the ADL's slow drift, you watch its momentum cross zero, flagging shifts in buying and selling pressure earlier.
    Mechanics: Compute the ADL from Money Flow Multiplier times volume; the oscillator = EMA(ADL,3) - EMA(ADL,10). Positive means the ADL is rising (buying pressure), negative means it is falling; divergences with price warn of pressure shifts before zero-line confirmation.
    Failure modes: Three derivations removed from price, it frequently disconnects from it, and the default (3,10) settings cross zero often, producing whipsaws that need confirmation.
    Misconceptions: A bullish divergence is not yet buying pressure - while the oscillator stays below zero, selling pressure still dominates; confirmation requires a cross into positive territory.
    Example: If the 3-day EMA of the ADL is 12,000 and the 10-day EMA is 11,000, the oscillator reads +1,000 - the threshold StockCharts uses in its bullish scan.
    Chaikin Oscillator = EMA(ADL, 3) - EMA(ADL, 10), with ADL the running sum of [((C-L) - (H-C)) / (H-L)] x V.
    Aliases: CO, Chaikin Osc
    Related:

    Aroon up #

    Tushar Chande's 1995 indicator measuring how recently price made a new n-period high, expressed as a 0-100 percentage: 100 when the high occurred in the current period, decaying toward 0 as it recedes into the past.
    Intuition: Instead of asking how far price moved, Aroon-Up asks how long ago the latest high was - frequent new highs keep it pinned near 100.
    Mechanics: Aroon-Up = 100 x (n - periods since the n-period high) / n, default n = 25. Readings above 50 mean a new high within the most recent half of the window; sustained 70-100 readings mark strong uptrends.
    Failure modes: It measures time, not magnitude - a market drifting sideways near its highs keeps Aroon-Up high without real momentum, and the reading decays mechanically as the window ages even when price is flat.
    Misconceptions: Aroon-Up at 100 does not mean 'overbought, expect reversal' - it only means the latest n-period high happened today.
    Example: With n = 25 and the 25-day high made 5 days ago: Aroon-Up = (25-5)/25 x 100 = 80.
    Aroon-Up(n) = ((n - PeriodsSinceHighestHigh(n)) / n) x 100.
    Aliases: Aroon-Up, Aroon Up
    Related:
    • Aroon down
    • Aroon oscillator
    • Average directional index
    Source: Aroon | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com — How To Calculate the Aroon Indicators

    Aroon down #

    The downside counterpart in Chande's Aroon system: a measure of how recently price made a new n-period low, on a 0-100 scale where 100 means the low was set in the current period.
    Intuition: It tracks the freshness of new lows; a market printing new lows today has Aroon-Down at 100, and sustained 70-100 readings mark strong downtrends.
    Mechanics: Aroon-Down = 100 x (n - periods since the n-period low) / n, default 25. Crosses of Aroon-Down above Aroon-Up, moves above 50, and surges to 100 are the three stages of an emerging downtrend signal.
    Failure modes: Same time-not-magnitude limitation as Aroon-Up; both lines can sit low together in consolidations, giving no directional read at all.
    Misconceptions: A high Aroon-Down is a description of recent lows, not an oversold buy signal.
    Example: With n = 25 and the 25-day low set 10 days ago: Aroon-Down = (25-10)/25 x 100 = 60.
    Aroon-Down(n) = ((n - PeriodsSinceLowestLow(n)) / n) x 100.
    Aliases: Aroon-Down, Aroon Down
    Related:
    • Aroon up
    • Aroon oscillator
    • Average directional index
    Source: Aroon | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com — How To Calculate the Aroon Indicators

    Aroon oscillator #

    The difference between Aroon-Up and Aroon-Down, compressing Chande's pair into one line bounded between -100 and +100; positive when new highs are more recent than new lows, negative when new lows are more recent.
    Intuition: It nets the two Aroon lines: +100 means today's bar made a new n-period high while the low is as old as possible; the zero line is the bull-bear divide.
    Mechanics: Aroon Oscillator = Aroon-Up - Aroon-Down with the same n (default 25). Used for zero-line trend bias and, with +/-50 or +/-90 thresholds, for strong-trend filtering.
    Failure modes: It lags because it only reacts once highs and lows age out of the window; choppy markets flip it across zero repeatedly, and it was not designed for classic oscillator divergence signals.
    Misconceptions: A positive reading does not measure trend strength in price terms - a +40 reading only ranks the recency of highs versus lows.
    Example: Aroon-Up 80 and Aroon-Down 20 give an oscillator of +60, an upside bias; +100 requires Up = 100 and Down = 0 simultaneously.
    AroonOsc(n) = AroonUp(n) - AroonDown(n) = 100 x (PeriodsSinceLowestLow(n) - PeriodsSinceHighestHigh(n)) / n.
    Aliases: Aroon Osc
    Related:
    • Aroon up
    • Aroon down

    Chande momentum oscillator #

    Tushar Chande's momentum oscillator: 100 times the difference between summed up-moves and summed down-moves over n periods divided by their total, bounding raw, unsmoothed momentum between -100 and +100.
    Intuition: It is a tug-of-war score between up days and down days: if all movement over the window was up, CMO is +100; all down, -100.
    Mechanics: Sum the gains of up closes and the losses of down closes separately over n periods; CMO = 100 x (Su - Sd)/(Su + Sd). Readings beyond +/-50 are treated as overbought/oversold; divergences and zero-line crosses give trend signals.
    Failure modes: Unsmoothed, so it is noisier than RSI and reaches its extremes more often; overbought/oversold levels fail in strong trends, as with all bounded oscillators.
    Misconceptions: CMO is not a smoothed RSI - it nets raw sums rather than Wilder-smoothed average gains and losses, and the +/-50 thresholds are conventions, not validated cutoffs.
    Example: Over 20 days with up-moves totaling 30 points and down-moves 10: CMO = 100 x (30-10)/(30+10) = +50.
    CMO(n) = 100 x (Su - Sd) / (Su + Sd), where Su = sum of max(C_i - C_{i-1}, 0) and Sd = sum of max(C_{i-1} - C_i, 0) over n periods.
    Aliases: CMO
    Related:

    • Variable index dynamic average

    Source: Chande Momentum Oscillator - Investopedia — How to Calculate the Chande Momentum Oscillator

    Ultimate oscillator #

    Larry Williams' three-timeframe momentum oscillator (1976; Stocks & Commodities, 1985): a weighted blend (4:2:1) of buying-pressure-to-true-range ratios over 7, 14, and 28 periods, designed to reduce the false divergences of single-timeframe oscillators.
    Intuition: Short-term momentum dominates the blend but is checked by two longer frames, so a divergence must show weakness across multiple horizons to count.
    Mechanics: BP = Close - min(Low, prior Close); TR = max(High, prior Close) - min(Low, prior Close); Average_k = sum(BP, k)/sum(TR, k) for k = 7, 14, 28; UO = 100 x (4xAvg7 + 2xAvg14 + Avg28)/7. Williams' buy signal: a bullish divergence whose oscillator low is below 30, confirmed by a break above the divergence high.
    Failure modes: The fixed 4:2:1 weighting and divergence-plus-level rules are intricate but still whipsaw; on quiet securities the oscillator may never reach 30 or 70 with default settings, inviting parameter fiddling.
    Misconceptions: Despite the name it is not 'ultimate' or proven superior - it is one multi-frame weighting scheme among many, and its signals still require confirmation.
    Example: If Avg7 = 0.60, Avg14 = 0.50 and Avg28 = 0.40, UO = 100 x (2.4 + 1.0 + 0.4)/7 ~ 54.3.
    UO = 100 x (4 x Avg7 + 2 x Avg14 + 1 x Avg28) / (4+2+1), with Avg_k = sum(BP, k) / sum(TR, k).
    Aliases: UO, Ultimate Osc
    Related:
    • Williams percent R
    • Relative strength index
    • Average true range
    Source: Ultimate Oscillator | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com — Calculating the Ultimate Oscillator

    TRIX #

    Jack Hutson's momentum oscillator (Technical Analysis of Stocks & Commodities, early 1980s): the one-period percentage rate of change of a triple-smoothed exponential moving average (EMA of EMA of EMA), engineered to filter out price moves too small to matter.
    Intuition: Three rounds of smoothing strip out noise; what remains turning up or down is, in theory, the significant trend cycle.
    Mechanics: Compute EMA(n) of the close, then EMA(n) of that series, then EMA(n) again (default n = 15); TRIX = 100 x (tripleEMA_t - tripleEMA_{t-1}) / tripleEMA_{t-1}. Traded via signal-line (9-period EMA) crosses, zero-line crosses, and divergences.
    Failure modes: The triple smoothing lags substantially - StockCharts' Intel example shows three of six signal-line crosses whipsawing in one stretch; in flat markets TRIX hugs zero with ambiguous crosses.
    Misconceptions: Filtering out 'insignificant' moves also filters the early part of significant ones; TRIX's smoothness is bought with delay, not accuracy.
    Example: If the triple-smoothed EMA rises from 100.0 to 100.2, TRIX prints +0.2 (percent) for that bar.
    EMA1 = EMA(C, n); EMA2 = EMA(EMA1, n); EMA3 = EMA(EMA2, n); TRIX = 100 x (EMA3_t - EMA3_{t-1}) / EMA3_{t-1}.
    Aliases: TRIX, Triple exponential average oscillator

    Detrended price oscillator #

    An oscillator that strips trend from price by subtracting a displaced (effectively centered) moving average: the close (n/2 + 1) periods ago minus the n-period SMA, designed to isolate cycle highs and lows rather than momentum.
    Intuition: Comparing a past price against a moving average centered on it cancels the trend component, leaving the shorter cycle around the trend visible.
    Mechanics: DPO(n) = Close_{t-(n/2+1)} - SMA(n)_t; for n = 20 the SMA is offset 11 bars to the left, centering it on the look-back window. Because of the displacement, the DPO does not extend to the latest bar and is used to measure cycle length from peak to peak or trough to trough.
    Failure modes: It deliberately does not cover the most recent n/2+1 bars, so it cannot time current action, and it presumes a discoverable cycle exists, which many markets do not have.
    Misconceptions: DPO is not a momentum oscillator, and shifting it right to line up with current price defeats its purpose - StockCharts notes right-shifted DPO matches current price action poorly.
    Example: 20-day DPO with the close 11 days ago at 103 and the 20-day SMA at 100: DPO = +3; troughs about 20 bars apart suggest a 20-day cycle.
    DPO(n) = Price_{t-(n/2 + 1)} - SMA(Price, n)_t.
    Aliases: DPO
    Related:

    • Rate of change
    • Moving average envelope
    Source: Detrended Price Oscillator (DPO) | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com — Calculation

    Know Sure Thing oscillator #

    Martin Pring's summed rate-of-change oscillator (Stocks & Commodities, 1992): four rate-of-change series over different periods, each SMA-smoothed, then combined with weights 1-2-3-4 so the longest cycle counts most.
    Intuition: Instead of one momentum look-back, KST blends four cycles - short-term turns nudge it, but only broad multi-timeframe momentum swings really move it.
    Mechanics: RCMA1 = SMA(ROC(10),10); RCMA2 = SMA(ROC(15),10); RCMA3 = SMA(ROC(20),10); RCMA4 = SMA(ROC(30),15); KST = 1xRCMA1 + 2xRCMA2 + 3xRCMA3 + 4xRCMA4, with a 9-period SMA signal line. Signals: signal-line crosses, centerline crosses, and trend-line breaks on the KST line itself.
    Failure modes: Unbounded, so no overbought/oversold levels exist, and signal-line crosses whipsaw - bearish crosses in strong uptrends often flag only slowing momentum, not reversal.
    Misconceptions: The name promises certainty it cannot deliver - Pring himself stresses confirmation and warns that one parameter set does not fit all securities.
    Example: If the four smoothed ROCs read +2, +3, +4, +5, then KST = 2 + 6 + 12 + 20 = +40.
    KST = sum(i x SMA(ROC(r_i), s_i), i=1..4) with (r,s) = (10,10), (15,10), (20,10), (30,15); Signal = SMA(KST, 9).
    Aliases: KST, Know Sure Thing, Pring's KST
    Related:

    • TRIX

    Coppock curve #

    Edwin Coppock's long-term momentum indicator (Barron's, October 1965): a 10-period weighted moving average of the sum of the 14-period and 11-period rates of change, designed to flag major stock-market bottoms on monthly charts when it turns up from below zero.
    Intuition: Coppock reportedly chose 11-14 months after hearing that grief over a loss typically runs about that long; the indicator waits out the mourning period and signals when the market has 'recovered.'
    Mechanics: Coppock = WMA(ROC(14) + ROC(11), 10). Classic usage is monthly charts of major indexes, where a cross from negative to positive is the buy signal; platforms also allow shorter timeframes and adjusted parameters (e.g., 20,10,10 on daily charts).
    Failure modes: On monthly data it signals only every few years and can lag bottoms by months; designed for major equity indexes, on individual stocks or short timeframes it is just another smoothed ROC.
    Misconceptions: Coppock never proposed sell signals - the positive-to-negative 'sell' cross is a later addition - and the 11/14/10 parameters were not statistically optimized.
    Example: After a decline, if the 14-month and 11-month ROCs sum to +3 and rise, the 10-month WMA climbs toward +3, crossing zero for the classic buy signal.
    Coppock = WMA(10) of [ROC(14) + ROC(11)], ROC(n) = ((C_t - C_{t-n}) / C_{t-n}) x 100.
    Aliases: Coppock Curve, Coppock guide
    Related:

    • Weighted moving average
    • Know Sure Thing oscillator
    Source: Coppock Curve | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com — SharpCharts Calculation

    Fisher transform #

    John Ehlers' transform (Stocks & Commodities, November 2002) that maps normalized prices through the Fisher function so the output has an approximately Gaussian distribution, producing sharper, better-defined turning points than the raw price series.
    Intuition: Price extremes are rare and squashed on a normal scale; the Fisher transform stretches the tails so that turning points stand out as crisp spikes.
    Mechanics: Normalize price (typically the bar midpoint) to roughly -1..+1 against its n-period range with a stochastic-like step and light smoothing, then apply y = 0.5 x ln((1+x)/(1-x)); a signal line is the transform lagged one bar. Turns of the Fisher line mark candidate price reversals.
    Failure modes: Sharper turning points also mean more apparent 'signals', many of which precede no real reversal; the transform assumes a bounded, mean-reverting input, so strong trends pin it at extremes.
    Misconceptions: The Gaussian reshaping is a display transform, not evidence that prices are normally distributed or that a reversal is due; Ehlers presents it as a timing aid, not a predictor.
    Example: If normalized X = 0.9, Fisher = 0.5 x ln(1.9/0.1) ~ 1.47 - a stretched extreme reading that flags a potential turn when it hooks down.
    Fisher = 0.5 x ln((1 + X) / (1 - X)), where X is price normalized to (-1, +1) over the look-back (inputs clamped just inside +/-1).

    Zigzag indicator #

    A price filter rather than a true indicator: it draws straight legs between swing points, ignoring all moves smaller than a set percentage (traditionally 5%), to reveal the significant highs and lows through the noise.
    Intuition: It answers 'what would this chart look like if every wiggle under x% were deleted?' - exposing the skeleton of swings for trend, pattern, and Elliott Wave analysis.
    Mechanics: Track price from the last confirmed pivot; extend the current leg while price moves favorably, and confirm a new pivot only after a reversal of at least the threshold percentage. On bar/candle charts the filter uses the high-low range, on line charts the closes.
    Failure modes: The last leg is provisional and repaints until the threshold is met - ZigZag is hindsight by design, and StockCharts states explicitly that it has no predictive power.
    Misconceptions: The clean historical legs are not tradeable signals; the current leg can disappear entirely if the reversal threshold is never reached.
    Example: From a 100 low, price rises to 112 (+12%): the leg extends to 112; only a decline to 100.8 (-10% with a 10% setting) confirms a new down leg.
    A pivot is confirmed when |P_t - P_pivot| / P_pivot >= threshold (e.g., 5%); legs connect successive confirmed pivots.
    Aliases: ZigZag, Zig Zag

    Fractal indicator #

    Bill Williams' five-bar reversal marker: an up fractal is a series of at least five successive bars with the highest high in the middle and two lower highs on each side, and a down fractal is the mirror image with lows; one of the five indicators of his Trading Chaos system.
    Intuition: A fractal flags a local extreme where the market pushed to a level and was rejected on both sides - a candidate swing point.
    Mechanics: Mark an up arrow when the middle bar of five has the highest high with two lower highs on each side, and a down arrow symmetrically on lows. Because two bars must follow, the signal is confirmed only two bars late; Williams filtered fractals against the Alligator's Teeth line.
    Failure modes: Five-bar fractals occur constantly and most are noise; the two-bar confirmation lag means entries come after part of the move, and counter-trend fractals fail repeatedly in trends.
    Misconceptions: A fractal arrow is not a standalone trade signal - Williams used it as a level (entry on a break of the fractal) filtered by the Alligator, not as a reversal call by itself.
    Example: Five bars with highs 10, 11, 12, 11, 10 put an up fractal on the middle bar (high 12), confirmed two bars later.
    Up fractal at bar t: H_t > H_{t-1}, H_t > H_{t-2}, H_t > H_{t+1}, H_t > H_{t+2}; down fractal: L_t below the four surrounding lows.
    Aliases: Williams fractals, Fractals (Bill Williams)

    Alligator indicator #

    Bill Williams' trend-detection overlay: three smoothed moving averages of the median price - Jaw (13-period, shifted 8 forward), Teeth (8, shifted 5), and Lips (5, shifted 3) - whose entanglement signals a sleeping (range) market and whose fanning signals a feeding (trend) phase.
    Intuition: In Williams' metaphor, when the three balance lines intertwine the alligator sleeps and you do not trade; when they open and order by speed, it hunts and you follow the trend.
    Mechanics: Compute the SMMA of (H+L)/2 over 13, 8, and 5 periods, displacing each line 8, 5, and 3 bars forward. Trend state: lines fanned and ordered (Lips above Teeth above Jaw for an uptrend); intertwined lines mean a range; lines joining after a trend mean the alligator is 'sated' - time to take profit.
    Failure modes: In choppy markets the lines braid continuously, generating repeated false wake-up signals - exactly the conditions Williams says to stand aside from, which the tool can only identify in hindsight.
    Misconceptions: The forward shifts do not predict anything - they are display offsets of lagging averages - and the 13/8/5 'Fibonacci' settings are convention, not optimization.
    Example: If the SMMA(13) of the median price is 100, it is drawn 8 bars ahead as the Jaw; when the Lips cross above the Teeth and then the Jaw and all three fan upward, the alligator is 'awake and eating'.
    Jaw = SMMA(MedianPrice, 13) shifted +8; Teeth = SMMA(MedianPrice, 8) shifted +5; Lips = SMMA(MedianPrice, 5) shifted +3; MedianPrice = (High + Low)/2.

    Awesome oscillator #

    Bill Williams' momentum histogram: the 5-period simple moving average of bar midpoints minus the 34-period SMA of bar midpoints - effectively a MACD of the median price computed with simple, not exponential, averages.
    Intuition: It compares very recent momentum with background momentum using midpoints rather than closes, so the intraday range feeds the reading.
    Mechanics: AO = SMA((H+L)/2, 5) - SMA((H+L)/2, 34), plotted as a histogram colored green when the bar's value exceeds the prior bar's and red when lower. Williams' named signals: zero-line cross, saucer (three-bar continuation pattern), and twin peaks (a divergence-like setup).
    Failure modes: Zero-line crosses whipsaw badly in chop (TradingSim's example: only 2 of 7 crosses captured sizable moves), and extreme AO spikes mark strong trends, so fading them is dangerous.
    Misconceptions: The histogram color shows change versus the prior bar, not sign - bars can be green below zero; and Williams' 'best momentum indicator' claim has no comparative evidence behind it.
    Example: If the 5-bar midpoint SMA is 102 and the 34-bar is 100, AO = +2.0; a bar printing +1.8 next paints red even though still positive.
    AO = SMA(MP, 5) - SMA(MP, 34), where MP = (High + Low)/2.

    Gator oscillator #

    Bill Williams' companion to the Alligator: a dual histogram showing the convergence and divergence of the Alligator's balance lines - the upper histogram is |Jaw - Teeth| and the lower is -|Teeth - Lips| - used to time the 'sleeping, awakening, eating, sated' phases.
    Intuition: Instead of eyeballing whether the Alligator's mouth is opening or closing, the Gator plots the mouth's spread directly: expanding bars mean feeding, contracting bars mean sleep.
    Mechanics: Compute the Alligator Jaw, Teeth and Lips (SMMA of the median price, periods 13/8/5 shifted 8/5/3); the top bar is |Jaw - Teeth| above zero and the bottom bar is -|Teeth - Lips| below zero, colored green when larger than the prior bar and red when smaller.
    Failure modes: Entirely derived from lagging smoothed averages, so phases are identified late; MotiveWave notes it generates no trading signals of its own - it only visualizes Alligator context.
    Misconceptions: Green bars on both histograms are not a buy signal by themselves - Williams' phases require alignment with the Alligator's line ordering and price position.
    Example: With Jaw = 100, Teeth = 101, Lips = 103: upper bar = 1, lower bar = -2; if yesterday's bars were 0.8 and -1.5, both expanded - the gator is 'eating'.
    Upper = |SMMA(MP,13,+8) - SMMA(MP,8,+5)|; Lower = -|SMMA(MP,8,+5) - SMMA(MP,5,+3)|.
    Aliases: GO, Gator Oscillator

    Elder Ray index #

    Alexander Elder's two-part oscillator: Bull Power = period high minus a 13-period EMA of closes, and Bear Power = period low minus the same EMA, measuring how far buyers and sellers can push price above and below the market's consensus of value.
    Intuition: The EMA is the agreed value; the high shows the maximum buyers could reach and the low the maximum sellers could reach - their distances from the EMA are the two camps' power.
    Mechanics: Compute EMA(13) of the close; Bull Power = High - EMA13 (typically positive in uptrends) and Bear Power = Low - EMA13 (typically negative). Elder's buy setup: EMA rising with Bear Power negative but rising; sell/short setup: EMA falling with Bull Power positive but falling.
    Failure modes: Moving-average-based, so it lags and whipsaws around zero - both limitations Investopedia lists explicitly - and signals in fast reversals arrive after much of the move.
    Misconceptions: Positive Bull Power alone is not a buy - Elder's rules require the EMA slope plus the opposing camp weakening (Bear Power below zero and rising), not one component in isolation.
    Example: With the 13-day EMA at 50, a bar's high of 51.5 and low of 48.5 give Bull Power +1.5 and Bear Power -1.5; Bear Power rising from -2.0 to -1.0 while the EMA climbs is Elder's buy configuration.
    Bull Power = High - EMA(Close, 13); Bear Power = Low - EMA(Close, 13).
    Aliases: Elder-Ray, Bull Power and Bear Power
