    Mass index #

    Donald Dorsey's volatility indicator: a 25-period sum of the ratio of a 9-period EMA of the high-low range to a double-smoothed 9-period EMA of that range, designed to spot 'reversal bulges' when the daily range expands abnormally.
    Intuition: When the range EMA swells relative to its own smoothing, the sum bulges - Dorsey's warning that the current trend may be about to reverse.
    Mechanics: Single = EMA(High - Low, 9); Double = EMA(Single, 9); Mass Index = 25-period sum of (Single / Double). Dorsey's signal is a move above 27 followed by a fall back below 26.5 (the 'reversal bulge'); the indicator is directionless - trade direction comes from the prior trend, and Dorsey paired it with TRIX for timing.
    Failure modes: Readings above 27 are rare, so signals are few; thresholds need per-security calibration, and the bulge warns of reversal without saying when or confirming direction.
    Misconceptions: It is not directional - a bulge after an uptrend warns of a downside reversal and vice versa, and it must be paired with a directional tool.
    Example: If the EMA ratio runs at 1.04 for 25 straight periods, the Mass Index = 26.0; a push to 27.2 followed by a drop through 26.5 completes a reversal bulge.
    MI = sum over 25 periods of [EMA(H - L, 9) / EMA(EMA(H - L, 9), 9)].
    Aliases: MI, Donald Dorsey's Mass Index

    Vortex indicator #

    Etienne Botes and Douglas Siepman's trend indicator (2010, inspired by Wilder's DMI): +VI and -VI compare 14-period sums of inter-bar directional movement - current high versus prior low and current low versus prior high - to the 14-period sum of True Range.
    Intuition: Where Wilder's DMI measures movement beyond the previous bar, the Vortex measures the connection between consecutive bars' extremes - the 'flow' between them.
    Mechanics: +VM = |High_t - Low_{t-1}| and -VM = |Low_t - High_{t-1}|; sum both over 14 periods and divide by the 14-period sum of True Range: +VI14 = sum(+VM)/sum(TR), -VI14 = sum(-VM)/sum(TR). +VI crossing above -VI is bullish, below bearish.
    Failure modes: Like DMI it whipsaws in ranges, and the cross signals lag the turn; absolute VI levels have no standard overbought/oversold meaning.
    Misconceptions: +VI above -VI states recent directional flow, not future odds; the indicator is sometimes marketed with Schauberger 'vortex' mystique that has no bearing on its arithmetic.
    Example: If the 14-period sums are +VM = 30, -VM = 20 and TR = 40: +VI = 0.75 and -VI = 0.50 - a mild bullish flow.
    +VI14 = sum(|H_t - L_{t-1}|, 14) / sum(TR, 14); -VI14 = sum(|L_t - H_{t-1}|, 14) / sum(TR, 14).
    Aliases: VI, VI+ / VI-

    Relative vigor index #

    John Ehlers' Relative Vigor Index: an oscillator comparing a smoothed close-minus-open to a smoothed high-minus-low, on the premise that closes tend to exceed opens in uptrends and fall below them in downtrends, so the ratio moves in phase with the price cycle.
    Intuition: A bar's 'vigor' is how much of its range the close captures from the open; persistently positive vigor means an uptrend.
    Mechanics: Numerator = symmetric 4-bar weighting of (Close - Open): (a + 2b + 2c + d)/6; denominator = the same weighting of (High - Low); RVI = SMA(numerator, 10) / SMA(denominator, 10); Signal = (RVI + 2i + 2j + k)/6 over the last four RVI values. Signals: RVI/signal-line crosses and divergences with price.
    Failure modes: The symmetric weighting uses surrounding bars, adding delay; in strong one-way trends RVI saturates and divergences mislead.
    Misconceptions: 'Vigor' is a metaphor for close-versus-open position, not a trend-strength measure like ADX; the close-above-open tendency is a heuristic, not a law.
    Example: Four bars with (C-O) of +1.0, +0.5, +0.5, +1.0 give numerator (1+1+1+1)/6 ~ 0.67; with a range denominator of 2.0, RVI ~ 0.33.
    RVI = SMA10[(C-O) weighted 1,2,2,1 / 6] / SMA10[(H-L) weighted 1,2,2,1 / 6]; Signal = (RVI + 2 x RVI_{-1} + 2 x RVI_{-2} + RVI_{-3}) / 6.
    Aliases: RVI

    Connors RSI #

    Larry Connors' composite short-term mean-reversion oscillator: the average of a 3-period RSI of closes, a 2-period RSI of the up/down streak length, and the 100-period percent rank of the one-day rate of change.
    Intuition: It blends three flavors of stretched-ness - price momentum, streak duration, and the size of today's move versus recent history - into one 0-100 short-term extreme gauge.
    Mechanics: CRSI(3,2,100) = [RSI(Close,3) + RSI(Streak,2) + PercentRank(ROC(1),100)] / 3, where Streak counts consecutive up closes (positive) or down closes (negative) and resets on direction change. Readings above 90 are treated as overbought and below 10 as oversold in Connors' mean-reversion systems.
    Failure modes: Built and tuned for short-term mean reversion in equities, so in trending or low-liquidity markets its extreme readings fail like any oscillator's; three tunable components invite overfitting.
    Misconceptions: CRSI overbought is a mean-reversion setup description, not a short signal in isolation - Connors' own published systems pair it with trend and price filters such as the 200-day moving average.
    Example: If RSI(3) = 85, the streak RSI(2) = 95, and today's +2.1% move ranks in the 90th percentile of the last 100 daily moves: CRSI = (85+95+90)/3 = 90, the overbought threshold.
    CRSI = (RSI(3) + RSI(Streak, 2) + PercentRank(100) of 1-day ROC) / 3.
    Aliases: CRSI, ConnorsRSI
