    Simple moving average #

    The unweighted arithmetic mean of the last n observations (usually closing prices), recomputed each period as the window slides forward; the most basic trend-following overlay.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A rolling average that smooths day-to-day noise so the underlying trend is easier to see. Every observation in the window counts equally.
    Mechanics: Sum the last n prices and divide by n; each new bar drops the oldest point and adds the newest, so the average moves through time. Used to define trend direction, support/resistance zones, and as a building block for other indicators such as Bollinger Bands and MACD.
    Failure modes: It is a lagging, trend-following measure: the longer the window the greater the lag, and in sideways, trendless markets price crosses it repeatedly, producing whipsaws.
    Misconceptions: A moving average does not predict future prices; it defines the current trend from past data. Nor is any single length (e.g. the 200-day) inherently significant beyond convention and self-reinforcing usage.
    Example: Closes of 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 give a 5-day SMA of 13; when the next close is 16, the SMA becomes (12+13+14+15+16)/5 = 14.
    SMA(n) = (P_1 + P_2 + ... + P_n) / n, where P_i are the last n prices (usually closes) and n is the window length.
    Aliases: SMA

    Exponential moving average #

    A recursively computed moving average whose weights decay geometrically with age, giving recent observations more influence and reducing lag relative to a same-length simple moving average.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Like a simple average, but today's price counts more than last week's, so it reacts faster to new information.
    Mechanics: EMA_t = k*P_t + (1-k)*EMA_{t-1} with k = 2/(n+1), seeded with an SMA. Because each value depends on all previous values, a long history (hundreds of bars) is needed for the series to converge to accurate values.
    Failure modes: Still a lagging trend-follower: less lag than an SMA means more sensitivity to noise, so it whipsaws sooner and more often in trading ranges.
    Misconceptions: An EMA is not inherently better than an SMA — it trades lag for noise sensitivity. Also, early EMA values depend on the seed, so short data histories yield inaccurate values.
    Example: A 10-day EMA applies an 18.18% weight (2/11) to the latest close; a 20-day EMA applies 9.52% (2/21).
    EMA_t = k * P_t + (1 - k) * EMA_{t-1}; k = 2/(n+1); P_t = current price; n = period.
    Aliases: EMA
    Related:

    • MACD line

    Weighted moving average #

    A moving average that weights observations linearly by recency — the newest price gets weight n, the next n-1, down to 1 — so recent data dominates while the window length stays fixed.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A compromise between the SMA and EMA: recent prices matter more, but the weights decline in a straight line rather than exponentially.
    Mechanics: Multiply each of the last n prices by its recency rank, sum the products, and divide by the sum of the weights n(n+1)/2. Recomputed fresh each bar; not recursive.
    Failure modes: Lags like all averages and whipsaws in ranges; the abrupt weight cutoff at the window edge makes it twitchy when a large old value exits the window.
    Misconceptions: A WMA is not an EMA: WMA weights decline linearly over a fixed window, whereas EMA weights decay exponentially over an effectively infinite history.
    Example: With closes 10, 11, 12, the 3-period WMA = (1*10 + 2*11 + 3*12)/6 = 68/6 = 11.33, versus an SMA of 11.
    WMA(n) = sum(i * P_i, i=1..n) / sum(i, i=1..n), with P_n the most recent price; the denominator equals n(n+1)/2.
    Aliases: WMA, Linearly weighted moving average, LWMA
    Related:
    • Simple moving average
    • Exponential moving average
    • Hull moving average
    • Triangular moving average

    Smoothed moving average #

    A recursive moving average (also called modified or running moving average) equivalent to an EMA with smoothing constant 1/n, so it smooths far more heavily than a same-length standard EMA.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The gentlest common average: each new price is absorbed at only 1/n weight, so the line moves slowly and filters a great deal of noise.
    Mechanics: Seeded with an n-period SMA; thereafter SMMA_t = (SMMA_{t-1}*(n-1) + P_t)/n. This is the smoothing Welles Wilder used for RSI, ATR, and ADX, and the average used in Bill Williams' Alligator lines.
    Failure modes: Very heavy lag; early values depend strongly on the seed and need long histories to stabilize, so short data sets give unreliable readings.
    Misconceptions: SMMA(n) is not the same as EMA(n): its alpha is 1/n rather than 2/(n+1), so a 14-period SMMA responds roughly like a 27-period standard EMA.
    Example: If the prior 14-period SMMA is 50 and the new close is 52, SMMA = (50*13 + 52)/14 = 50.14.
    SMMA_t = (SMMA_{t-1} * (n - 1) + P_t) / n; first value = SMA of the first n prices.
    Aliases: SMMA, Modified moving average, Running moving average, MMA

    Hull moving average #

    Alan Hull's moving average (2005), engineered to nearly eliminate lag while retaining smoothness by combining weighted moving averages of different lengths with a lag-canceling correction term.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: An average designed to hug price closely without becoming jagged — fast like a short WMA, smooth like a long one.
    Mechanics: Compute WMA(n/2) and WMA(n) of price, form the raw series 2*WMA(n/2) - WMA(n) to cancel lag, then smooth the result with a WMA of length sqrt(n), rounding non-integer periods to whole numbers.
    Failure modes: Reduced lag means reduced noise filtering: it whipsaws in sideways markets and remains a trend-follower, not a predictor.
    Misconceptions: Hull himself advises against HMA crossover systems — crossovers exploit lag differences that the HMA was built to remove; use turns in the HMA's direction instead.
    Example: For an 11-period HMA, use WMA(6) and WMA(11) for the raw line, then smooth with a 3-period WMA (sqrt(11) = 3.3 rounds to 3).
    HMA(n) = WMA( 2*WMA(P, n/2) - WMA(P, n), sqrt(n) ), where WMA(P, m) is the m-period weighted moving average of price.
    Aliases: HMA
    Related:

    Triangular moving average #

    A double-smoothed simple moving average — an SMA of an SMA — whose effective weights form a triangle that peaks at the middle of the window, giving mid-window data the most influence.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Averaging an average: extra smooth at the cost of extra lag, with the middle of the window weighted most.
    Mechanics: TMA(n) = SMA( SMA(P, m1), m2 ) with m1 = ceil((n+1)/2) and m2 = floor((n+1)/2); equivalently a single convolution with triangular weights 1, 2, ..., m, ..., 2, 1.
    Failure modes: Twice the smoothing means roughly twice the lag of its component SMA, so it is poor for timing and usable mainly as a slow trend reference.
    Misconceptions: Despite the peaked weighting it is not centered in the signal sense — it uses past data only and still lags; a true centered moving average uses future data and cannot be computed in real time.
    Example: A 5-period TMA = SMA(SMA(P, 3), 3); if the last three 3-period SMA values are 10, 11, 12, the TMA is 11.
    TMA(n) = SMA( SMA(P, ceil((n+1)/2)), floor((n+1)/2) ).
    Aliases: TMA
    Related:
    • Simple moving average
    • Weighted moving average
    Source: Triangular Moving Average - Tulip Indicators — Description

    Variable index dynamic average #

    Tushar Chande's Variable Index Dynamic Average: an exponential-style moving average whose smoothing constant is scaled each bar by a volatility index — the absolute Chande Momentum Oscillator — so it reacts faster in decisive, volatile markets and slower in quiet ones.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: An adaptive average that automatically speeds up when the market is moving decisively and slows down when it is drifting.
    Mechanics: VIDYA_t = VIDYA_{t-1} + alpha * |CMO_t|/100 * (P_t - VIDYA_{t-1}), with alpha = 2/(n+1). When |CMO| nears 100 (one-way market) VIDYA behaves like a fast EMA; when CMO nears 0 it barely moves.
    Failure modes: In choppy markets CMO hovers near zero and VIDYA effectively freezes, missing genuine moves; results are sensitive to the CMO look-back choice.
    Misconceptions: VIDYA is not the same as KAMA: KAMA adapts via an efficiency ratio (net change over path length), while VIDYA adapts via a momentum-volatility index.
    Example: If alpha = 0.2 and the CMO reads +60, the effective smoothing constant is 0.12; at CMO = +10 it falls to 0.02.
    VIDYA_t = VIDYA_{t-1} + alpha * (|CMO_t|/100) * (P_t - VIDYA_{t-1}); alpha = 2/(n+1); CMO = 100*(Su - Sd)/(Su + Sd) with Su, Sd the sums of up and down close-to-close changes over the look-back.
    Aliases: VIDYA
    Related:
    • Exponential moving average
    • Kaufman adaptive moving average
    • Chande momentum oscillator

    Kaufman adaptive moving average #

    Perry Kaufman's adaptive moving average, which varies its smoothing constant with an Efficiency Ratio measuring how directional (versus noisy) recent price movement has been, hugging price in trends and going flat in chop.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: An average that asks how much of the recent movement is real trend versus random wiggle, and speeds up only when the answer is mostly trend.
    Mechanics: ER = |Close - Close_n| / sum|Close - prior Close| over n bars (0 to 1); SC = [ER*(fast - slow) + slow]^2 with fast = 2/(2+1) and slow = 2/(30+1); KAMA_t = KAMA_{t-1} + SC*(P_t - KAMA_{t-1}). Recommended settings KAMA(10,2,30); the first value is seeded with an SMA.
    Failure modes: A simple price/KAMA crossover system still whipsaws without price or time filters, and in persistently choppy markets ER stays low, leaving KAMA flat and uninformative.
    Misconceptions: Lengthening the ER look-back does not necessarily improve the indicator — the documented guidance is to adjust the fast (middle) parameter for smoothing, not the ER period.
    Example: If price rises for 10 straight bars, change equals volatility so ER = 1 and KAMA tracks a 2-period EMA; if price ends unchanged after 10 choppy bars, ER is near 0 and KAMA barely moves.
    ER = |P_t - P_{t-n}| / sum(|P_i - P_{i-1}|, i=t-n+1..t); SC = [ER*(2/3 - 2/31) + 2/31]^2; KAMA_t = KAMA_{t-1} + SC*(P_t - KAMA_{t-1}).
    Aliases: KAMA
    Related:

    Zero-lag exponential moving average #

    A lag-corrected EMA (John Ehlers and Ric Way, 'Zero Lag (Well, Almost)', TASC 2010): an EMA applied to de-lagged data — price plus the difference between the current price and the price (n-1)/2 bars ago — compensating for the EMA's inherent lag.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Pre-correct the data for the lag you know the EMA will add, so the smoothed output lands close to the current price.
    Mechanics: Form the adjusted series Z_t = P_t + (P_t - P_{t-lag}) with lag = floor((n-1)/2), then take a standard n-period EMA of Z. The construction is related to Mulloy's DEMA/TEMA lag-cancellation idea.
    Failure modes: The de-lagging term amplifies noise and overshoots on sharp reversals; in sideways markets it whipsaws more than a plain EMA.
    Misconceptions: Zero lag is aspirational: the correction removes the systematic EMA lag in smooth trends but cannot anticipate turning points, where lag remains.
    Example: For n = 10, lag = 4; if P_t = 105 and P_{t-4} = 100, the EMA is applied to 110 instead of 105, pulling the average up toward the trending price.
    lag = floor((n-1)/2); Z_t = P_t + (P_t - P_{t-lag}); ZLEMA_t = EMA_n(Z)_t with k = 2/(n+1).
    Aliases: ZLEMA, Zero-lag EMA
    Related:
    • Exponential moving average
    • Double exponential moving average
    • Hull moving average
    Source: Zero-Lag Exponential Moving Average - Tulip Indicators — Description

    Double exponential moving average #

    Patrick Mulloy's Double Exponential Moving Average (Technical Analysis of Stocks & Commodities, January 1994): a composite of a single and a double EMA that subtracts the extra lag of the double-smoothed series from the single-smoothed one to track price more closely.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Measure how much double-smoothing lags single-smoothing, and add that gap back to the fast EMA as a correction.
    Mechanics: EMA1 = EMA(price), EMA2 = EMA(EMA1); DEMA = 2*EMA1 - EMA2. The double-smoothed EMA2 serves only as a lag-correction term, not as the output. Default 20 periods; signals via price/line crosses or fast/slow DEMA crossovers, which occur earlier than equivalent EMA crossovers.
    Failure modes: Greater responsiveness means more noise and more false signals in choppy markets; long-term investors may be better served by a traditional, smoother average.
    Misconceptions: DEMA is not simply the EMA of an EMA — that double-smoothed series alone would lag more, not less; the output is 2*EMA1 - EMA2.
    Example: If EMA1 = 50 and EMA2 = 48, DEMA = 2*50 - 48 = 52, pulling the average above EMA1 toward current price.
    DEMA = 2*EMA_n(P) - EMA_n(EMA_n(P)).
    Aliases: DEMA
    Related:
    • Exponential moving average
    • Triple exponential moving average
    • Zero-lag exponential moving average
    Source: Double Exponential Moving Average (DEMA) | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com — Calculation

    Triple exponential moving average #

    Patrick Mulloy's Triple Exponential Moving Average, which extends DEMA with a third smoothing layer, combining single, double, and triple EMAs to remove even more lag for short-term trading.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: DEMA with one more correction term — closer still to price, noisier still.
    Mechanics: TEMA = 3*EMA1 - 3*EMA2 + EMA3, where EMA2 = EMA(EMA1) and EMA3 = EMA(EMA2). Used via price crosses or fast/slow TEMA crossovers, which fire earlier than equivalent DEMA or EMA crosses; default 20 periods.
    Failure modes: Minimal lag means minimal noise filtering — it whipsaws the most of the EMA family in sideways markets, and some lag is actually useful for longer-term analysis.
    Misconceptions: Faster is not universally better: TEMA's responsiveness amplifies noise, so it is not a straight upgrade over EMA or DEMA. Also, TEMA is not TRIX — TRIX is the 1-period rate of change of a triple EMA.
    Example: If EMA1 = 50, EMA2 = 48, EMA3 = 46, TEMA = 3*50 - 3*48 + 46 = 52.
    TEMA = 3*EMA_n(P) - 3*EMA_n(EMA_n(P)) + EMA_n(EMA_n(EMA_n(P))).
    Aliases: TEMA
    Related:
    • Double exponential moving average
    • Exponential moving average
    • TRIX

    Moving average crossover #

    A signal framework in which a shorter-period moving average crossing a longer-period one (or price crossing a moving average) is read as a shift in trend direction; John Murphy calls the two-average version the double crossover method. It is an analytical convention, not a proven predictive law.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: When the fast average overtakes the slow one, recent prices have moved enough to bend the trend measure — a simple, mechanical trend-change rule.
    Mechanics: Bullish signal: the short MA crosses above the long MA; bearish: short crosses below. Common pairs are 5/35 (short-term) and 50/200 (long-term); a triple-crossover variant uses three averages. MACD(12,26,1) quantifies EMA crossovers as the fast-minus-slow difference.
    Failure modes: Both averages lag, so signals are late by construction and whipsaw repeatedly in trendless markets; the documented Home Depot example shows three whipsaws before one successful 20%+ signal.
    Misconceptions: A crossover describes what has already happened; it does not predict continuation. Unfiltered crossover systems are unreliable — price or time filters are typically needed.
    Example: A 10-day EMA crossing above a 50-day EMA is a bullish crossover; with MACD(10,50,1), the MACD line crossing zero marks the same event.
    Aliases: Double crossover method, MA crossover
    Related:
    • Golden cross
    • Death cross
    • Simple moving average
    • Moving average convergence divergence
    Source: Moving Averages - Simple and Exponential | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com — Double Moving Average Crossovers

    Golden cross #

    A bullish moving-average crossover — classically the 50-day SMA crossing above the 200-day SMA — interpreted by technicians as confirmation of a long-term uptrend. It is a widely watched analytical convention, not a proven predictive law.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The market's medium-term average climbing above its long-term average suggests that upward momentum has been sustained long enough to matter.
    Mechanics: Identified on daily charts when the shorter (typically 50-day) moving average crosses up through the longer (typically 200-day) one; because both averages lag, the cross usually prints well after the actual low.
    Failure modes: Late by construction and whipsaw-prone in range-bound markets; by the time a golden cross prints, much of the advance has often already occurred.
    Misconceptions: It is not a guaranteed buy signal or a law of markets — it records that an advance has been large and persistent; any forward-looking edge is an empirical question, not a property of the construction.
    Example: MACD(50,200,1) turning positive marks the same 50/200 EMA golden cross in oscillator form.

    Death cross #

    A bearish moving-average crossover — classically the 50-day SMA crossing below the 200-day SMA — read as confirmation of a long-term downtrend; the mirror image of the golden cross and equally a convention rather than a proven law.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The medium-term average sinking below the long-term average suggests the decline has been deep and persistent enough to bend long-term trend measures.
    Mechanics: The shorter (typically 50-day) moving average crosses down through the longer (typically 200-day) one; like the golden cross, it lags and typically prints well after the actual top.
    Failure modes: Late and whipsaw-prone in sideways markets; many historical death crosses have occurred near market lows rather than before extended declines.
    Misconceptions: A death cross does not forecast a crash — it records that a decline has already been large enough to pull the 50-day average below the 200-day.
    Example: MACD(50,200,1) turning negative marks the same 50/200 EMA death cross in oscillator form.
    Aliases: Dead cross

    Moving average convergence divergence #

    Gerald Appel's Moving Average Convergence/Divergence oscillator (late 1970s): a momentum indicator built from the difference between a fast and a slow EMA (default 12 and 26) with a 9-period EMA signal line, combining trend-following and momentum information in one unbounded oscillator.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: When the fast average pulls away from the slow one, momentum is accelerating; when they converge, the move is running out of steam.
    Mechanics: MACD line = EMA(12) - EMA(26) of closes; signal line = 9-period EMA of the MACD line; histogram = line minus signal. Signals include signal-line crossovers, zero-line (centerline) crossovers — equivalent to the underlying EMA crossover — and divergences with price.
    Failure modes: Signal-line crossovers whipsaw in flat or volatile markets, and crossovers at extremes often mark momentum exhaustion rather than reversal; being unbounded, MACD cannot flag overbought or oversold conditions.
    Misconceptions: MACD is not a bounded oscillator, so high readings are not inherently overbought; nor does a signal-line cross guarantee a trend change.
    Example: If the 12-day EMA is 102 and the 26-day EMA is 100, MACD = +2; with a 9-day signal line at 1.5, the histogram reads +0.5.
    MACD = EMA_12(Close) - EMA_26(Close); Signal = EMA_9(MACD); Histogram = MACD - Signal.
    Aliases: MACD
    Related:

    MACD line #

    The core series of the MACD: the fast (default 12-period) EMA minus the slow (default 26-period) EMA of closing prices, oscillating around zero as the two averages converge and diverge.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The spread between a fast and a slow average: positive and widening means upside momentum is building.
    Mechanics: Computed each bar as EMA_12(C) - EMA_26(C); it crosses zero exactly when the two EMAs cross, and its distance from zero measures how far the averages have diverged.
    Failure modes: As an EMA spread it lags and whipsaws in trendless markets; absolute values are price-scale dependent and not comparable across securities (use the PPO for that).
    Misconceptions: A falling MACD line is not the same as falling prices — the EMA spread can shrink while prices are still rising, reflecting decelerating momentum only.
    Example: A 12-day EMA of 50 and a 26-day EMA of 48 give a MACD line of +2: the fast average sits 2 points above the slow one.
    MACD line = EMA_12(Close) - EMA_26(Close).
    Aliases: Fast MACD line
    Related:
    • Moving average convergence divergence
    • MACD signal line
    • MACD histogram
    • Moving average crossover
    Source: MACD (Moving Average Convergence/Divergence Oscillator) | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com — How Do You Calculate the MACD?

    MACD signal line #

    A 9-period EMA of the MACD line, plotted alongside it; crossovers of the MACD line through this signal line are the most common MACD trading signal.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A moving average of the MACD itself, smoothing its turns so crossovers are easier to read.
    Mechanics: Signal_t = EMA_9(MACD line). A bullish crossover occurs when the MACD line rises above the signal line, bearish when it falls below; because it trails the MACD line, it adds a further layer of lag.
    Failure modes: Adds lag to an already lagging construction, and crossovers occurring at positive or negative extremes often mark exhaustion and reverse quickly.
    Misconceptions: The 9-period setting is a convention, not an optimum, and the signal line has no meaning independently of the MACD line it smooths.
    Example: If the MACD line is 2.0 and its 9-period EMA is 1.5, the MACD sits above its signal line — a post-crossover bullish state.
    Signal = EMA_9(MACD), where MACD = EMA_12(Close) - EMA_26(Close).
    Aliases: Signal line
    Related:
    • MACD line
    • MACD histogram
    • Moving average convergence divergence
    Source: MACD (Moving Average Convergence/Divergence Oscillator) | ChartSchool | StockCharts.com — Signal Line Crossovers

    MACD histogram #

    The bar plot of the MACD line minus its signal line, visualizing the distance between them; it expands and contracts ahead of crossovers and is used to spot momentum shifts early.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Instead of waiting for the two MACD lines to cross, watch the gap between them shrink or grow.
    Mechanics: Histogram = MACD - Signal; positive when MACD is above its signal line, negative below. It crosses zero exactly at a signal-line crossover, and its peaks and troughs precede the crossovers themselves.
    Failure modes: More sensitive than the lines themselves, so it flips sign more often in choppy markets; a shrinking histogram warns of a crossover but says nothing about whether that crossover will matter.
    Misconceptions: A declining histogram does not mean prices are falling — only that the MACD's lead over its signal line is narrowing.
    Example: MACD = 2.0 with signal = 1.5 gives a histogram of +0.5; if the next bar's MACD is 1.8 and signal 1.6, the histogram shrinks to +0.2, warning that upside momentum is fading.
    Histogram = MACD line - Signal line = (EMA_12(C) - EMA_26(C)) - EMA_9(MACD).
    Aliases: MACD-Histogram
    Related:
    • MACD line

    Relative strength index #

    J. Welles Wilder's momentum oscillator (New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems, 1978) comparing smoothed average gains to smoothed average losses over a look-back period (default 14), bounded between 0 and 100.
    Intuition: A speedometer for price: it shows how persistently and strongly the market has been closing up versus down.
    Mechanics: RS = Average Gain / Average Loss, with first values simple 14-period means and subsequent values Wilder-smoothed ((prior*13 + current)/14); RSI = 100 - 100/(1+RS). Wilder treated readings above 70 as overbought and below 30 as oversold; also used for divergences, failure swings, and centerline analysis.
    Failure modes: In strong trends RSI can stay above 70 or below 30 for extended periods, so fading overbought/oversold readings fails; bearish divergences can recur for months before any reversal.
    Misconceptions: Overbought does not mean the price must fall — it can signal strength. RSI is also not a comparison between two securities; that is relative strength (price relative).
    Example: If the 14-day average gain is 1.0 and the average loss is 0.5, RS = 2 and RSI = 100 - 100/3 = 66.7.
    RSI = 100 - 100/(1 + RS); RS = AvgGain / AvgLoss; AvgGain_t = (AvgGain_{t-1}*13 + Gain_t)/14 (likewise losses, expressed as positive values).

    Stochastic oscillator #

    George Lane's momentum indicator (late 1950s) measuring where the close sits within the high-low range of the look-back period, bounded 0-100 and published in Fast, Slow, and Full versions. Lane's premise: momentum changes direction before price.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: In an uptrend prices tend to close near the top of their recent range; the stochastic quantifies that tendency.
    Mechanics: %K = (C - LL)/(HH - LL)*100 over (default) 14 periods; %D = 3-period SMA of %K acts as the signal line; the Slow version smooths %K once more. Readings above 80 are overbought, below 20 oversold; signals come from %K/%D crosses, divergences, and Lane's bull/bear set-ups.
    Failure modes: Can remain overbought or oversold through an entire strong trend, so counter-trend signals fail repeatedly; fast versions whipsaw, and signals work best in trading ranges or zigzag trends.
    Misconceptions: The stochastic does not follow price or volume — per Lane it follows the speed (momentum) of price; and an overbought reading alone is not a sell signal.
    Example: With a 14-day high of 110, low of 100, and close of 108, %K = (108-100)/(110-100)*100 = 80.
    %K = 100 * (C - LL_n)/(HH_n - LL_n); %D = SMA_3(%K); HH_n and LL_n are the highest high and lowest low over n periods.

    Stochastic RSI #

    Chande and Kroll's oscillator (The New Technical Trader, 1994) that applies the stochastic formula to RSI values instead of price, measuring where the current RSI sits within its own high-low range over the look-back period; an indicator of an indicator, bounded between 0 and 1.
    Intuition: RSI can drift between 80 and 20 for weeks without reaching its classic extremes; StochRSI restores sensitivity by tracking RSI's position within its recent range rather than its absolute level.
    Mechanics: Compute n-period RSI, then take (RSI - lowest RSI over n) / (highest RSI over n - lowest RSI over n), with n typically 14. A value of 1 means RSI is at its n-period high, 0 at its low; some platforms scale it to 0-100 and smooth it with short moving averages.
    Failure modes: Two transformations removed from price, it is very jumpy and whipsaws frequently; in strong trends it pins at 0 or 1 for long stretches, issuing repeated false reversal alerts.
    Misconceptions: An overbought StochRSI reading is not a sell signal by itself - Chande and Kroll used 0.80/0.20 extremes within a trend context, and readings can stay extreme while the trend continues.
    Example: If 14-day RSI has ranged from 30 to 70 over the last 14 days and current RSI is 50, StochRSI = (50-30)/(70-30) = 0.5.
    StochRSI = (RSI - min(RSI, n)) / (max(RSI, n) - min(RSI, n)), with RSI and the range window typically both 14 periods.

    Williams percent R #

    Larry Williams' momentum oscillator showing where the close sits relative to the highest high of the look-back period, plotted on an inverted 0 to -100 scale; mathematically the inverse of the fast stochastic %K line.
    Intuition: It answers 'how close is the latest close to the top of the recent range?': readings near 0 mean closes at the range top, readings near -100 mean closes at the bottom.
    Mechanics: Subtract the close from the highest high of the last n periods (default 14), divide by the high-low range, and multiply by -100. Readings from 0 to -20 are conventionally overbought, -80 to -100 oversold; the -50 centerline and momentum failures (failed retests of -20) give additional signals.
    Failure modes: Like all range-position oscillators it sticks at extremes during strong trends and whipsaws across the -50 centerline in choppy markets.
    Misconceptions: Overbought (above -20) does not mean sell: sustained closes near the top of the range signal persistent buying pressure, and %R can stay overbought through an entire uptrend.
    Example: Highest high 110, lowest low 100, close 103: %R = ((110-103)/10) x -100 = -70; a close at 108 gives -20.
    %R = ((HighestHigh(n) - Close) / (HighestHigh(n) - LowestLow(n))) x -100, default n = 14.

    Rate of change #

    A pure momentum oscillator measuring the percentage change between the current close and the close n periods ago, plotted as an unbounded line around zero; the StockCharts ROC page notes the indicator is also referred to simply as Momentum.
    Intuition: It is rise over run for prices: positive while the price is higher than n periods ago, negative while lower, with distance from zero showing how fast the move is.
    Mechanics: Compute (Close - Close n periods ago) / (Close n periods ago) x 100, default n = 12. Used for zero-line trend bias, security-specific overbought/oversold extremes (often +/-10%), and divergences.
    Failure modes: Zero-line crosses whipsaw in ranges, and divergences fail often because strong trends frequently start with the sharpest ROC spike, printing a bearish divergence mid-advance.
    Misconceptions: A falling ROC is not a falling price - price still rises as long as ROC is positive; it only means the advance is decelerating.
    Example: Close today 55, close 12 days ago 50: ROC = (55-50)/50 x 100 = +10%.
    ROC(n) = ((C_t - C_{t-n}) / C_{t-n}) x 100, where C_t is the current close.
    Aliases: ROC, Price rate of change, PROC

    Momentum indicator #

    The simplest momentum measure: the absolute point difference between the current close and the close n periods ago, plotted as an unbounded oscillator around zero; the unscaled sibling of the rate-of-change, which expresses the same comparison as a percentage.
    Intuition: It tracks how many points price gained or lost over the window, ignoring the price level, so its slope shows acceleration or deceleration of the move.
    Mechanics: Compute Close - Close(n periods ago), conventionally with n around 10-14. Positive values show net gains over the window, negative values net losses; zero-line crosses define the momentum bias.
    Failure modes: Being unscaled in points, readings are not comparable across securities or across large price changes in one security; like ROC it whipsaws around zero in sideways markets.
    Misconceptions: Momentum indicators describe past speed; a high positive reading can persist or reverse without warning and is not a prediction of continued gains.
    Example: Close today 55, close 12 days ago 50: momentum = +5 points, whereas the rate of change is +10%.
    Momentum(n) = C_t - C_{t-n}; the percentage variant is ROC(n) = ((C_t - C_{t-n}) / C_{t-n}) x 100.
    Aliases: MTM, Momentum (points)

    Commodity channel index #

    Donald Lambert's oscillator (Commodities magazine, 1980) measuring how far the typical price has deviated from its moving average, expressed in units of mean absolute deviation scaled by 0.015 so that roughly 70-80% of readings fall between -100 and +100.
    Intuition: CCI normalizes 'how stretched is price from its own average?' - excursions beyond +/-100 mark statistically unusual strength or weakness that can flag a new trend or an extreme.
    Mechanics: Compute the typical price TP = (H+L+C)/3, subtract its n-period SMA (default 20), and divide by 0.015 times the mean absolute deviation of TP from that SMA. Used for +/-100 trend-emergence signals, +/-200 extremes, and confirmed divergences.
    Failure modes: CCI is unbounded, so overbought/oversold levels are arbitrary and must be calibrated per security; zero-line crosses whipsaw, and in strong trends CCI stays beyond +/-100 while price keeps running.
    Misconceptions: Despite the name it is not commodity-specific, and +/-100 is a statistical convention, not a hard ceiling - the oscillator routinely exceeds it.
    Example: TP = 24, 20-period SMA of TP = 22, mean deviation 1.33: CCI = (24-22)/(0.015 x 1.33) ~ +100.
    CCI = (TP - SMA(TP, n)) / (0.015 x MeanDev), TP = (High + Low + Close)/3, MeanDev = (1/n) x sum of |TP_i - SMA(TP, n)| over the last n periods.
    Aliases: CCI
