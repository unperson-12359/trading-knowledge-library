    Average directional index reviewed #

    Welles Wilder's trend-strength gauge (New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems, 1978): the Wilder-smoothed average of the Directional Movement Index (DX), which measures the spread between +DI and -DI relative to their sum; ADX quantifies trend strength on a 0-100 scale regardless of direction.
    Intuition: ADX answers 'is the market trending at all?', not which way - Wilder suggested readings above 25 indicate a strong trend and below 20 the absence of one.
    Mechanics: Compute +DM, -DM and True Range, smooth each with Wilder's technique over 14 periods, derive +DI and -DI, then DX = 100 x |+DI - -DI| / (+DI + -DI); ADX is the 14-period Wilder-smoothed average of DX. Roughly 150 periods of data are needed for stable values.
    Failure modes: Heavy double smoothing makes ADX very laggy - it often confirms a trend only near its end; it cannot distinguish up from down, and in flat markets it offers no usable signal.
    Misconceptions: A rising ADX does not mean prices are rising - ADX also rises in strong downtrends; and a falling ADX means the trend is weakening, not that it has reversed.
    Example: If +DI = 32 and -DI = 12, DX = 100 x 20/44 ~ 45.5; sustained DX values near this level push ADX above 25, Wilder's strong-trend threshold.
    DX = 100 x |+DI14 - -DI14| / (+DI14 + -DI14); first ADX = 14-period average of DX, then ADX_t = ((ADX_{t-1} x 13) + DX_t) / 14.
    Aliases: ADX, Average directional movement index

    Positive directional indicator reviewed #

    The positive half of Wilder's Directional Movement System: 100 times the ratio of smoothed positive directional movement (upward range expansion beyond the prior bar's high) to smoothed True Range over 14 periods, measuring the strength of upside directional movement.
    Intuition: +DI tracks how much of the recent true-range movement was upside expansion; when +DI is above -DI, buyers hold the directional edge.
    Mechanics: +DM = current high minus prior high when that exceeds the prior-low-minus-current-low move and is positive, else 0 (inside bars give 0). Smooth +DM and TR with Wilder's method; +DI = 100 x smoothed +DM / smoothed TR. Crosses of +DI above -DI are buy signals, conventionally filtered by ADX above 20-25.
    Failure modes: Inside bars force +DM to zero and +DI/-DI crosses whipsaw badly in flat markets; Wilder's smoothing lag means crosses often come well after the move began.
    Misconceptions: +DI measures relative directional movement, not the probability of further gains; a +DI > -DI cross without trend strength (ADX) is a weak signal - StockCharts notes many such crosses invalidate.
    Example: Today's high 52 vs prior 50 (+2) while lows moved down only 0.5: +DM = 2; if the smoothed +DM is 20 against a smoothed TR of 100, +DI = 20.
    +DI14 = 100 x (Wilder-smoothed +DM14) / (Wilder-smoothed TR14); +DM = max(High_t - High_{t-1}, 0) if High_t - High_{t-1} > Low_{t-1} - Low_t, else 0.
    Aliases: +DI, Plus DI, PDI

    Negative directional indicator reviewed #

    The negative half of Wilder's Directional Movement System: 100 times the ratio of smoothed negative directional movement (downward range expansion beyond the prior bar's low) to smoothed True Range over 14 periods, measuring the strength of downside directional movement.
    Intuition: -DI tracks how much of the recent true-range movement was downside expansion; when -DI is above +DI, sellers hold the directional edge.
    Mechanics: -DM = prior low minus current low when that exceeds the current-high-minus-prior-high move and is positive, else 0. Smooth -DM and TR with Wilder's method; -DI = 100 x smoothed -DM / smoothed TR. A cross of -DI above +DI signals downside control, conventionally filtered by ADX.
    Failure modes: Same whipsaw and lag problems as +DI; volatile outside bars make the pair oscillate noisily, and many crosses fail without an ADX trend filter.
    Misconceptions: A high -DI is not inherently bearish for future prices - it describes recent downside movement, and sharp declines can exhaust themselves exactly when -DI peaks.
    Example: Prior low 48, current low 45 (-3) while highs rose only 1: -DM = 3; if smoothed -DM is 30 against smoothed TR of 100, -DI = 30, above a +DI of 20 - sellers in control.
    -DI14 = 100 x (Wilder-smoothed -DM14) / (Wilder-smoothed TR14); -DM = max(Low_{t-1} - Low_t, 0) if Low_{t-1} - Low_t > High_t - High_{t-1}, else 0.
    Aliases: -DI, Minus DI, NDI, MDI

    Average true range reviewed #

    Welles Wilder's volatility measure (New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems, 1978): the Wilder-smoothed average of True Range, where True Range is the greatest of the current high-low span, |high - prior close|, and |low - prior close| - capturing gap and limit-move volatility that a plain high-low range misses.
    Intuition: ATR tells you how much an instrument typically moves per bar, with no directional content: it expands in violent markets and contracts in quiet ones.
    Mechanics: TR_t = max(High - Low, |High - Close_prev|, |Low - Close_prev|). The first 14-period ATR is the SMA of the first 14 TR values; thereafter ATR_t = (ATR_{t-1} x 13 + TR_t) / 14. Used for stop distances, position sizing, and breakout validation.
    Failure modes: ATR is in absolute price units, so values are not comparable across securities or across large price changes in one security; the recursive Wilder smoothing makes early values seed-dependent and needs long histories (StockCharts calculates back 250+ periods) to stabilize.
    Misconceptions: ATR says nothing about direction - a falling ATR during a rally is not bearish, just quietening; and a high ATR is not by itself a top signal.
    Example: StockCharts' spreadsheet: the first 14-day ATR comes out at 0.56, and ATR surged as QQQ plunged with long candlesticks in May.
    TR = max(H - L, |H - C_{t-1}|, |L - C_{t-1}|); ATR(14)_t = (ATR_{t-1} x 13 + TR_t) / 14.
    Aliases: ATR

    Bollinger Bands reviewed #

    John Bollinger's volatility bands: a 20-period simple moving average flanked by upper and lower bands set k standard deviations (default 2) of price above and below it, so the envelope widens as volatility rises and contracts as it falls.
    Intuition: The bands define 'relatively high' and 'relatively low' statistically: price at the upper band is high relative to recent volatility, at the lower band relatively low - without implying a reversal by itself.
    Mechanics: Middle band = SMA(20); upper = middle + k x sigma(20), lower = middle - k x sigma(20), where sigma is the 20-period standard deviation of price (same window as the SMA). Bollinger suggests k = 2.1 for 50-period and 1.9 for 10-period settings. Used for band tags, M-top/W-bottom confirmation, and the Squeeze via BandWidth.
    Failure modes: Band touches are 'tags', not signals: in strong trends price walks the band for long stretches, and the SMA base lags; squeezes flag volatility compression but give no direction, and post-squeeze breaks can be head-fakes.
    Misconceptions: A tag of the upper band is not an automatic sell - 'relatively high' is not 'about to fall'; nor do the bands contain a normal distribution's 95% of prices, since returns are not normal (Bollinger cites 88-89% empirically).
    Example: SMA(20) = 50 with sigma = 1.5: upper band = 53, lower = 47; a close at 53.4 is above the band - a tag, not a signal.
    Middle = SMA(P, 20); Upper = Middle + k x StdDev(P, 20); Lower = Middle - k x StdDev(P, 20); k = 2 by default.
    Aliases: BB, Bollinger Bands (20,2)

    Bollinger bandwidth reviewed #

    A Bollinger-derived indicator measuring the percentage width between the upper and lower bands relative to the middle band, tracking volatility expansion and contraction on a normalized scale comparable across securities and timeframes.
    Intuition: It turns the visual 'pinch' of the bands into a number: falling BandWidth means volatility is drying up; the famous Squeeze is BandWidth at the low end of its own historical range.
    Mechanics: Compute (Upper - Lower) / Middle x 100 from the Bollinger Bands (default 20, 2). Narrowness is judged against the indicator's own 6-12 month range; the Squeeze alerts to coming volatility, and the direction comes from the subsequent band break.
    Failure modes: 'Narrow' is relative to each security's own history, so thresholds do not transfer between instruments; the Squeeze is directionless and the first band break after a squeeze can fail.
    Misconceptions: A low BandWidth does not guarantee an imminent big move - volatility can stay compressed for months, and low-volatility securities always show lower BandWidth than volatile ones.
    Example: Upper 53, lower 47, middle 50: BandWidth = (53-47)/50 x 100 = 12; StockCharts' Alaska Airlines squeeze triggered below a BandWidth of 10.
    BandWidth = ((UpperBand - LowerBand) / MiddleBand) x 100.
    Aliases: BandWidth, BBW, Bollinger BandWidth

    Percent B reviewed #

    A Bollinger-derived indicator expressing the close's position within the bands as a percentage of band width: 100 at the upper band, 0 at the lower band, 50 at the middle band, and beyond those values when price is outside the bands.
    Intuition: %B states exactly where price sits inside the Bollinger envelope, making band tags, divergences and pattern confirmations quantifiable and comparable across securities.
    Mechanics: Compute (Price - LowerBand) / (UpperBand - LowerBand) x 100 using the standard (20, 2) bands. Values above 100 mean closes above the upper band, below 0 below the lower band; used for walking-the-bands trend confirmation and W-bottom/M-top pattern work.
    Failure modes: Inherits the Bollinger SMA's lag, and readings above 100 can persist through an entire uptrend, so extremes are weak standalone timing signals.
    Misconceptions: %B above 100 is not an automatic overbought-sell reading - upper-band tags in uptrends indicate strength; interpretation depends on trend and pattern context.
    Example: Bands 47-53 with a close at 50: %B = (50-47)/(53-47) x 100 = 50; a close at 53.6 gives %B = 110.
    %B = ((P - LowerBand) / (UpperBand - LowerBand)) x 100, with bands from Bollinger Bands(20, 2) by default.
    Aliases: %B, Percent b, Bollinger %B

    Keltner Channel reviewed #

    Volatility envelopes around an exponential moving average, with channel lines set a multiple of Average True Range above and below the EMA; the modern version popularized by Linda Raschke replaces Chester Keltner's 1960 10-day typical-price SMA plus/minus high-low range with EMA plus/minus ATR.
    Intuition: Like Bollinger Bands but built from ATR instead of standard deviation, Keltner Channels are smoother and more constant in width, which suits trend identification and breakout detection.
    Mechanics: Middle line = EMA(20); upper = EMA + m x ATR(10), lower = EMA - m x ATR(10), with m = 2 by default. A close outside the channel signals extraordinary strength or weakness; a flat channel marks a range where the lines serve as overbought/oversold references.
    Failure modes: Trend-following at heart, it lags and whipsaws in flat markets; original (SMA +/- range) and modern (EMA +/- ATR) versions differ, so values are not comparable across platforms without checking the convention.
    Misconceptions: A close beyond the channel is not an automatic fade - it more often marks the start of a trend move than an exhaustion.
    Example: EMA(20) = 100 and ATR(10) = 2: upper channel = 104, lower = 96; a close at 105 is an upside channel breakout.
    Upper = EMA(P, 20) + 2 x ATR(10); Lower = EMA(P, 20) - 2 x ATR(10).
    Aliases: Keltner Channels, KC

    Donchian Channel reviewed #

    Richard Donchian's price channel plotting the highest high and lowest low of the last n periods (commonly 20) as upper and lower bands, with an optional midline at their average; the basis of classic breakout systems such as the Turtle Traders' rules.
    Intuition: The channel shows the extremes of the recent trading range; a close outside it is by definition a new n-period high or low, which breakout traders treat as trend initiation.
    Mechanics: Upper = max(High, n); Lower = min(Low, n); Middle = (Upper + Lower)/2. Breakouts beyond the bands generate trend-following signals, and the channel width reads volatility; the bands also serve as trailing stop and support/resistance references.
    Failure modes: False breakouts are common in sideways markets where price repeatedly pokes the bands; as a purely lagging construction it gives no warning before the breakout occurs.
    Misconceptions: Touching the upper band is not 'overbought' - it is a new n-period high, the exact condition trend-followers buy; the channel records extremes, it does not predict them.
    Example: 20-day high 105, low 95: upper band 105, lower 95, midline 100; a close at 106 is an upside breakout.
    UC = max(High, N); LC = min(Low, N); Middle = (UC + LC)/2, typically N = 20.
    Aliases: Donchian Channels, Price channel

    Standard deviation channel reviewed #

    A channel built on a least-squares linear regression line through the last n closes, with parallel upper and lower lines plotted a set number of standard deviations (typically 2) of price away from the regression line.
    Intuition: The regression line is the best-fit trend through the window and the bands mark statistically typical deviation around it - about two-thirds of observations within +/-1 sigma if deviations were normal.
    Mechanics: Fit y = a + mx to the last n closes by least squares, compute the standard deviation of closes over the window, and plot the regression line with parallel lines offset by +/-k sigma (k = 2 default). The slope gives trend direction and the width gives volatility; it generates no signals by itself.
    Failure modes: The channel is refit every bar, so historical channel lines repaint and backtests on drawn channels are unreliable; the symmetric deviation assumption fails in trending or fat-tailed markets.
    Misconceptions: Unlike Bollinger Bands the center is a regression line, not a moving average, and price does not owe a return to it - in strong trends price rides one side of the channel.
    Example: A 40-bar regression of closes gives y = 98 + 0.1x with sigma = 1.2; at the last bar the center is 102, the 2-sigma upper band 104.4 and the lower 99.6.
    Center: y(x) = a + mx from the least-squares fit over n bars; Upper = y + k x sigma, Lower = y - k x sigma, sigma = standard deviation of the n closes, k typically 2.
    Aliases: Linear regression channel, SDC

    Moving average envelope reviewed #

    Parallel bands set a fixed percentage above and below a simple or exponential moving average, creating a constant-width channel used for trend confirmation and, in flat markets, as overbought/oversold references.
    Intuition: Unlike volatility-adaptive bands, the percentage offset never changes, so a touch of the envelope always means 'price is x% stretched from its average.'
    Mechanics: Upper = MA x (1 + p), Lower = MA x (1 - p), e.g., a 20-day SMA with p = 2.5%. Parameters must be chosen per security volatility - indexes need narrow envelopes (2-3%), volatile stocks wide ones (5-10%).
    Failure modes: The fixed width does not adapt: too narrow in volatility expansions (constant false breaks) and too wide in quiet periods (no signals at all); it also inherits the base average's lag.
    Misconceptions: Envelope breaks in a strong trend are signs of strength, not fade setups; overbought/oversold logic applies mainly when the trend is flat.
    Example: A 20-day SMA of 40 with 2.5% envelopes plots the upper band at 41.0 and the lower at 39.0.
    Upper = MA(n) x (1 + p); Lower = MA(n) x (1 - p); e.g., MA = SMA(20), p = 0.025.
    Aliases: MA envelopes, Percentage envelope, Trading bands

    Parabolic SAR reviewed #

    Welles Wilder's stop-and-reverse system (New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems, 1978): a trailing stop plotted as dots below price in uptrends and above price in downtrends, accelerating toward price as the trend extends and flipping sides when price crosses it.
    Intuition: It behaves like a trailing stop that tightens the longer a trend runs - the acceleration factor pulls the dots toward price, forcing an exit when momentum stalls.
    Mechanics: SAR_{t+1} = SAR_t + AF x (EP - SAR_t) for uptrends (minus for downtrends), where EP is the trend's extreme point and AF starts at 0.02, stepping up 0.02 on each new extreme to a cap of 0.20. The SAR is clamped so it never enters the prior two bars' range; a price touch flips trend direction.
    Failure modes: Wilder himself estimated markets trend only about 30% of the time; in ranges SAR whipsaws constantly, and its always-in-the-market design has no neutral state.
    Misconceptions: SAR is a stop-and-reverse mechanism, not a prediction of reversal - a flip only says price crossed the trailing stop.
    Example: Prior SAR 48.28, extreme point 49.20, AF 0.14: new SAR = 48.13 + 0.14 x (49.20 - 48.13) = 48.28 (StockCharts' worked example).
    Rising: SAR_{t+1} = SAR_t + AF x (EP - SAR_t); Falling: SAR_{t+1} = SAR_t - AF x (SAR_t - EP); AF: start 0.02, +0.02 per new EP, max 0.20.
    Aliases: PSAR, SAR, Parabolic stop and reverse

    Supertrend reviewed #

    An ATR-based trend-following overlay (Olivier Seban, 2009) that plots a single line below price in uptrends and above it in downtrends, at a distance of multiplier x ATR from the bar midpoint, flipping sides when price closes through the line.
    Intuition: It is a volatility-adjusted trailing stop: wider when the market is volatile, tighter when calm, so ordinary noise does not stop you out but a genuine reversal does.
    Mechanics: Basic upper band = (H+L)/2 + m x ATR(n), basic lower = (H+L)/2 - m x ATR(n), with common defaults n = 10, m = 3. Final bands are ratcheted - the upper band can only fall and the lower only rise - until a close crosses the active band, which flips the trend state.
    Failure modes: Lagging by construction and whipsaw-prone in sideways markets; it signals direction only - not trend strength - and results are sensitive to the (period, multiplier) choice.
    Misconceptions: A green (uptrend) line is not a standalone buy recommendation, and the plotted line is a stop level, not a price target.
    Example: High 20.23, low 19.04, ATR 1.15, multiplier 1: upper band = 19.635 + 1.15 = 20.79, lower = 18.48 (Investopedia's worked example).
    Upper = (High + Low)/2 + Multiplier x ATR(n); Lower = (High + Low)/2 - Multiplier x ATR(n); ATR(n) = (ATR_prev x (n-1) + TR)/n (Wilder smoothing).
    Aliases: SuperTrend

    Ichimoku Kinko Hyo reviewed #

    Goichi Hosoda's 'one-look equilibrium chart' (published 1969): a five-line system of high-low midpoint averages - Conversion Line, Base Line, two Leading Spans forming the cloud (Kumo), and a Lagging Span - designed to show trend, momentum, and support/resistance at a glance.
    Intuition: Price above the cloud means uptrend, below means downtrend, inside means no trend; the cloud projected ahead sketches where support or resistance is expected to lie.
    Mechanics: Tenkan-sen = midpoint of the 9-period high-low; Kijun-sen = 26-period midpoint; Senkou A = average of the two, plotted 26 bars ahead; Senkou B = 52-period midpoint, also 26 ahead; Chikou = close plotted 26 back. Signals combine price/cloud position, cloud color (A vs B), Tenkan-Kijun crosses, and price-Kijun crosses.
    Failure modes: Midpoint averages lag like all trend tools and whipsaw in ranges; the cloud shifted 'into the future' is computed entirely from past data and is not a forecast.
    Misconceptions: Ichimoku is an analytical framework, not a proven predictive system - and the 9/26/52 defaults reflect the 1960s Japanese six-day trading week, not universal constants.
    Example: 9-day high 55, low 51: Tenkan-sen = 53; with a 26-day midpoint of 50, Senkou A plotted 26 days ahead = 51.5.
    Tenkan = (max(H,9)+min(L,9))/2; Kijun = (max(H,26)+min(L,26))/2; Senkou A = (Tenkan+Kijun)/2 shifted +26; Senkou B = (max(H,52)+min(L,52))/2 shifted +26; Chikou = Close shifted -26.
    Aliases: Ichimoku Cloud, Ichimoku
    Related:

    Tenkan-sen reviewed #

    The fastest Ichimoku line: the midpoint of the 9-period high-low range, (9-period high + 9-period low)/2, used as the short-term equilibrium level and the faster line in Ichimoku crossover signals.
    Intuition: Rather than averaging closes, it averages the range extremes, so it sits at the center of recent price action and flattens immediately when the market goes quiet.
    Mechanics: Compute (max(High,9) + min(Low,9))/2 each bar. A Tenkan cross above Kijun-sen is the classic bullish momentum signal (and below, bearish), conventionally filtered by price position relative to the cloud.
    Failure modes: As a 9-period range midpoint it moves in steps whenever the extreme bar rolls out of the window, and it whipsaws around Kijun-sen in flat markets.
    Misconceptions: It is not a 9-period moving average of closes - it tracks the range midpoint, so it behaves differently (steppy, bounded by extremes) from an SMA or EMA.
    Example: If the last 9 bars posted a highest high of 108 and a lowest low of 100, Tenkan-sen = 104.
    Tenkan-sen = (max(High, 9) + min(Low, 9)) / 2.
    Aliases: Conversion Line, Tenkan

    Kijun-sen reviewed #

    The slower Ichimoku line: the midpoint of the 26-period high-low range, serving as the medium-term equilibrium level, signal-confirmation line, and a common trailing reference.
    Intuition: It marks the recent balance point of the market; price far above it is extended, and pullbacks to a flat Kijun are watched as equilibrium tests.
    Mechanics: Compute (max(High,26) + min(Low,26))/2 each bar. Price-Kijun crosses and Tenkan-Kijun crosses generate Ichimoku momentum signals, while the Kijun's slope and flatness gauge the medium-term trend.
    Failure modes: Stepwise movement and lag; in ranges price crosses it repeatedly with no follow-through.
    Misconceptions: A touch of the Kijun is not automatically support - it is only the midpoint of the last 26 bars' range.
    Example: With a 26-bar high of 110 and low of 96, Kijun-sen = 103.
    Kijun-sen = (max(High, 26) + min(Low, 26)) / 2.
    Aliases: Base Line, Kijun

    Senkou Span A reviewed #

    The faster cloud boundary: the average of Tenkan-sen and Kijun-sen, plotted 26 periods ahead of the current bar, forming the top of the Kumo (cloud) when above Senkou Span B and the bottom when below it.
    Intuition: It averages the short and medium equilibria and projects them forward, marking the near edge of expected future support or resistance.
    Mechanics: Senkou Span A = (Tenkan-sen + Kijun-sen)/2, shifted 26 bars forward. The cloud is bullish (green) when Span A is above Span B and bearish (red) when below; an A-B cross changes cloud color, an ebb-and-flow trend signal.
    Failure modes: The forward shift is a display convention, not foresight - the value is fully determined by past data; cloud signals whipsaw when the market has no trend.
    Misconceptions: 'Plotted in the future' does not mean it predicts future support - it is today's midpoint average drawn ahead of the current bar.
    Example: Tenkan 104 and Kijun 103 give Senkou Span A = 103.5, shown 26 bars ahead of today.
    Senkou Span A = (Tenkan-sen + Kijun-sen) / 2, plotted 26 periods into the future.
    Aliases: Leading Span A, Senkou A

    Senkou Span B reviewed #

    The slower cloud boundary: the midpoint of the 52-period high-low range, plotted 26 periods ahead, forming the long-term equilibrium edge of the Kumo.
    Intuition: The slowest Ichimoku line, it moves rarely and marks durable long-term balance; flat, thick sections of cloud built on Span B are watched as major support or resistance.
    Mechanics: Senkou Span B = (max(High,52) + min(Low,52))/2, shifted 26 bars forward. Its relation to Senkou Span A sets the cloud color, and a price break through a Span-B-defined cloud edge signals a trend change.
    Failure modes: Extreme inertia: it can sit far from price for months, making cloud-break signals late; like all Ichimoku lines it is descriptive, not predictive.
    Misconceptions: A thick cloud is often called 'strong support/resistance', but that is a practitioner heuristic, not a tested law.
    Example: With a 52-bar high of 112 and low of 90, Senkou Span B = 101, plotted 26 bars ahead.
    Senkou Span B = (max(High, 52) + min(Low, 52)) / 2, plotted 26 periods into the future.

    Chikou Span reviewed #

    The Ichimoku confirmation line: today's close plotted 26 periods back on the chart, so it can be compared directly with where price and the cloud stood 26 bars ago.
    Intuition: By shifting the close into the past, you see at a glance whether the current price is above or below the price and cloud of 26 periods ago - a built-in momentum comparison.
    Mechanics: Plot Close_t at chart position t-26. Standard confirmation rules: bullish when the Chikou is above the price of 26 bars ago (and ideally above the past cloud), bearish when below.
    Failure modes: It is purely a displaced close and adds no information beyond a 26-period momentum comparison; the rightmost 26 bars of any chart show no Chikou at all.
    Misconceptions: Despite being drawn through past prices it is not a forecast line - it simply re-displays the close offset backward.
    Example: If today's close is 105 and the close 26 bars ago was 98, the Chikou point sits above that past price - a bullish confirmation.
    Chikou Span_t = Close_t, displayed at bar t - 26 (default displacement 26).

    On-balance volume reviewed #

    Joe Granville's cumulative volume indicator (Granville's New Key to Stock Market Profits, 1963): a running total that adds the period's volume when the close rises and subtracts it when the close falls, tracking cumulative buying versus selling pressure.
    Intuition: Granville's thesis is that volume precedes price - if volume consistently flows in on up days, the OBV line rises even before price breaks out.
    Mechanics: OBV_t = OBV_{t-1} + volume when close_t > close_{t-1}, minus volume when lower, unchanged when equal. The absolute level is meaningless; the line's trend, divergences from price, and support/resistance breaks are the signals.
    Failure modes: A single huge-volume day permanently shifts the line, distorting later readings; it ignores intraday position, so a gap-down day closing near its high still counts as all-negative volume.
    Misconceptions: OBV's absolute value is not meaningful - only its direction and pattern matter; and a rising OBV does not guarantee price will follow.
    Example: From OBV 100,000: an up close on 8,200 shares takes it to 108,200, and a subsequent down day on 6,000 shares brings it to 102,200.
    OBV_t = OBV_{t-1} + sign(C_t - C_{t-1}) x V_t (no change when closes are equal).

    Accumulation distribution line reviewed #

    Marc Chaikin's cumulative money-flow line: each period's volume is weighted by where the close falls within the high-low range (the Money Flow Multiplier) and added to a running total, measuring cumulative buying and selling pressure.
    Intuition: Closing near the high on big volume means buyers dominated the session; the A/D line accumulates that evidence and often diverges from price before reversals.
    Mechanics: Money Flow Multiplier = ((C - L) - (H - C)) / (H - L), ranging from +1 (close on the high) to -1 (close on the low); Money Flow Volume = multiplier x volume; ADL is the running sum of Money Flow Volume. Used for trend confirmation and divergences.
    Failure modes: It ignores close-to-close gaps: a stock that gaps down 5% but closes near the day's high still registers accumulation, so the line can disconnect from price reality.
    Misconceptions: Unlike OBV it does not score a day all-or-nothing; and a rising A/D line is evidence of accumulation, not a promise of higher prices.
    Example: H 50, L 48, C 49.5: multiplier = ((49.5-48) - (50-49.5))/2 = 0.5; on 10,000 shares the Money Flow Volume is +5,000.
    MFM = ((C - L) - (H - C)) / (H - L); MFV = MFM x V; ADL_t = ADL_{t-1} + MFV_t.
    Aliases: A/D line, ADL, Accumulation/Distribution Line

    Chaikin money flow reviewed #

    Marc Chaikin's oscillator version of the Accumulation/Distribution concept: the sum of Money Flow Volume over n periods (typically 20 or 21) divided by the sum of volume, oscillating between +1 and -1 around a zero line.
    Intuition: It compresses the A/D logic into a fixed window: positive CMF means closes have clustered in the upper half of their ranges, volume-weighted, over the look-back.
    Mechanics: Compute the Money Flow Multiplier and Money Flow Volume exactly as for the A/D line; CMF(n) = sum of MFV over n periods / sum of volume over n periods. Zero-line crosses (often buffered at +/-0.05) and divergences are the standard signals.
    Failure modes: Whipsaws around the zero line in choppy markets and inherits the A/D gap blind spot - a down-gap that closes near the high reads as positive money flow.
    Misconceptions: Readings near +1 or -1 are essentially impossible (they would need 20 straight closes on the high or low); typical range is about +/-0.5, so small values are noise, not signal.
    Example: Over 20 days, if Money Flow Volume sums to 2,000,000 on total volume of 10,000,000, CMF = +0.20, indicating net buying pressure.
    CMF(n) = sum(MFM_i x V_i, n) / sum(V_i, n), where MFM = ((C - L) - (H - C)) / (H - L).

    Money flow index reviewed #

    Gene Quong and Avrum Soudack's volume-weighted momentum oscillator: an RSI-style 0-100 calculation applied to typical-price money flows, so both the direction and the volume of each bar feed the reading.
    Intuition: It is RSI with volume mixed in - an overbought reading requires not just rising prices but rising prices backed by rising dollar volume.
    Mechanics: Typical price = (H+L+C)/3; raw money flow = typical price x volume, classified positive or negative by the change in typical price; MFI = 100 - 100/(1 + positive sum / negative sum) over 14 periods. Conventional overbought above 80 (truly extreme above 90), oversold below 20 (below 10).
    Failure modes: In strong trends it pins above 80 or below 20 while price continues; volume spikes (expiry days, index rebalancing) distort it without any change in sentiment.
    Misconceptions: MFI overbought is not a sell signal - the creators recommended the stricter 90/10 levels precisely because 80/20 fires too often in trends; and MFI is not interchangeable with RSI, since volume can make them diverge.
    Example: If 14-day positive money flow is 120M and negative is 60M, the ratio is 2 and MFI = 100 - 100/3 ~ 66.7.
    MFI = 100 - 100/(1 + MoneyRatio); MoneyRatio = sum(PositiveMF, 14) / sum(NegativeMF, 14); RawMF = TypicalPrice x Volume; TP = (H+L+C)/3.
    Aliases: MFI, Volume-weighted RSI

    Ease of movement reviewed #

    Richard Arms' oscillator relating midpoint change to volume and range: it measures how much price moved per unit of volume, rising when prices advance with ease (large range, light volume) and falling when they decline with ease.
    Intuition: If a big price move needs little volume, movement is 'easy'; if heavy volume produces a tiny range, movement is labored - EMV quantifies the effort-versus-result idea behind Arms' Equivolume charts.
    Mechanics: Distance moved = midpoint_t - midpoint_{t-1} with midpoint = (H+L)/2; Box ratio = (V / 100,000,000) / (H - L); 1-period EMV = distance / box ratio; the traded indicator is a 14-period SMA of 1-period EMV. Positive readings mean rising with ease, negative falling with ease.
    Failure modes: The volume scale divisor is arbitrary and platform-dependent, so absolute values are not comparable across platforms; tiny-range or low-volume bars make the ratio explode into spikes.
    Misconceptions: A high positive EMV is not automatically bullish strength - it can simply mean price drifted up on falling volume, which many traders read as weakness.
    Example: If the midpoint rises 1.00 on volume of 20M and a range of 2.00, the box ratio is 0.2/2 = 0.1 and 1-period EMV = 10.0 - an 'easy' advance.
    EMV = ((H+L)/2 - (H_prev+L_prev)/2) / ((V/100,000,000)/(H-L)); EMV(14) = SMA(1-period EMV, 14); the volume scale divisor is platform-dependent.
    Aliases: EMV, Ease of Movement (EOM)

    Force index reviewed #

    Alexander Elder's oscillator (Trading for a Living) multiplying the close-to-close price change by volume, combining direction, extent, and participation into one measure of the force behind a move; the smoothed 13-period EMA form is the standard version.
    Intuition: A one-point gain on huge volume shows more conviction than the same gain on a trickle - Force Index scores moves by both size and participation.
    Mechanics: Force(1) = (Close_t - Close_{t-1}) x Volume_t; Force(13) = 13-period EMA of Force(1). Used for trend confirmation at the zero line, divergences, and - in Elder's pullback system - a 2-period Force Index against a 22-day EMA trend filter.
    Failure modes: The raw 1-period version is extremely jagged and the smoothed version lags; because it is price change times volume in absolute terms, values are not comparable across securities.
    Misconceptions: A positive Force Index does not mean buyers are 'in control' going forward - it records that the last bars rose on volume, which can reverse immediately.
    Example: A close rising from 50.00 to 50.84 on 162,619 shares gives Force(1) = 0.84 x 162,619 ~ +136,600, the largest reading in StockCharts' Pfizer example.
    FI(1) = (Close_t - Close_{t-1}) x Volume_t; FI(13) = EMA(FI(1), 13).
    Aliases: FI, Elder's Force Index
