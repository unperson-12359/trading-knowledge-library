    Portfolio reviewed #

    The combined holdings of stocks, bonds, commodities, real estate, and other investments owned by an individual or institutional investor, analyzed and managed as a single unit.
    Intuition: Your portfolio is the whole basket of things you own, not any single holding; its behavior is what you actually experience as an investor.
    Mechanics: A portfolio is characterized by a weight vector w (each w_i is the fraction of value in asset i, weights summing to 1). Its return is the weighted sum of asset returns, while its risk depends on the full covariance structure of the components, not just their individual volatilities.
    Failure modes: Treating a collection of separate accounts or funds as independent obscures aggregate exposures: overlapping holdings and shared factor bets can make a 'many-fund' portfolio far more concentrated than it looks.
    Misconceptions: Owning many different funds does not automatically make a portfolio diversified; what matters is how the underlying returns co-move, not the count of positions.
    Example: An investor holding 60% in a total stock market index fund, 30% in a bond index fund, and 10% in a money market fund has one portfolio whose return each month is 0.6*R_stocks + 0.3*R_bonds + 0.1*R_cash.
    R_p = sum_i w_i * R_i, with sum_i w_i = 1, where w_i is the value weight of asset i and R_i its return.
    Aliases: investment portfolio

    Asset allocation reviewed #

    The practice of dividing an investment portfolio among different asset categories, such as stocks, bonds, and cash, with the mix chosen according to the investor's time horizon and risk tolerance.
    Intuition: Before picking individual securities, you decide how much of your money goes into broad buckets like stocks versus bonds, because those buckets drive most of the portfolio's risk and return.
    Mechanics: The investor sets target weights for each asset class; because the returns of different asset categories historically have not moved up and down at the same time, the chosen mix largely determines the portfolio's volatility and its ability to meet a financial goal. Weights drift with market moves and are restored through rebalancing.
    Failure modes: A fixed allocation becomes inappropriate when the investor's horizon or risk tolerance changes, and its risk properties degrade when correlations between asset classes shift, especially in crises.
    Misconceptions: There is no single 'correct' allocation model for a given goal; the SEC stresses the right mix is a personal choice driven by time horizon and risk tolerance, not a universal formula.
    Example: A 30-year-old saving for retirement might choose 90% stocks and 10% bonds, while the same person at 63, close to needing the money, might shift to 50% stocks, 40% bonds, and 10% cash.
    Aliases: asset mix

    Strategic asset allocation reviewed #

    The long-term target allocation across asset classes that an investor commits to based on goals, time horizon, and risk tolerance, typically reviewed over years or decades and maintained through periodic rebalancing.
    Intuition: It is the 'default setting' of the portfolio - the mix you plan to hold through market cycles rather than the mix you happen to hold today.
    Mechanics: Target weights are derived from long-term capital market expectations and the investor's circumstances; actual weights are periodically rebalanced back to the policy targets, and the policy itself is revisited only when goals, horizon, or risk tolerance change materially.
    Failure modes: A strategic mix built on stale long-run return, volatility, or correlation assumptions can silently deliver a very different risk profile than intended, and rigid adherence can be costly if the investor's circumstances change mid-horizon.
    Misconceptions: Strategic allocation is not 'buy and forget': it requires rebalancing to stay at target weights and periodic re-derivation when the investor's situation changes.
    Example: A pension plan sets a policy mix of 60% equities and 40% fixed income, rebalancing annually; this 60/40 target itself - not any short-term deviation from it - is the strategic asset allocation.
    Aliases: SAA, policy portfolio, policy mix
    Related:

    Tactical asset allocation reviewed #

    An active portfolio management strategy in which an investor deliberately makes short-term, typically modest adjustments to the long-term strategic asset allocation in response to changing market conditions, valuations, or economic trends.
    Intuition: You keep your long-term plan but lean a few percent toward or away from an asset class when conditions look unusually favorable or risky, then drift back to the strategic targets.
    Mechanics: Practitioners monitor indicators such as market trends, valuations, volatility, and interest rates; when predefined criteria are met, they shift a small portion of the portfolio (often 5-10% of assets) for a horizon of months rather than years, and unwind the tilt as conditions normalize.
    Failure modes: Tactical shifts require being right frequently enough to overcome transaction costs and taxes; mistimed tilts, especially those driven by emotion rather than rules, can leave the portfolio worse off than simple buy-and-hold rebalancing.
    Misconceptions: Tactical allocation is not the same as all-or-nothing market timing: it uses predefined signals to make incremental adjustments rather than attempting to call exact market tops and bottoms.
    Example: An investor whose strategic mix is 60% stocks might cut equities to 52% during a period of sharply elevated volatility, then move back toward 60% as conditions normalize.
    Aliases: TAA
    Related:

    Rebalancing reviewed #

    The process of bringing a portfolio back to its original or target asset allocation mix, which becomes necessary because some investments grow faster than others and push the portfolio out of alignment with its intended risk level.
    Intuition: If your winners grow faster than your losers, your portfolio quietly becomes riskier (or safer) than you planned; rebalancing trims the winners and tops up the laggards to restore the plan.
    Mechanics: Rebalancing can be calendar-based (e.g., every 6 or 12 months) or threshold-based (when an asset class weight moves beyond a preset band), and is executed by selling overweighted categories to buy underweighted ones, by directing new contributions to underweighted categories, or both.
    Failure modes: Each rebalance can trigger transaction costs and taxable gains, and in strong trending markets disciplined rebalancing systematically sells the asset that keeps rising, lagging a drifted buy-and-hold portfolio.
    Misconceptions: Rebalancing is a risk-control discipline, not a return-boosting trick: its 'buy low, sell high' effect does not guarantee higher returns, only that the portfolio's risk stays near its target.
    Example: An investor targets 60% stocks; after a rally stocks reach 80% of the portfolio, so she sells stocks or directs new savings into bonds until the 60/40 mix is restored.
    Aliases: portfolio rebalancing

    Diversification reviewed #

    A risk-management strategy of spreading money across different investments - both between and within asset categories - so that losses in one holding may be offset by gains or stability in others, reducing portfolio-level volatility.
    Intuition: Don't put all your eggs in one basket: if one investment loses money, the hope is that the others make up for it.
    Mechanics: Because portfolio variance contains covariance terms, combining assets whose returns are imperfectly correlated yields a portfolio volatility below the weighted average of individual volatilities; this removes much asset-specific (idiosyncratic) risk but cannot remove market-wide (systematic) risk.
    Failure modes: Diversification works least when it is most needed: correlations across markets rise in bear markets and crises, so apparently diversified portfolios can fall together. It reduces risk but does not eliminate the risk of loss.
    Misconceptions: Holding several investments is not the same as being diversified - a handful of stocks in one sector, or several funds holding the same stocks, leaves concentrated exposure intact.
    Example: A total stock market index fund holding thousands of companies diversifies away most single-company risk, while adding a bond fund diversifies across asset categories.

    Correlation matrix reviewed #

    The symmetric matrix of pairwise correlation coefficients between the returns of a set of assets, with ones on the diagonal, summarizing the linear co-movement structure used in portfolio risk analysis and optimization.
    Intuition: It is the table that tells you, for every pair of assets, how much they tend to move together - the raw material for judging how diversified a portfolio really is.
    Mechanics: Each off-diagonal element is rho_ij = cov(r_i, r_j) / (sigma_i * sigma_j), estimated from historical returns or from a factor model; multiplying by volatilities recovers the covariance matrix used in portfolio variance and optimization.
    Failure modes: With N assets, (N^2 - N)/2 distinct correlations must be estimated, so sample matrices are noisy and near-singular when assets outnumber observations; correlations are also unstable, rising in bear markets and breaking portfolio constructions calibrated on calm periods.
    Misconceptions: A historical correlation matrix is not a stable property of the market: it is a sample estimate whose entries can shift substantially across regimes and estimation windows.
    Example: Sharpe's three-asset example uses the correlation matrix cash/bonds 0.40, cash/stocks 0.15, bonds/stocks 0.35 to derive covariances and optimal portfolio weights.
    rho_ij = cov(r_i, r_j) / (sigma_i * sigma_j), where sigma_i and sigma_j are the return standard deviations of assets i and j.

    Covariance matrix reviewed #

    The symmetric matrix whose diagonal elements are the return variances of a set of assets and whose off-diagonal elements are their pairwise return covariances, the central input to mean-variance portfolio analysis.
    Intuition: It packs everything about how assets' returns vary and move together into one object, so a portfolio's risk can be computed in a single matrix multiplication.
    Mechanics: Element C_ij = E[(r_i - E[r_i])(r_j - E[r_j])]. Portfolio variance is v_p = x'Cx for weight vector x. Because estimating all entries from history is noisy, practitioners often impose structure via a factor model: C = B*C_F*B' + diag(rv), where B holds factor exposures, C_F the factor covariances, and rv the residual variances.
    Failure modes: Estimation error explodes with the number of assets (a 1,000-asset universe needs about 500,500 distinct covariances), and optimizers amplify estimation noise into extreme, unstable weights; non-stationarity makes past covariances a poor guide in regime shifts.
    Misconceptions: The sample covariance matrix from historical returns is not the 'true' matrix; it contains estimation noise, and factor-structure estimators exist precisely because raw sample matrices are unreliable for optimization.
    Example: In Sharpe's example the covariance matrix of cash, bonds, and stocks has entries such as cov(bonds, stocks) = 39.886 and var(stocks) = 237.160, from which any portfolio's variance follows as x'Cx.
    C_ij = E[(r_i - E[r_i]) * (r_j - E[r_j])]; factor-model estimator: C = B*C_F*B' + diag(rv).
    Aliases: variance-covariance matrix

    Portfolio variance reviewed #

    The variance of a portfolio's return, which depends on the portfolio weights, the variances of the component assets, and all pairwise covariances between them; its square root (volatility) is the standard risk measure in mean-variance analysis.
    Intuition: Portfolio risk is not the average of individual risks: assets that zig while others zag cancel each other out, so covariances do most of the work in determining total variance.
    Mechanics: For weight vector x and covariance matrix C, v_p = x'Cx. Expanding for two assets: v_p = w_1^2*sigma_1^2 + w_2^2*sigma_2^2 + 2*w_1*w_2*rho_12*sigma_1*sigma_2. With a factor model the same quantity can be computed as v_p = b_p*C_F*b_p' + (x.^2)'*rv using portfolio factor exposures b_p, avoiding the full asset covariance matrix.
    Failure modes: Variance is only as good as its inputs: estimated variances and covariances drift over time, and variance as a symmetric measure treats upside and downside moves identically, which may not match investor loss aversion.
    Misconceptions: Portfolio variance is not the weighted average of asset variances; ignoring the covariance terms dramatically overstates the risk of diversified portfolios.
    Example: Two equally weighted assets with volatilities 15% and 10% and correlation 0.3 have variance 0.5^2*0.0225 + 0.5^2*0.01 + 2*0.5*0.5*0.3*0.15*0.10 = 0.010375, a volatility of about 10.2%, below the 12.5% naive average.
    v_p = x'Cx = sum_i sum_j x_i * x_j * cov(r_i, r_j); two-asset case: v_p = w_1^2*sigma_1^2 + w_2^2*sigma_2^2 + 2*w_1*w_2*rho_12*sigma_1*sigma_2.

    Marginal risk contribution reviewed #

    The sensitivity of total portfolio risk (typically volatility) to a small change in the weight of one asset, holding other weights fixed: the partial derivative of portfolio volatility with respect to that asset's weight.
    Intuition: It answers: if I add one more dollar of this asset, by how much does total portfolio risk go up?
    Mechanics: For volatility sigma_p = sqrt(w'Cw), the marginal risk contribution of asset i is MRC_i = d(sigma_p)/d(w_i) = (Cw)_i / sigma_p, i.e., the asset's covariance with the portfolio divided by portfolio volatility. It is the building block of risk contribution decompositions and risk budgeting.
    Failure modes: MRC is defined relative to a volatility risk measure and a specific covariance estimate; it changes with the risk measure (e.g., VaR or expected shortfall) and inherits all estimation error and non-stationarity of the covariance matrix.
    Misconceptions: A marginal risk contribution is not a property of the asset alone - it depends on the current portfolio composition, so the same asset has different MRCs in different portfolios.
    Example: If a small-cap fund has covariance 0.03 with the current portfolio and portfolio volatility is 12%, its marginal risk contribution is 0.03/0.12 = 0.25: adding a small weight raises portfolio volatility by roughly 0.25 per unit weight added.
    MRC_i = d(sigma_p)/d(w_i) = (Cw)_i / sigma_p, where C is the asset covariance matrix, w the weight vector, and (Cw)_i the covariance of asset i with the portfolio.
    Aliases: MRC, marginal contribution to risk, MCTR
    Related:

    Component risk contribution reviewed #

    An asset's total contribution to portfolio risk, computed as its weight times its marginal risk contribution; component risk contributions sum exactly to total portfolio volatility by Euler's homogeneous-function theorem.
    Intuition: It splits total portfolio risk into slices, one per asset, so you can see which positions actually eat your risk budget - often very different from which positions eat your capital.
    Mechanics: CRC_i = w_i * MRC_i = w_i * (Cw)_i / sigma_p, and sum_i CRC_i = sigma_p. Expressed as percentages, CRC_i/sigma_p gives each asset's share of total risk, the quantity equalized in risk-parity (equal risk contribution) portfolios.
    Failure modes: Risk contributions depend on the chosen covariance estimate and risk measure, so they can be badly wrong in crises when correlations shift; negative contributions from hedging positions can also confuse naive budget checks.
    Misconceptions: Dollar weights are not risk weights: a 60/40 stock/bond portfolio typically derives around 90% of its volatility from the equity sleeve, not 60%.
    Example: In a 60/40 portfolio with 10% volatility, if the equity CRC is 9% and the bond CRC is 1%, equities consume 90% of the risk budget despite holding 60% of the capital.
    CRC_i = w_i * (Cw)_i / sigma_p, with sum_i CRC_i = sigma_p.
    Aliases: CRC, risk contribution, total risk contribution
    Related:

    Minimum-variance portfolio reviewed #

    The portfolio of risky assets with the lowest attainable variance, located at the leftmost point of the mean-variance efficient frontier; it is the optimal portfolio for an investor with zero tolerance for risk.
    Intuition: It is the mix that wobbles the least, whatever its expected return - found purely from the covariance matrix, with no return forecasts needed.
    Mechanics: Solving min_w w'Cw subject to sum_i w_i = 1 gives w_MVP = C^{-1}1 / (1'C^{-1}1), where 1 is a vector of ones. In Sharpe's equilibrium framework it is the portfolio z_p, the optimal portfolio for an investor with zero risk tolerance.
    Failure modes: Because it is driven entirely by the estimated covariance matrix, MVP weights are extremely sensitive to estimation error and can be concentrated and unstable; low in-sample variance does not guarantee low out-of-sample variance.
    Misconceptions: Minimum variance does not mean minimum possible loss or maximum risk-adjusted return: the MVP still carries market-level drawdowns and generally has a lower Sharpe ratio than the tangency portfolio.
    Example: In Sharpe's three-asset example, the zero-risk-tolerance optimal portfolio z_p holds about 104% cash, -4% bonds, and 0% stocks - the variance-minimizing combination of the three assets.
    w_MVP = C^{-1}*1 / (1'*C^{-1}*1), where C is the asset covariance matrix and 1 a vector of ones.
    Aliases: MVP, global minimum-variance portfolio, GMV
    Related:

    Efficient frontier reviewed #

    The set of portfolios that offer the maximum expected return for each level of return variance (equivalently, the minimum variance for each target expected return), the central construct of Markowitz's mean-variance portfolio selection theory.
    Intuition: It is the best menu available: every portfolio below the frontier is dominated, because a frontier portfolio gives either more return for the same risk or less risk for the same return.
    Mechanics: For each target expected return e*, solve min_w w'Cw subject to w'e = e* and sum_i w_i = 1; tracing e* yields the frontier, a hyperbola in (volatility, expected return) space. All mean-variance-optimal portfolios lie on it, and with a risk-free asset the relevant frontier becomes the tangent line through the tangency portfolio.
    Failure modes: The frontier is only as good as its inputs: portfolios that appear efficient on unadjusted historical estimates 'will almost certainly plot below the true efficient frontier' because optimizers exploit estimation noise (Sharpe); the true frontier is unobservable.
    Misconceptions: The efficient frontier is not a fixed, knowable curve in the market - it moves with expected return, volatility, and correlation estimates, so two analysts with different inputs get different frontiers.
    Example: Given cash, bond, and stock expected returns, volatilities, and correlations, the frontier runs from the minimum-variance portfolio upward toward higher stock weights, with each point the lowest-variance mix for its target return.
    min_w w'Cw subject to w'e = e* and 1'w = 1, traced over all feasible target returns e*.
    Aliases: mean-variance efficient frontier

    Capital market line reviewed #

    In CAPM equilibrium with a risk-free asset, the line connecting the risk-free rate to the tangency (market) portfolio in (volatility, expected return) space; every efficient portfolio is a combination of the risk-free asset and the market portfolio.
    Intuition: Once you can borrow and lend at the risk-free rate, the best risky portfolio is the same for everyone - you just dial risk up or down by levering or diluting that one portfolio.
    Mechanics: By two-fund separation, optimal holdings are linear in risk tolerance: each investor holds the risk-free asset plus the tangency portfolio. The CML has intercept r_f and slope equal to the market's Sharpe ratio, so E(R_p) = r_f + [(E(R_m) - r_f)/sigma_m] * sigma_p for efficient portfolios.
    Failure modes: The CML describes only efficient portfolios; individual securities and inefficient portfolios plot below it. It rests on CAPM assumptions (mean-variance preferences, common expectations, frictionless borrowing at r_f) that hold only approximately in practice.
    Misconceptions: The CML is frequently confused with the security market line: the CML plots expected return against total volatility for efficient portfolios only, while the SML plots expected return against beta for all assets.
    Example: With r_f = 2%, E(R_m) = 8%, sigma_m = 15%, an efficient portfolio with sigma_p = 7.5% (half market risk) earns 2% + 0.5*(8% - 2%) = 5% on the CML.
    E(R_p) = r_f + [(E(R_m) - r_f) / sigma_m] * sigma_p, for portfolios p on the CML, where m is the market (tangency) portfolio.
    Aliases: CML

    Tangency portfolio reviewed #

    The portfolio of risky assets with the highest Sharpe ratio (expected excess return per unit of risk), located where the capital allocation line from the risk-free rate is tangent to the risky-asset efficient frontier; in CAPM equilibrium it is the market portfolio.
    Intuition: It is the one risky portfolio everyone should hold - investors differ only in how much they lever or dilute it with the risk-free asset.
    Mechanics: Weights solve max_w (w'e - r_f) / sqrt(w'Cw) subject to 1'w = 1, giving w proportional to C^{-1}(e - r_f*1). A basic CAPM premise is that all agents invest in this highest-Sharpe-ratio portfolio and leverage or de-leverage it to suit their risk preferences.
    Failure modes: Maximizing the Sharpe ratio over estimated inputs makes the tangency portfolio the portfolio most sensitive to estimation error: small changes in expected return or covariance estimates can swing weights wildly, and realized out-of-sample Sharpe ratios routinely disappoint.
    Misconceptions: The tangency portfolio is not the highest-return or lowest-risk portfolio, and its theoretical equivalence with the market portfolio holds only under CAPM equilibrium assumptions, not as an empirical identity.
    Example: With a risk-free rate of 2%, if the risky frontier's best risk-return trade-off is a stock/bond mix with expected return 8% and volatility 12%, the tangency portfolio has Sharpe ratio (8-2)/12 = 0.5, and all efficient investors hold that mix in some proportion.
    w* = C^{-1}(e - r_f*1) / (1'*C^{-1}(e - r_f*1)), where e is the vector of expected returns, r_f the risk-free rate, and C the covariance matrix.
    Aliases: maximum Sharpe ratio portfolio, optimal risky portfolio

    Beta reviewed #

    A measure of the sensitivity of an asset's or portfolio's returns to the returns of the market portfolio, equal to the covariance of the asset's return with the market return divided by the variance of the market return; it is the slope of the regression of the asset's excess return on the market's excess return.
    Intuition: Beta tells you how much an asset typically amplifies market moves: beta 2 means twice the market's swings, beta 0.5 means half.
    Mechanics: Estimated as the slope of the best-fit line when the asset's (excess) returns are regressed on market (excess) returns; under CAPM, expected return is linear in beta: E(r_i) = r_f + beta_i*(E(r_m) - r_f). Portfolio beta is the weighted average of component betas.
    Failure modes: Betas are estimated from historical data and are unstable across periods and regimes; beta says nothing about idiosyncratic risk, and the empirical security market line is flatter than CAPM predicts, so low-beta stocks have historically earned more than the model implies.
    Misconceptions: Beta is not a complete measure of risk: a low-beta stock can be very volatile in absolute terms if its moves are uncorrelated with the market, and beta 1 does not mean 'same volatility as the market' unless correlation is also 1.
    Example: A portfolio with beta 2.0 is twice as sensitive to index moves as one with beta 1.0, so hedging it with index futures requires twice as many contracts per dollar of exposure.
    beta_i = cov(r_i, r_m) / var(r_m); CAPM: E(r_i) = r_f + beta_i * (E(r_m) - r_f).
    Aliases: market beta

    Alpha reviewed #

    The portion of an asset's or portfolio's expected or realized return that is not explained by its exposures to the factors in a given model; in a factor regression it is the intercept term, conventionally interpreted as return in excess of what factor risk alone would deliver.
    Intuition: After accounting for the market and other systematic bets the manager was riding, alpha is what's left - the part that might reflect skill, luck, or an omitted factor.
    Mechanics: In the factor model r_i = a_i + b_i1*f_1 + ... + b_im*f_m + e_i, the term a_i is the asset's expected residual return, its alpha; it is estimated as the intercept of a time-series regression of excess returns on factor returns (e.g., versus the CAPM market factor for Jensen's alpha).
    Failure modes: Alpha is defined relative to a chosen factor model: a positive alpha may simply reflect exposure to an omitted factor, and estimates are noisy, so statistically significant alpha over short windows is weak evidence of skill.
    Misconceptions: Positive historical alpha does not prove managerial skill - it can arise from luck across thousands of funds, from hidden factor or illiquidity exposures, or from benchmark misspecification.
    Example: A fund returns 9% while its CAPM beta of 0.8 with a 10% market return and 2% risk-free rate predicts 2% + 0.8*8% = 8.4%; its estimated Jensen's alpha is 0.6%.
    alpha_i = intercept in r_i - r_f = alpha_i + sum_j b_ij * f_j + e_i; equivalently alpha_i = E(r_i) - sum_j b_ij * E(f_j) when residuals have zero mean.
    Aliases: Jensen's alpha, abnormal return

    Systematic risk reviewed #

    The component of an asset's return risk that is related to the return of the market or to pervasive common factors; it affects broad classes of assets simultaneously and cannot be diversified away by holding a large portfolio of different assets.
    Intuition: It is the tide that lifts or sinks all boats: recessions, rate shocks, and market crashes hit nearly every stock at once, no matter how many you hold.
    Mechanics: In factor-model terms, systematic risk is the variance contributed by factor exposures, b_i*C_F*b_i', leaving residual variance as the diversifiable part. In CAPM, beta measures an asset's quantity of systematic risk, and only systematic risk is compensated with expected return in equilibrium.
    Failure modes: What counts as systematic depends on the factor model: omit a real common factor and its variance gets mislabeled as idiosyncratic. In crises, rising correlations effectively convert more asset risk into common (systematic-like) risk.
    Misconceptions: Adding more securities cannot reduce systematic risk below the market level; conversely, 'safe' defensive assets still carry systematic risk - they just carry less of it (low beta).
    Example: In 2008, nearly all equity sectors fell together regardless of stock selection, an expression of systematic risk; a diversified equity portfolio's losses tracked the market factor rather than any single company.
    Systematic variance of asset i = b_i * C_F * b_i', where b_i is the asset's vector of factor exposures and C_F the factor covariance matrix; for the single-index model this reduces to beta_i^2 * sigma_m^2.
    Aliases: market risk, non-diversifiable risk, factor risk

    Idiosyncratic risk reviewed #

    The component of an asset's return risk that is specific to that asset - the residual return after removing the influence of common factors - which is uncorrelated with the residuals of other assets and can therefore be diversified away in a large portfolio.
    Intuition: It is the risk of company-specific news - a scandal, a drug trial, an earnings miss - that averages out when you hold hundreds of names.
    Mechanics: In the linear factor model, the residual return e_i carries the idiosyncratic risk; the model's key assumption is corr(e_i, e_j) = 0 for i != j, so residual variances shrink toward zero in a well-diversified portfolio, leaving factor-driven variance dominant.
    Failure modes: If the factor model omits a genuine common factor, residual returns remain correlated across assets and 'idiosyncratic' risk fails to diversify away as predicted; concentrated portfolios remain fully exposed to it.
    Misconceptions: Idiosyncratic risk is not 'unimportant' risk: for a concentrated holder it dominates total risk, and under CAPM it earns no expected-return premium precisely because it is diversifiable.
    Example: A pharmaceutical firm's stock plunges 40% on a failed drug trial while the market is flat; holders of a 500-stock index fund barely notice because the event is idiosyncratic.
    Idiosyncratic variance of asset i = var(e_i) = rv_i; portfolio idiosyncratic variance = sum_i w_i^2 * rv_i, which tends to zero as weights become small and numerous.
    Aliases: specific risk, unsystematic risk, residual risk, security-specific risk

    Factor exposure reviewed #

    The sensitivity of an asset's or portfolio's return to a given factor - the change in return per unit change in the factor - represented by the coefficient b_ij multiplying that factor in a linear factor model.
    Intuition: It is a dial reading showing how strongly an asset or portfolio is tuned to a driver like the market, size, or value: exposure 1.2 to the market factor means roughly 1.2x the factor's moves.
    Mechanics: Asset exposures b_ij are estimated by regression or specified from characteristics; a portfolio's exposure to factor j is the weighted average of its assets' exposures, b_pj = sum_i w_i * b_ij, i.e., b_p = x'B in matrix form. Exposures map factor returns into asset returns and factor covariances into the asset covariance matrix.
    Failure modes: Exposures are estimated, not observed: they drift over time as companies and funds change, differ across data windows and estimators, and characteristic-based proxies (e.g., book-to-market for value) only approximate true loadings.
    Misconceptions: A fund labeled 'value' does not necessarily have high value-factor exposure - exposure must be measured from return behavior or holdings, not inferred from the label.
    Example: A portfolio of 70% in a stock with market exposure 1.2 and 30% in a bond fund with market exposure 0.1 has portfolio market exposure 0.7*1.2 + 0.3*0.1 = 0.87.
    r_i = sum_j b_ij * f_j + e_i, where b_ij is the exposure of asset i to factor j; portfolio exposure b_p = x'B.
    Aliases: factor loading, factor sensitivity, factor beta

    Market factor reviewed #

    The excess return of the broad value-weighted stock market portfolio over the risk-free rate, the first factor in the CAPM and in all Fama-French models, proxied in the Kenneth French data library by the value-weight return of all eligible CRSP U.S. stocks minus the one-month Treasury bill rate.
    Intuition: It is simply 'the stock market's return above T-bills' - the baseline common driver that every equity shares and against which other factors are measured.
    Mechanics: Constructed as Rm - Rf, where Rm is the value-weight return of all CRSP firms incorporated in the U.S. and listed on NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ with valid share code, price, and return data, and Rf is the one-month T-bill rate; an asset's loading on it is its beta.
    Failure modes: The theoretical CAPM market portfolio includes all wealth (real estate, human capital), so any tradable index is only a proxy; results can be sensitive to the proxy's universe and weighting.
    Misconceptions: The market factor is not the raw index return - it is the excess return over the risk-free rate, and it is value-weighted, not equal-weighted, in standard academic construction.
    Example: If the CRSP value-weight market returns 1.2% in a month and the one-month T-bill yields 0.3%, the market factor realization MKT is 0.9% for that month.
    MKT_t = Rm_t - Rf_t, with Rm the value-weight market return and Rf the one-month Treasury bill rate.
    Aliases: Rm-Rf, excess market return, MKT

    Size factor reviewed #

    A Fama-French stock factor measuring the return difference between small-capitalization and large-capitalization stocks: SMB (Small Minus Big) is the average return on the small-stock portfolios minus the average return on the big-stock portfolios formed on market equity.
    Intuition: It isolates the historical tendency of small companies' stocks to behave differently - and on average to earn more - than big companies' stocks.
    Mechanics: Stocks are sorted on market equity using NYSE breakpoints; SMB is the average return of the three small portfolios minus the average return of the three big portfolios from the 2x3 size-by-book-to-market sorts (in the five-factor model, averaged across the book-to-market, profitability, and investment sorts).
    Failure modes: The small-cap premium is episodic and much weaker after its publication in the early 1980s; SMB returns are also sensitive to breakpoint choices, microstructure noise in small caps, and whether January effects are included.
    Misconceptions: SMB is not a bet that small stocks always beat big stocks - it is a long-short factor return that is negative in many periods, and loading on it measures exposure to whatever common shocks drive the small-big return gap.
    Example: In a month when the three small-cap portfolios average 2.1% and the three big-cap portfolios average 0.6%, the SMB realization is +1.5%.
    SMB = (1/3)(Small Value + Small Neutral + Small Growth) - (1/3)(Big Value + Big Neutral + Big Growth).
    Aliases: SMB, Small Minus Big, size premium

    Value factor reviewed #

    A Fama-French stock factor measuring the return difference between high book-to-market (value) and low book-to-market (growth) stocks: HML (High Minus Low) is the average return on the value portfolios minus the average return on the growth portfolios.
    Intuition: It captures the long-documented pattern that stocks cheap relative to their book value have on average outperformed expensive 'glamour' stocks.
    Mechanics: Within small and big size groups, stocks are sorted on book-to-market equity; HML = (1/2)(Small Value + Big Value) - (1/2)(Small Growth + Big Growth), using value-weight portfolios. A positive HML month means value stocks beat growth stocks.
    Failure modes: Value strategies suffer long drawdowns (e.g., the growth-dominated 2010s), and in the five-factor model HML becomes largely redundant once profitability and investment factors are included; whether the premium is risk compensation or mispricing remains unsettled.
    Misconceptions: The value factor is not the same as buying low P/E 'bargains' by any metric: HML is defined specifically on book-to-market equity within the Fama-French construction, and different value definitions can behave differently.
    Example: If in a month the two value portfolios average 1.8% while the two growth portfolios average -0.2%, HML is +2.0%, and a fund with HML loading 0.5 gains about 1.0% from the factor.
    HML = (1/2)(Small Value + Big Value) - (1/2)(Small Growth + Big Growth).
    Aliases: HML, High Minus Low, value premium

    Momentum factor reviewed #

    A stock factor that is long stocks with high recent returns and short stocks with low recent returns, formalizing the cross-sectional momentum pattern documented by Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) and incorporated into the Carhart four-factor model; in the French library, Mom is built from portfolios sorted on prior 2-12 month returns.
    Intuition: Stocks that went up over roughly the past year (skipping the most recent month) have tended to keep outperforming recent losers for a while - the factor trades that continuation.
    Mechanics: Ken French's Mom uses six value-weight portfolios formed monthly on size and prior (2-12) returns: Mom = (1/2)(Small High + Big High) - (1/2)(Small Low + Big Low), with NYSE median size breakpoint and 30th/70th prior-return percentiles; the most recent month is skipped to avoid short-term reversal.
    Failure modes: Momentum is prone to infrequent but severe crashes when trends reverse sharply, typically in rebounds from bear markets (e.g., 2009), when the short side of crowded losers rallies violently; the standard Fama-French models exclude momentum precisely because it behaves differently.
    Misconceptions: Momentum is not simply 'buy what went up last week': the academic factor uses 12-month formation returns skipping the most recent month, and it is a relative cross-sectional bet, not a market-direction bet.
    Example: Ranking stocks each month on months t-12 to t-2 returns and going long the top third while shorting the bottom third yields the Mom factor; if winners return 1.5% and losers -0.5% in the next month, Mom realizes +2.0%.
    Mom = (1/2)(Small High + Big High) - (1/2)(Small Low + Big Low), where High/Low refer to prior (t-12 to t-2) cumulative returns.
    Aliases: Mom, UMD, WML, winners minus losers

    Quality factor reviewed #

    A stock factor that is long high-quality stocks - companies that are profitable, growing, safe, and well managed - and short low-quality 'junk' stocks; Asness, Frazzini, and Pedersen formalize it as the Quality Minus Junk (QMJ) factor, with the Fama-French profitability factor RMW being a closely related construction.
    Intuition: All else equal, the market underprices boring excellence: profitable, stable firms have historically delivered high risk-adjusted returns relative to their struggling counterparts.
    Mechanics: Quality scores combine measures of profitability (e.g., return on equity, accruals quality), growth, safety (low beta, low volatility, low leverage), and payout; the QMJ factor goes long the top-quality group and shorts the bottom. Fama and French's RMW uses operating profitability sorts (robust minus weak) as a single-characteristic version.
    Failure modes: The quality premium is regime-dependent and its economic source is unresolved - Asness, Frazzini, and Pedersen note they cannot tie quality returns to risk, leaving anomaly, data-mining, or unidentified-risk explanations open; quality also crashes when junk rallies sharply.
    Misconceptions: Quality is not just 'good companies at any price': the factor is a relative long-short bet, and the price of quality (how much extra investors pay for it) varies over time, affecting forward returns.
    Example: A QMJ portfolio long stocks with high ROE, low leverage, and stable earnings and short stocks with the opposite traits earns the quality spread; Fama-French's RMW approximates one dimension of this with profitability sorts.
    RMW = (1/2)(Small Robust + Big Robust) - (1/2)(Small Weak + Big Weak), where Robust/Weak are operating-profitability sorts; QMJ is a composite long-short quality score portfolio.
    Aliases: QMJ, Quality Minus Junk, profitability factor
