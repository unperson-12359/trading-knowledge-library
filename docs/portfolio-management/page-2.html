    Low-volatility factor #

    A factor built on the empirical finding that low-beta and low-volatility stocks have historically earned higher risk-adjusted returns than the CAPM predicts - the security market line is empirically too flat - formalized by Frazzini and Pedersen as the Betting Against Beta (BAB) factor, which goes long leveraged low-beta assets and short de-leveraged high-beta assets.
    Intuition: Risky stocks don't pay as much per unit of risk as theory says they should, so tilting toward calm stocks has historically improved Sharpe ratios.
    Mechanics: Stocks are ranked by estimated beta (or volatility); the long side holds low-beta names (scaled up toward comparable risk) and the short side holds high-beta names (scaled down). Frazzini and Pedersen attribute the effect to leverage constraints: investors unable or unwilling to lever instead overweight high-beta securities, depressing their risk-adjusted returns.
    Failure modes: The factor underperforms sharply when high-beta stocks rally (strong bull rebounds), is exposed to crowding and valuation risk in the low-vol sleeve, and can load heavily on interest-rate-sensitive sectors such as utilities, adding hidden rate risk.
    Misconceptions: Low volatility is not low absolute risk in all senses: low-beta portfolios still fall in market crashes (they just fall less), and the anomaly concerns risk-adjusted, not raw, returns.
    Example: Sorting U.S. stocks by beta and holding the lowest-beta decile against the highest, with each side scaled to beta 1, reproduces the BAB factor; Frazzini and Pedersen document the flat security market line in 18 of 19 international equity markets.
    BAB return = (1/beta_L)(r_L - r_f) - (1/beta_H)(r_H - r_f), where r_L, r_H are returns of low- and high-beta portfolios and beta_L, beta_H their betas.
    Aliases: low-beta anomaly, betting against beta, BAB, low-risk anomaly

    Source: AQR Capital Management, Frazzini and Pedersen, Betting Against Beta (Journal of Financial Economics) — Abstract

    Carry factor #

    A cross-asset factor based on an asset's carry, defined by Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen, and Vrugt as its expected return assuming that market conditions, including its price, stay the same; carry strategies go long high-carry and short low-carry securities across global equities, bonds, currencies, commodities, and other markets.
    Intuition: Carry is what you earn if nothing changes: the coupon, the yield differential, the futures roll-down - the 'getting paid to wait' component of return.
    Mechanics: Carry can be measured in advance without an asset-pricing model (e.g., interest-rate differentials for currencies, yield minus financing cost for bonds, roll yield for commodities); an asset's total expected return decomposes into carry plus expected price appreciation. The factor is implemented as long-short portfolios ranking assets on carry within each asset class.
    Failure modes: Carry trades earn steady small gains punctuated by crash risk: despite low unconditional correlations across asset classes, carry strategies in all asset classes tend to do poorly at the same times, and those episodes coincide with global recessions and liquidity spirals.
    Misconceptions: Carry is not a free yield: the positive average return compensates for exposure to rare, severe drawdowns (the classic 'picking up pennies in front of a steamroller' profile), and naive FX carry violates uncovered interest parity empirically rather than confirming it.
    Example: An FX carry trade borrows in a low-rate currency (e.g., Japanese yen) to invest in a high-rate currency (e.g., Australian dollar); if exchange rates don't move, the trade earns the rate differential, but it loses heavily in sharp risk-off reversals.
    E(r_i) = carry_i + E(price appreciation_i); in FX, carry_i approximately equals the foreign minus domestic short-term interest rate differential.
    Aliases: carry, carry trade
    Related:

    Source: AQR Capital Management, Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen, and Vrugt, Carry (Journal of Financial Economics) — Abstract

    Term factor #

    One of the two bond-market factors in Fama and French (1993), capturing maturity risk: TERM is the difference between the return on long-term government bonds and the return on short-term risk-free instruments (the one-month Treasury bill rate), proxying the term premium for bearing interest-rate risk.
    Intuition: Long bonds swing more when rates move, and investors historically demanded extra return for that - TERM measures that extra return month by month.
    Mechanics: Constructed as the total return of a long-term government bond index minus a short-term risk-free rate (e.g., 1-month T-bill); bond and stock returns load on TERM to the extent they are exposed to shifts in the term structure of interest rates.
    Failure modes: TERM captures exposure only to roughly parallel rate moves; non-parallel yield-curve shifts (steepeners, flatteners) leave residual risk, and the term premium itself varies over time and has been compressed or negative in some eras.
    Misconceptions: TERM is not the level or slope of the yield curve: it is a realized return spread between long government bonds and bills, which differs from the ex-ante term premium embedded in forward rates.
    Example: If the long-term Treasury index returns 1.5% in a month when T-bills return 0.4%, TERM realizes +1.1%; a bond fund with high duration will show high sensitivity to this factor.
    TERM_t = R_LTG,t - R_f,t, where R_LTG is the return on long-term government bonds and R_f the one-month T-bill rate.
    Aliases: TERM, term premium factor, maturity factor
    Related:

    Source: Bektic, Wenzler, Wegener, Schiereck, and Spielmann, Extending Fama-French Factors to Corporate Bond Markets (working paper, describing the Fama-French 1993 TERM and DEF factors) — Section 4.1 (TERM construction)

    Credit factor #

    One of the two bond-market factors in Fama and French (1993), capturing default risk: DEF is the difference between the return on long-term corporate bonds and the return on long-term government bonds, proxying the compensation for bearing corporate default risk.
    Intuition: Corporate bonds pay more than Treasuries because they can default; DEF measures, each period, how much that credit exposure actually earned or lost.
    Mechanics: Constructed as the return spread of long-term corporate bonds over long-term government bonds; in factor regressions for corporate bond portfolios, DEF typically carries the majority of explanatory power because long-only credit portfolios share common market exposure to the default premium.
    Failure modes: DEF bundles duration-matched credit spread changes with actual default losses and flight-to-quality episodes; it spikes negatively in crises (credit widening) and cannot distinguish compensation for expected defaults from compensation for default-risk volatility.
    Misconceptions: DEF is not the credit spread itself: it is the realized excess return of corporate over government bonds, which is negative whenever spreads widen enough to offset the yield advantage.
    Example: If long-term corporate bonds return -0.5% in a month while long-term Treasuries return +1.0% amid a flight to quality, DEF realizes -1.5%, hitting credit-heavy portfolios.
    DEF_t = R_LTC,t - R_LTG,t, where R_LTC is the return on long-term corporate bonds and R_LTG the return on long-term government bonds.
    Aliases: DEF, default factor, default premium factor, credit premium
    Related:

    Liquidity factor #

    A factor capturing exposure to market-wide liquidity fluctuations: Pastor and Stambaugh (2003) show that stocks whose returns are more sensitive to innovations in aggregate liquidity earn higher expected returns, so liquidity risk is a priced state variable beyond market, size, value, and momentum exposures.
    Intuition: Stocks that fall hardest when market liquidity dries up are unpleasant to hold, so investors demand a premium for owning them - that premium is the liquidity factor's return.
    Mechanics: Pastor and Stambaugh measure each stock's liquidity monthly by the tendency of signed volume (order flow) to be followed by return reversals, average across stocks for an aggregate series, extract innovations, and then estimate each stock's 'liquidity beta' - its return sensitivity to those innovations. Their long-short liquidity-risk spread earned about 7.5% annually (1966-1999), adjusted for market, size, value, and momentum exposures.
    Failure modes: Liquidity is a broad, elusive concept and every proxy captures only one dimension; results depend on the specific measure, the factor is hard to trade directly, and liquidity betas are imprecisely estimated at the individual-stock level.
    Misconceptions: The priced liquidity factor is about systematic liquidity risk (sensitivity of returns to aggregate liquidity swings), not about how liquid an individual stock is; the level of illiquidity and the covariance with liquidity shocks are distinct constructs.
    Example: Long-Term Capital Management's portfolio was by design positively exposed to market-wide liquidity; when liquidity collapsed in 1998, its liquidity-sensitive positions dropped sharply, forcing liquidation - exactly the risk the liquidity factor prices.
    Liquidity measure gamma_i,t from the regression r^e_{i,d+1,t} = theta + phi*r_{i,d,t} + gamma_i,t * sign(r^e_{i,d,t}) * v_{i,d,t} + eps; a stock's liquidity beta is the sensitivity of its return to innovations L_t in the aggregate series.
    Aliases: liquidity risk factor, Pastor-Stambaugh liquidity factor, traded liquidity factor

    Factor model #

    A model that relates an asset's return linearly to a limited number of common factors plus an asset-specific residual, used to decompose returns, estimate expected returns, and build covariance matrices with far fewer parameters than direct estimation.
    Intuition: Instead of tracking how every pair of thousands of assets moves together, you describe each asset by how it responds to a handful of shared drivers - the rest is treated as idiosyncratic noise.
    Mechanics: r_i = b_i1*f_1 + ... + b_im*f_m + e_i. The model's power comes from two assumptions: residuals are uncorrelated with the factors, and residuals of different assets are uncorrelated with each other, so all cross-asset correlation flows through factor exposures. This yields C = B*C_F*B' + diag(rv), reducing, e.g., a 1,000-asset problem from ~500,500 covariances to a few thousand estimates.
    Failure modes: A factor model only constrains reality if its assumptions hold: omit an important common factor and residuals correlate across assets, breaking the covariance estimate; exposures also drift over time, so fitted models can go stale.
    Misconceptions: Any linear decomposition can be made to fit trivially - Sharpe stresses the model has economic content only through the assumptions on residuals; a good R-squared does not validate the factor structure.
    Example: A three-factor model with market, size, and value factors explains a stock's 16% annual return as 14.5% from its factor exposures times factor realizations plus 1.5% residual return.
    r_i = sum_{j=1..m} b_ij * f_j + e_i, with corr(e_i, f_j) = 0 for all j and corr(e_i, e_j) = 0 for i != j; covariance structure C = B*C_F*B' + diag(rv).
    Aliases: linear factor model, multi-factor model

    Single-index model #

    The simplest factor model, in which each asset's return is related to a single common factor - typically the return on a market index - plus an asset-specific residual; introduced by Sharpe (1963) as a simplified model for portfolio analysis that drastically cuts the number of estimates needed for covariance-based optimization.
    Intuition: Assume everything moves together only because everything moves with the market; then you only need each stock's beta to rebuild the whole covariance matrix.
    Mechanics: r_i = alpha_i + beta_i*r_m + e_i, with residuals uncorrelated across assets. This implies cov(r_i, r_j) = beta_i*beta_j*sigma_m^2 for i != j, so the covariance matrix is rebuilt from N betas, N residual variances, and one market variance instead of N(N-1)/2 sample covariances.
    Failure modes: One factor cannot capture industry, style, or macro co-movements, so residuals of similar stocks remain correlated and the model understates diversification benefits within sectors; it inherits all instability of beta estimation.
    Misconceptions: The single-index model is a statistical simplification, not the CAPM: the CAPM adds equilibrium asset-pricing implications (expected returns linear in beta), while the index model merely describes return covariation.
    Example: With beta_i = 1.2, beta_j = 0.8, and market variance 0.04, the single-index model assigns cov(r_i, r_j) = 1.2*0.8*0.04 = 0.0384 without ever computing their historical sample covariance.
    r_i = alpha_i + beta_i * r_m + e_i; cov(r_i, r_j) = beta_i * beta_j * sigma_m^2 for i != j; var(r_i) = beta_i^2 * sigma_m^2 + var(e_i).
    Aliases: market model, single-factor model, Sharpe's diagonal model

    Fama-French three-factor model #

    An empirical asset-pricing model from Fama and French (1993, 'Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds') that explains stock returns with three factors: the market excess return (Rm-Rf), the size factor SMB, and the value factor HML.
    Intuition: How a stock moves is largely explained by three dials: how the whole market did, how small caps did relative to big caps, and how cheap (high book-to-market) stocks did relative to expensive ones.
    Mechanics: R_i - R_f = alpha_i + b_i*(R_m - R_f) + s_i*SMB + h_i*HML + e_i, estimated by time-series regression; factor series are constructed from value-weight portfolios sorted on size and book-to-market using NYSE breakpoints, as distributed in the Kenneth French data library.
    Failure modes: The model cannot capture the momentum pattern and leaves other documented anomalies unexplained, which motivated the Carhart and five-factor extensions; its factors are empirical constructs whose risk-versus-behavioral interpretation remains debated.
    Misconceptions: A near-zero three-factor alpha is not proof a manager added no value in all senses - it only says returns are explained by market, size, and value exposures; conversely, positive alpha may reflect omitted factors rather than skill.
    Example: Regressing a small-cap value fund's monthly excess returns on MKT, SMB, and HML typically yields large positive s and h loadings, quantifying how much of its performance is just small-value exposure.
    R_i - R_f = alpha_i + b_i*(R_m - R_f) + s_i*SMB + h_i*HML + e_i.
    Aliases: FF3, Fama-French model

    Fama-French five-factor model #

    An extension of the Fama-French three-factor model (Fama and French 2015, 'A Five-Factor Asset Pricing Model') that adds a profitability factor, RMW (robust minus weak operating profitability), and an investment factor, CMA (conservative minus aggressive asset growth), to the market, size, and value factors.
    Intuition: Beyond market, size, and value, two more dials matter: profitable firms tend to beat unprofitable ones, and firms that invest conservatively tend to beat firms that expand aggressively.
    Mechanics: R_i - R_f = alpha_i + b_i*(R_m - R_f) + s_i*SMB + h_i*HML + r_i*RMW + c_i*CMA + e_i. The factors are built from 2x3 value-weight sorts on size paired with book-to-market, operating profitability, and investment; SMB is averaged across the three pairings.
    Failure modes: Even five factors leave momentum and several anomalies unexplained, and Fama and French note the model's main problem is the low average returns of small stocks that invest a lot despite low profitability; HML becomes redundant for describing average returns once RMW and CMA are included.
    Misconceptions: More factors do not mean a complete model of returns - the five-factor model is still an empirical approximation whose factor premia vary across periods and markets.
    Example: A highly profitable, low-asset-growth fund shows positive RMW and CMA loadings in a five-factor regression, which can absorb what looked like alpha under the three-factor model.
    R_i - R_f = alpha_i + b_i*(R_m - R_f) + s_i*SMB + h_i*HML + r_i*RMW + c_i*CMA + e_i.
    Aliases: FF5
    Related:

    Carhart four-factor model #

    An extension of the Fama-French three-factor model introduced by Mark Carhart (1997, 'On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance') that adds a momentum factor - long stocks with high prior 2-12 month returns and short those with low prior returns - to the market, size, and value factors.
    Intuition: To judge a fund fairly you must also control for whether it was simply riding recent winners, because momentum explains much of what looked like persistent skill.
    Mechanics: R_i - R_f = alpha_i + b_i*(R_m - R_f) + s_i*SMB + h_i*HML + m_i*Mom + e_i. Carhart used the Jegadeesh-Titman momentum factor (top minus bottom decile of prior 12-2 month returns); the standard data-library Mom variant averages the high-minus-low prior-return spread across small and big value-weight portfolios.
    Failure modes: The momentum leg exposes the model to momentum crashes in sharp market reversals, and alpha rankings of funds can flip depending on whether momentum is included; like all such models it is an empirical benchmark, not a theory of required returns.
    Misconceptions: Carhart's key finding was that much apparent mutual-fund performance persistence disappears after controlling for momentum and expenses - the four-factor alpha, not raw return rank, is the meaningful persistence measure.
    Example: Evaluating a growth-fund manager, a three-factor regression shows 2% alpha; adding the momentum factor reveals a large positive Mom loading and shrinks alpha toward zero, showing the 'skill' was largely momentum exposure.
    R_i - R_f = alpha_i + b_i*(R_m - R_f) + s_i*SMB + h_i*HML + m_i*Mom + e_i, with Mom = (1/2)(Small High + Big High) - (1/2)(Small Low + Big Low) on prior (2-12) returns.
    Aliases: Carhart model, four-factor model

    Risk budgeting #

    A portfolio construction approach that allocates risk rather than capital: each asset, asset class, or strategy is assigned a budget for its contribution to total portfolio risk, and weights are solved so realized risk contributions match those budgets; the special case of equal budgets for all assets is the risk-parity (equal risk contribution) portfolio.
    Intuition: Instead of deciding '60% of my money in stocks', you decide 'stocks may consume at most 60% of my total risk' - because equal dollars in stocks and bonds produce wildly unequal risk.
    Mechanics: With risk contribution CRC_i = w_i*(Cw)_i/sigma_p, budgets b_i (summing to 1) are imposed by solving w_i*(Cw)_i = b_i * w'Cw for all i; for equal budgets the ERC solution equalizes all component risk contributions. Risk budgeting became a popular alternative to Markowitz optimization after the 2008 crisis because it needs only covariance estimates, not return forecasts.
    Failure modes: Risk budgets calibrated on historical covariances break in crises when correlations jump; equal-risk portfolios typically need leverage to match the expected return of capital-weighted portfolios, adding financing and margin risk.
    Misconceptions: Risk parity is not 'equal weights': equal capital weights concentrate risk in the most volatile assets, whereas equal risk contributions deliberately hold less of high-volatility assets.
    Example: In a stock/bond risk-parity portfolio, because stocks are several times as volatile as bonds, the bond weight must be much larger than the stock weight so that each sleeve contributes 50% of portfolio volatility.
    Find w such that w_i*(Cw)_i / sigma_p = b_i for all i, with sum_i b_i = 1; ERC is the case b_i = 1/N for all i.
    Aliases: risk parity, equal risk contribution, ERC
    Related:

    Source: Thierry Roncalli, Introduction to Risk Parity and Budgeting — Abstract and risk-budgeting chapters (arXiv:1403.1889)

    Volatility scaling #

    A position-sizing technique that adjusts exposure inversely to recent volatility - leveraging up when volatility is low and scaling down when it is high - so the portfolio targets a roughly constant level of volatility rather than a constant notional exposure.
    Intuition: Because vol clusters (calm follows calm, storm follows storm), you can keep risk steadier by trading smaller when markets are stormy and larger when they are calm.
    Mechanics: The scaled return is r_scaled_t = (sigma_target / sigma_hat_{t-1}) * r_t (times a constant near 1 to match the ex-post target), where sigma_hat is estimated from daily returns with exponentially decaying weights using data known in advance. Harvey, Hoyle, Korgaonkar, Rattray, Sargaison, and Van Hemert (2018) find scaling raises Sharpe ratios mainly for risk assets (equities, credit) via the leverage effect, and reduces the likelihood of extreme returns across all asset classes.
    Failure modes: Scaling cuts both tails - it shrinks right-tail gains as well as left-tail losses; for bonds, currencies, and commodities the Sharpe-ratio benefit is negligible, turnover adds transaction costs, and a sudden vol spike can force selling into the drawdown.
    Misconceptions: Volatility scaling is not return prediction: volatility is persistent while mean returns show no clear pattern across vol quintiles, so the technique manages risk exposure, not alpha.
    Example: With a 10% volatility target, if estimated S&P 500 volatility jumps from 15% to 30%, the strategy halves its equity exposure (15/30 = 0.5 scaling factor), as volatility-target funds did in episodes like the 2020 crash.
    r_scaled_t = (sigma_target / sigma_hat_{t-1}) * r_t, where sigma_hat_{t-1} is the conditional volatility estimate known before period t and sigma_target the target annualized volatility (e.g., 10%).
    Aliases: volatility targeting, vol targeting, volatility management
    Related:

    Source: Harvey, Hoyle, Korgaonkar, Rattray, Sargaison, and Van Hemert, The Impact of Volatility Targeting, Journal of Portfolio Management 45(1), 14-33 — Volatility Scaling section

    Correlation breakdown #

    The empirically documented tendency for correlations between markets and assets to rise sharply in bear markets and crises - but not in bull markets - so that diversification benefits shrink precisely when they are most needed; Longin and Solnik (2001) established the asymmetry formally using extreme value theory.
    Intuition: In a panic, everything sells off together: the low correlations that justified your diversification in calm times evaporate in the crash.
    Mechanics: Longin and Solnik model the tails of the multivariate return distribution with extreme value theory and test exceedance correlations; they find conditional correlation between international equity markets increases strongly in bear markets but not in bull markets, rejecting multivariate normality in the negative tail only. They also warn that naive tests conditioning on absolute return size spuriously link correlation to volatility.
    Failure modes: As a finding it indicts static models: any risk estimate assuming constant correlation (including constant-correlation GARCH) understates joint crash risk. Conversely, simplistic studies that condition on large absolute returns confuse conditioning effects with true correlation shifts.
    Misconceptions: It is not literally true that 'all correlations go to one': the documented effect is an asymmetric increase in bear markets related to market trend rather than to volatility per se, and positive extreme returns show no corresponding increase.
    Example: In the 2008 crisis, developed-market equity indexes that normally correlated around 0.5 fell together far more tightly; Longin-Solnik's earlier US-UK analysis already showed exceedance correlation of 0.578 on the downside versus 0.226 on the upside.
    Aliases: correlations go to one, correlation asymmetry, crisis correlation

    Hedge ratio #

    The ratio of the size of the position taken in a hedging instrument (e.g., futures contracts) to the size of the exposure being hedged; it determines how much of the price risk is offset by the hedge.
    Intuition: It is the proportionality dial on a hedge: ratio 1.0 means you hedge dollar-for-dollar; 0.6 means you take a hedge position only 60% as large as your exposure.
    Mechanics: When the asset underlying the futures contract is the same as the asset being hedged, a hedge ratio of 1.0 is commonly used; when cross hedging, the hedger chooses the ratio that minimizes the variance of the hedged position. The number of contracts is N = h * Q_A / Q_F, where Q_A is the exposure size and Q_F the contract size.
    Failure modes: A naive 1.0 hedge ratio ignores differences in volatility and correlation between the exposure and the hedging instrument, so it can over- or under-hedge; it also assumes the spot-futures relationship stays stable over the hedge life.
    Misconceptions: A hedge ratio of 1.0 is not automatically 'full protection': it is optimal only when spot and futures price changes move one-for-one, which cross hedges and basis risk routinely violate.
    Example: A producer expecting to sell 2 million gallons of fuel hedges with 42,000-gallon futures contracts; at a hedge ratio of 1.0 this requires about 48 contracts, but at the optimal ratio of 0.8 it requires 38.
    h = (size of hedge position) / (size of exposure); number of contracts N = h * Q_A / Q_F.

    Minimum-variance hedge ratio #

    The hedge ratio that minimizes the variance of the value of the hedged position, equal to the correlation between spot-price changes and futures-price changes times the ratio of their standard deviations; equivalently, the slope of the best-fit regression of spot-price changes on futures-price changes.
    Intuition: It is the hedge size that makes your combined position wobble the least, given how tightly the hedge instrument actually tracks your exposure.
    Mechanics: h* = rho * sigma_S / sigma_F, where rho is the correlation between Delta-S (spot change) and Delta-F (futures change) over the hedge horizon and sigma_S, sigma_F their standard deviations; parameters are usually estimated from historical data over non-overlapping intervals matching the hedge horizon. Hedge effectiveness is the R-squared of that regression - the proportion of variance eliminated.
    Failure modes: The ratio is estimated from historical spot-futures relationships and degrades when that relationship shifts (regime change, contract roll, structural breaks); it minimizes variance, not downside risk, and ignores transactions costs and margining (though tailing the hedge adjusts for daily settlement).
    Misconceptions: The optimal hedge ratio is generally not 1.0 even for liquid hedges, and minimizing variance is not the same as maximizing expected utility - a hedger may rationally choose a different ratio once costs and residual basis risk are considered.
    Example: An airline hedging jet fuel with heating-oil futures estimates rho = 0.9, sigma_S = 0.03, sigma_F = 0.04, giving h* = 0.675; for 2 million gallons and 42,000-gallon contracts it uses N* = 0.675 * 2,000,000/42,000, about 32 contracts.
    h* = rho * (sigma_S / sigma_F); optimal contracts N* = h* * Q_A / Q_F; effectiveness = R^2 of the regression of Delta-S on Delta-F.
    Aliases: optimal hedge ratio, MVHR, h*

    Cross hedge #

    A hedge in which the asset giving rise to the hedger's exposure differs from the asset underlying the futures (or other derivative) contract used for hedging, employed when no suitable contract exists on the exposure itself.
    Intuition: If you can't buy insurance on the exact thing you own, you insure something that tends to move with it - imperfectly.
    Mechanics: The hedger selects a correlated contract (e.g., heating-oil futures for a jet-fuel exposure) and typically uses the minimum-variance hedge ratio rather than 1.0, because spot and futures changes no longer match one-for-one; the residual mismatch is basis risk, which cross hedging usually increases.
    Failure modes: The hedge inherits basis risk between two different assets: their price relationship can weaken exactly in stressed markets, so the hedge may fail when most needed, and liquidity in the proxy contract adds rollover and margin costs.
    Misconceptions: A cross hedge is not a failed hedge by definition - with a well-chosen proxy and minimum-variance ratio it removes most variance - but it never removes all of it, and calling it 'fully hedged' overstates the protection.
    Example: An airline concerned about jet fuel prices uses heating-oil futures to hedge because jet-fuel futures are not actively traded; the price link between the two fuels makes the hedge useful but imperfect.
    Aliases: cross hedging

    Delta hedge #

    An options-hedging technique that offsets a position's delta - the theoretical sensitivity of the option's premium to a $1 move in the underlying - by taking an opposing position in the underlying, so the total position is approximately insensitive to small price moves.
    Intuition: If your option gains $0.50 when the stock rises $1, shorting half a share per option makes the combined position roughly flat for small moves.
    Mechanics: Delta is the first derivative of option value with respect to the underlying price (0 to 1 for long calls, 0 to -1 for long puts). A delta hedge sets total position delta to zero, e.g., shorting delta*N*100 shares against N long calls. Because delta changes as the underlying moves (gamma), the hedge must be rebalanced dynamically; Greeks are theoretical guideposts from a pricing model, not guarantees.
    Failure modes: Delta hedging is exact only locally and continuously: discrete rebalancing, gaps, and transaction costs leave residual P&L, and a delta-neutral book remains exposed to gamma (large moves), vega (volatility changes), and theta (time decay).
    Misconceptions: Delta-neutral does not mean risk-neutral: it neutralizes only the first-order price exposure at one point in time, and delta is also not literally the probability of finishing in the money, despite the common trader shorthand.
    Example: An investor long 10 calls with delta 0.50 shorts 500 shares (10 * 100 * 0.50); a $1 stock rise adds about $500 to the calls and loses about $500 on the shares, keeping the position near flat until delta changes.
    Position delta = sum over positions of (quantity * contract multiplier * delta_i); hedge shares = -position delta (per share delta of 1).
    Aliases: delta hedging, delta-neutral hedging

    Gamma hedge #

    An options-hedging technique that neutralizes a position's gamma - the rate at which delta changes per $1 move in the underlying - so that the delta hedge remains effective for larger price moves without constant rebalancing.
    Intuition: Gamma is how fast your delta hedge goes stale; hedging gamma keeps the hedge from drifting out of alignment every time the underlying moves.
    Mechanics: Long options (calls or puts) have positive gamma, short options negative gamma, and the underlying itself has zero gamma, so gamma can only be hedged by adding other options: the trader first sets total gamma to zero with a traded option, then restores delta neutrality with the underlying. Gamma is highest for at-the-money options near expiration.
    Failure modes: Gamma hedges are themselves unstable near expiration, when ATM gamma spikes, and require trading additional options with their own costs and vega exposure; a gamma-neutral book still carries higher-order and volatility risk.
    Misconceptions: You cannot gamma-hedge with the underlying stock - stock positions have no gamma because their delta is fixed at 1 or -1; only options change a position's gamma.
    Example: A market maker short a 0.50-delta call with gamma 0.06 buys enough of another listed option with gamma 0.03 to bring book gamma to zero, then adjusts shares to restore delta neutrality.
    Position gamma = sum over positions of (quantity * multiplier * gamma_i); choose an options position Q such that position gamma + Q * multiplier * gamma_hedge_instrument = 0, then re-neutralize delta.
    Aliases: gamma hedging, gamma-neutral hedging

    Vega hedge #

    An options-hedging technique that neutralizes a position's vega - the change in option premium for a one-percentage-point change in implied volatility - so the book is insulated from shifts in the market's volatility expectations.
    Intuition: Options get more expensive when expected volatility rises, even if prices don't move; a vega hedge makes your position indifferent to that repricing.
    Mechanics: Vega measures premium change per 1% (100-basis-point) change in implied volatility; it is larger for longer-dated options. Because the underlying has zero vega, vega must be hedged with offsetting option positions, after which delta (and possibly gamma) is re-neutralized; vega hedges across maturities leave term-structure-of-volatility risk.
    Failure modes: Implied volatility is not directly tradable, so vega hedges rely on other options whose own prices embed supply-demand distortions; vega also changes with price, time, and vol level, so the hedge decays, and short-dated and long-dated implied vols do not move in lockstep.
    Misconceptions: A vega-neutral book is not immune to volatility: it is hedged against a uniform 1% implied-vol shift, not against realized volatility gaps or non-parallel shifts across the volatility surface.
    Example: A trader long a 12-month call with vega 0.15 (gaining about $0.30 if implied vol rises 2%) shorts enough nearer-dated options totaling -0.15 vega per share equivalent, flattening the book's response to a volatility spike.
    Position vega = sum over positions of (quantity * multiplier * vega_i); offset with an options position whose aggregate vega equals -position vega.
    Aliases: vega hedging, volatility hedging

    Duration hedge #

    A fixed-income hedging technique that sizes an interest-rate futures (or bond) position so that the dollar sensitivity of the hedge to a small parallel yield change offsets the dollar duration of the portfolio being hedged.
    Intuition: If your bond portfolio loses $X when yields rise 1 basis point, you short enough futures to gain $X on that same move - matching sensitivities rather than face values.
    Mechanics: Price changes are approximated by Delta-P = -P * D_P * Delta-y for the portfolio and Delta-F = -F * D_F * Delta-y for the futures contract; setting the two to cancel gives the duration-based hedge ratio N = -(P * D_P) / (F * D_F). The key assumption is that all rates move by the same amount (parallel yield-curve shifts only).
    Failure modes: Duration is a first-order, parallel-shift approximation: non-parallel curve moves (steepening/flattening), convexity, and cheapest-to-deliver switching in bond futures all create residual risk, and short rates are generally more volatile than long rates in practice.
    Misconceptions: Matching durations is not matching maturities or notionals: two portfolios with the same face value can have very different interest-rate sensitivity, which is why the hedge ratio uses dollar duration (value times duration) rather than par amounts.
    Example: A $10 million bond portfolio with duration 8.8 years is hedged with Treasury futures priced at $91,000 with underlying duration 9.2: N = -(10,000,000*8.8)/(91,000*9.2), about -105 contracts short.
    N = -(P * D_P) / (F * D_F), where P and D_P are the portfolio's value and duration and F and D_F the futures contract's price and underlying duration; hedge effectiveness degrades under non-parallel shifts.
    Aliases: duration-based hedging, duration-based hedge ratio
