    Expansion #

    A broadening of price swings and bar ranges, reflecting rising volatility as one side of the market gains decisive control. It is the counterpart of compression and typically accompanies breakouts, breakdowns, and trend acceleration.
    Intuition: The coiled spring releases: small quiet bars give way to large directional ones.
    Mechanics: Observed as enlarging bar ranges and directional closes, often from a prior consolidation; quantified by widening Bollinger Bands (bands expand as the standard deviation of price rises) or rising ATR/BandWidth.
    Failure modes: Expansion driven by news spikes can be one-bar events that immediately mean-revert; chasing expansion late buys the extreme of the move.
    Misconceptions: That expansion confirms a durable new trend; an initial volatility burst can be exhaustion rather than initiation.
    Example: After weeks of narrow ranges, a stock prints consecutive wide-range up bars on heavy volume as it breaks out of its base — a volatility expansion phase.
    Aliases: range expansion, volatility expansion (synonym in price-action usage)

    Volatility contraction #

    A measurable decline in price volatility — shrinking bar ranges, falling standard deviation, narrowing volatility bands — typically during consolidation. Frameworks such as Bollinger's squeeze treat contraction as a precondition that often precedes a volatility expansion, a statistical tendency rather than a law.
    Intuition: The market goes quiet: each day's high-low span shrinks as price compresses into a tighter and tighter zone.
    Mechanics: Quantified with indicators such as Bollinger BandWidth (which falls as the bands contract with declining standard deviation), ATR, or historical volatility; identified visually as progressively smaller bars and converging swings.
    Failure modes: Contraction gives no directional information and no timing guarantee; volatility can stay depressed long after a squeeze registers.
    Misconceptions: That a volatility contraction must be followed by an explosive move; the tendency is documented, but individual squeezes frequently fizzle.
    Example: A stock's 20-day Bollinger Bands narrow to multi-month lows during a flat consolidation — the classic 'squeeze' setup watched for a subsequent break.
    Aliases: volatility squeeze, VCP (volatility contraction pattern, Minervini usage)

    Volatility expansion #

    A measurable increase in price volatility — widening bar ranges, rising standard deviation, expanding volatility bands — typically accompanying breakouts and trend legs. It is the mirror of volatility contraction and marks regimes where price travels further per unit time.
    Intuition: The quiet market wakes up: bars get longer and price starts covering ground fast.
    Mechanics: Quantified by rising Bollinger BandWidth (bands widen automatically as the standard deviation of price increases), rising ATR, or rising historical volatility; observed visually as a sequence of wide-range directional bars.
    Failure modes: Volatility expansion cuts both ways: it raises opportunity and risk simultaneously, and late entries during expansion phases suffer the widest adverse excursions.
    Misconceptions: That expanding volatility is bullish; it is direction-neutral — crashes are volatility expansions too.
    Example: A stock breaking out of a months-long base prints daily ranges three times its recent average, and its Bollinger Bands widen sharply as the new trend leg begins.
    Aliases: volatility breakout, range expansion

    Gap #

    A blank space on a price chart where no trading occurred between two consecutive bars — typically when the open of one session is entirely above the prior session's high (up gap) or below its low (down gap). Gaps are classified (common, breakaway, runaway, exhaustion) by location and context; the classifications are interpretive, not mechanical laws.
    Intuition: Overnight news or sentiment shifts the supply-demand balance so much that the market has to reopen at a different price entirely, skipping the levels in between.
    Mechanics: Detected by comparing one bar's range to the next: an up gap requires the new session's low to exceed the prior session's high; a down gap requires the new session's high to fall below the prior session's low. Significance is judged by size, volume, and position within the trend or pattern.
    Failure modes: Gap-type labels are only reliably assignable in hindsight (an exhaustion gap looks like a runaway gap when it prints); the popular belief that 'all gaps get filled' fails badly for breakaway and runaway gaps.
    Misconceptions: That every gap must soon be filled; waiting for breakaway or runaway gaps to fill can mean missing or fighting an entire trend leg.
    Example: A stock closes at 50, reports strong earnings after the bell, and opens the next day at 54, never trading below 53.8: the 50-53.8 void is an up gap.
    Aliases: price gap

    Gap up #

    A gap in which a session's low is higher than the previous session's high, leaving an untraded void below the new session's range. Generally read as a sign of strong buying interest; its analytical meaning depends on where in the trend or pattern it occurs.
    Intuition: Demand overwhelmed supply overnight, so the market had to reopen above yesterday's entire trading range.
    Mechanics: Identified when today's low exceeds yesterday's high on the chart. Interpretation follows gap typology: in a congestion area it may be a common gap, breaking out of a range a breakaway gap, mid-trend a runaway gap, and late in an extended move a possible exhaustion gap.
    Failure modes: Gap ups into extended moves can be exhaustion gaps that mark the top; and opening gaps that fail to hold through the close are just intraday volatility, not structural gaps.
    Misconceptions: That a gap up is automatically bullish follow-through fuel; context decides, and faded gaps are a daily occurrence in thin markets.
    Example: After weeks of consolidation, a stock gaps above its range resistance on heavy volume following earnings — a bullish breakaway-style gap up.
    Aliases: up gap

    Gap down #

    A gap in which a session's high is lower than the previous session's low, leaving an untraded void above the new session's range. Generally read as a sign of strong selling pressure; its analytical meaning depends on where in the trend or pattern it occurs.
    Intuition: Supply overwhelmed demand overnight, so the market had to reopen below yesterday's entire trading range.
    Mechanics: Identified when today's high falls below yesterday's low. Interpretation follows gap typology: common gap inside congestion, breakaway gap on a range breakdown, runaway gap mid-downtrend, or exhaustion gap late in an extended decline.
    Failure modes: Gap downs late in a decline can be exhaustion gaps that mark capitulation lows; intraday-only gaps that do not hold into the close carry no structural significance.
    Misconceptions: That a gap down guarantees further downside; panic gap downs are frequently filled quickly as the selling exhausts itself.
    Example: A stock in a trading range gaps below range support on heavy volume after a profit warning — a bearish breakaway-style gap down.
    Aliases: down gap

    Exhaustion gap #

    A gap occurring near the end of an extended up or down move, typically on very high volume and a large price jump, interpreted as the final surge of euphoria or panic before the move ends. The label is only assignable with confidence in hindsight, since at the time it can be indistinguishable from a runaway gap.
    Intuition: The last burst of FOMO buying (or panic selling) — the crowd all rushes in at once, and then there is nobody left to push further.
    Mechanics: Identified by context: a gap late in a prolonged trend, accompanied by exceptionally high volume and often a wide open-to-close range, followed quickly by the gap being filled as price reverses. ChartSchool notes the exceptionally high volume is what separates it from a runaway gap.
    Failure modes: Mistaking a runaway gap for an exhaustion gap leads to premature counter-trend positioning; the two look identical at print time and are separated mainly by what volume and follow-through do next.
    Misconceptions: That any big late-trend gap is exhaustion; trends can produce several large gaps before truly exhausting.
    Example: After a long advance, a stock gaps up on huge volume, stalls as profit-taking dries up demand, and within days fills the gap and reverses — the exhaustion signature.

    Breakaway gap #

    A gap that occurs as price breaks out of a trading range or congestion area, interpreted as marking the start of a new directional move. Breakaway gaps are conventionally expected NOT to be filled soon, distinguishing them from common gaps; the classification remains an interpretive heuristic.
    Intuition: The market escapes its cage in one jump — enthusiasm is so strong that price skips the boundary levels entirely.
    Mechanics: Identified by location (at the boundary of a congestion area or chart pattern) and by volume, which should pick up significantly. The breakout point is then expected to act as new support (upside break) or resistance (downside break); a prompt fill argues against the breakaway reading.
    Failure modes: A gap out of a range that is quickly filled was a false breakout with extra steps; low-volume breakaway gaps are especially suspect.
    Misconceptions: That a breakaway gap will soon be filled, offering a better entry; the whole point of the classification is that waiting for the fill often means missing the move.
    Example: ChartSchool's example: after weeks of consolidation and a low-volume poke above resistance, a stock gaps up on high volume, starting a new uptrend — a breakaway gap.
    Aliases: breakout gap

    Runaway gap #

    A gap occurring in the middle of an established trend, driven by a sudden surge of interest (or liquidation) in the trend's direction, and interpreted as evidence the trend is accelerating rather than ending. Also called a measuring gap, from the heuristic that it tends to appear roughly halfway through a move.
    Intuition: Traders who waited for a pullback give up waiting and pile in all at once, gapping price further along the trend.
    Mechanics: Identified by location mid-trend with a notable volume increase during and after the gap. The measuring heuristic projects the remaining move as approximately equal to the move from the trend's start to the gap.
    Failure modes: The halfway-point measuring heuristic fails routinely, and a supposed runaway gap can turn out to be the exhaustion gap that ends the move.
    Misconceptions: That mid-trend gaps are safe to chase; runaway gaps in downtrends reflect liquidation cascades where price must fall further to find buyers, and entries against them are dangerous.
    Example: A stock in a strong uptrend gaps up mid-move on surging volume as sidelined traders stop waiting for a retracement — the classic runaway gap described by ChartSchool.
    Measuring heuristic: projected objective approximately equals gap price plus (gap price minus move origin) for an up-move; a rule of thumb, not a validated formula.
    Aliases: continuation gap, measuring gap

    Common gap #

    A small, uneventful gap occurring within a trading range or congestion area, typically on low volume (e.g., around ex-dividend dates), with no trend significance. Common gaps are usually filled quickly.
    Intuition: A mundane hiccup in an already quiet market — price skips a few ticks and nobody much cares.
    Mechanics: Identified by context rather than size: the gap appears inside an existing range or congestion area on unremarkable volume. Because the range itself is expected to contain price, the gap is normally retraced ('filled') within days to weeks.
    Failure modes: The main risk is misclassification — treating a genuine breakaway gap as a common gap and fading a real breakout.
    Misconceptions: That all gaps are meaningful signals; common gaps in thin securities are closer to noise and offer little trading information.
    Example: A thinly traded stock goes ex-dividend and opens slightly lower inside its range, leaving a small gap that is filled within the week — a common gap.
    Aliases: trading gap, area gap

    Trendline #

    A straight line drawn on a chart connecting two or more price points and extended forward, used to visualize a trend and to act as a dynamic reference for support (up trendline, connecting rising lows) or resistance (down trendline, connecting falling highs). It is a discretionary drawing tool, not an objective market feature.
    Intuition: Connect the dots of the trend's pullbacks, and the resulting line sketches the trend's natural floor (or ceiling).
    Mechanics: An up trendline connects two or more ascending lows (the second low must exceed the first); a down trendline connects two or more descending highs. More touches without a break are taken as evidence of significance; a close beyond the line is a warning that the trend may be changing, to be confirmed by other evidence.
    Failure modes: Placement is subjective — different analysts draw different lines on the same chart; trendline breaks generate frequent false warnings, especially on steep or shallow trends.
    Misconceptions: That a trendline break proves the trend has reversed; Fidelity itself cautions it should serve only as a warning requiring confirmation.
    Example: Connecting the pullback lows at 50, 52, and 54 in a rising stock yields an up trendline; price bouncing off the line a fourth time is read as the uptrend remaining intact.
    Aliases: trend line, up trendline / down trendline

    Channel #

    A chart structure formed by two parallel trendlines that enclose price action: a primary trendline along the trend's pullbacks and a parallel channel (return) line along the opposite swings. Channels frame trend direction plus likely reaction zones; they are descriptive constructs, not predictive guarantees.
    Intuition: Price travels between two parallel rails — one rail supports it, the other caps it — until it jumps the track.
    Mechanics: Draw the trendline through the trend's lows (uptrend) or highs (downtrend), then draw a parallel line through the opposite extreme(s). Traders reference the lower line as support and the upper as resistance; breaks of either rail are read as acceleration or potential trend change depending on direction.
    Failure modes: True parallel structure is rarer than textbooks suggest; forced parallelism produces channels that price ignores, and channel breaks fail like any other level break.
    Misconceptions: That price must reverse at the channel rails; strong trends ride one rail for extended periods, and breaks of the far rail often accelerate rather than reverse the move.
    Example: A rising stock respects a rising support line at its pullback lows while a parallel line through its rally highs caps each advance, forming an ascending channel.
    Aliases: price channel, trend channel

    Ascending channel #

    A channel whose two parallel boundary lines slope upward, enclosing an uptrend's higher highs and higher lows. The lower (support) line connects rising lows and the parallel upper (resistance) line caps the rallies; classically read as trend-consistent structure until broken.
    Intuition: An uptrend with guardrails: each dip bounces off the lower rail, each push fades near the upper rail.
    Mechanics: Constructed by drawing an up trendline through two or more rising swing lows and a parallel line through the intervening swing high(s). Range-trading heuristics buy near the lower rail and take profits near the upper; a break below the lower rail warns of trend change.
    Failure modes: Upside breaks of the upper rail can be climactic exhaustion moves; downside breaks of the support rail during what looked like orderly ascent catch range traders long at the top rail.
    Misconceptions: That an ascending channel is purely bullish; a downside break of its support rail is a recognized reversal warning, and rising channels in weak trends often fail downward.
    Example: A stock's pullbacks bottom on a rising line through 50, 53, 56 while rallies stall on the parallel line through 55, 58, 61 — an ascending channel.
    Aliases: rising channel, up channel

    Descending channel #

    A channel whose two parallel boundary lines slope downward, enclosing a downtrend's lower highs and lower lows. The upper (resistance) line connects falling highs and the parallel lower (support) line floors the declines; classically read as trend-consistent structure until broken.
    Intuition: A downtrend with guardrails: each bounce dies at the upper rail, each drop finds footing near the lower rail.
    Mechanics: Constructed by drawing a down trendline through two or more falling swing highs and a parallel line through the intervening swing low(s). Heuristics sell rallies near the upper rail and cover near the lower; a break above the upper rail warns of trend change.
    Failure modes: Downside breaks of the lower rail can be capitulation spikes that immediately reverse; upside breaks catch shorts who assumed the rails would hold.
    Misconceptions: That a descending channel is purely bearish; an upside break of its resistance rail is a recognized reversal warning.
    Example: A stock's bounces stall on a falling line through 100, 95, 90 while declines bottom on the parallel line through 92, 87, 82 — a descending channel.
    Aliases: falling channel, down channel

    Horizontal channel #

    A channel whose two parallel boundary lines are roughly horizontal, enclosing a sideways market: resistance above and support below at approximately constant prices. It represents consolidation or indecision and is the channel equivalent of a trading range.
    Intuition: Price bounces between a flat floor and a flat ceiling while the market makes up its mind.
    Mechanics: Constructed by drawing a horizontal line across the reaction highs and a parallel horizontal line across the reaction lows. Range-trading heuristics buy support and sell resistance until a decisive breakout occurs; the direction of the eventual break is treated as the resolution signal.
    Failure modes: Horizontal channels produce repeated false breaks at both rails; tight stops just outside the rails are exactly where whipsaws cluster.
    Misconceptions: That a horizontal channel's eventual break is equally likely up or down in all contexts; prior trend and volume behavior skew the odds, though never to certainty.
    Example: A stock oscillates between 70 support and 75 resistance for weeks, forming a horizontal channel, until a high-volume close above 75 resolves it upward.
    Aliases: flat channel, rectangular channel, rectangle

    Fibonacci retracement #

    A charting technique that marks horizontal levels at percentages of a prior price swing — conventionally 23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8%, and 78.6% (ratios derived from the Fibonacci sequence, except 50%, a Dow Theory convention) — as candidate support/resistance zones for a counter-trend move. It is an analytical framework with no established predictive validity on its own.
    Intuition: If trends tend to give back part of a move before resuming, these ratios are the conventional yardsticks for how much.
    Mechanics: The analyst selects a swing low and swing high; the platform draws levels at the fixed percentages of that range. In an uptrend the levels below the high are watched as pullback support; in a downtrend, as rally resistance. Shallow (38.2%) retracements are read as trend strength, deep (61.8%-78.6%) as reversal risk.
    Failure modes: Swing-point selection is subjective, so different analysts get different levels; prices break through Fibonacci levels routinely, and the tool is unreliable in choppy, trendless markets.
    Misconceptions: That the ratios have a natural or mathematical claim on market behavior; any efficacy is usually attributed to self-fulfilling order clustering, and critics note confirmation bias in how levels are applied.
    Example: S&P 500 e-mini futures, January 2025 (Investopedia worked example): pivot high 6162.25, pivot low 5809; the 61.8% retracement level is computed at 5944.00 and watched as potential pullback support.
    Retracement level (up-leg) = High - r x (High - Low), with r in {0.236, 0.382, 0.5, 0.618, 0.786}; e.g., High 6162.25, Low 5809 gives the 61.8% level at 6162.25 - 0.618 x 353.25 = 5944.00.
    Aliases: Fib retracement, Fib levels

    Fibonacci extension #

    A charting technique that projects levels beyond the prior swing — conventionally 100%, 161.8%, and 261.8% of the measured move — as candidate profit-target or exhaustion zones once the trend resumes. Like retracements, it is an analytical convention, not a validated forecasting law.
    Intuition: If the retracement yardstick measures how far price pulls back, the extension yardstick guesses how far the next leg might reach.
    Mechanics: Applied after a trend resumes: the prior swing's range (or the swing plus its retracement, in three-point projection tools) is multiplied by extension ratios and projected past the old extreme. The 161.8% projection is the most widely watched target.
    Failure modes: All the subjectivity of retracements applies, compounded by the choice of anchor points; trends frequently stop well short of, or blow through, projected extensions.
    Misconceptions: That extension targets are high-probability exit prices; they are reference zones at best, and many moves never reach the 161.8% projection.
    Example: After a stock rallies 40 to 50 and resumes upward from a 46 pullback, the 161.8% extension of the 40-50 leg projects a target zone near 62.2 (46 + 1.618 x 10).
    Extension level (up-move) = Low + e x (High - Low) with e > 1 (e.g., 1.618); three-point variant: target = RetracementLow + e x (High - Low).
    Aliases: Fib extension, Fibonacci projection

    Pivot point #

    A calculated price level derived from the prior period's high, low, and close, used to frame the current period's directional bias and to derive support/resistance levels. Originating with floor traders, pivot points are deterministic calculations; their use as turning-point levels is a convention, not a validated edge.
    Intuition: Yesterday's average price becomes today's line in the sand: above it leans bullish, below it leans bearish.
    Mechanics: The base pivot P = (High + Low + Close)/3 of the prior period; derived levels include S1 = 2P - High, S2 = P - (High - Low), R1 = 2P - Low, R2 = P + (High - Low). Levels are computed once per period and remain fixed; variants include Fibonacci and Demark pivots.
    Failure modes: In strongly trending periods price may never interact with the pivot, and in whippy markets it slices through the levels repeatedly; the levels have no inherent causal force.
    Misconceptions: That pivot levels are precise reversal points; ChartSchool treats them as a framework to be confirmed with price action and other indicators.
    Example: Yesterday's H/L/C are 105, 99, 102: P = 102, R1 = 105, S1 = 99. A move above 102 early today is read as positive, targeting R1 at 105.
    P = (H + L + C)/3; S1 = 2P - H; S2 = P - (H - L); R1 = 2P - L; R2 = P + (H - L), where H, L, C are the prior period's high, low, close.
    Aliases: floor trader pivot, standard pivot point, PP

    Daily pivot #

    A pivot point computed from the previous trading day's high, low, and close, applied to the current day's trading — the original floor-trader usage. On StockCharts' convention, pivots for 1- to 15-minute intraday charts use the prior day's data and remain fixed all day.
    Intuition: Yesterday's H/L/C distill into today's map of where buyers and sellers are expected to contest control.
    Mechanics: Computed at the start of the day as P = (prior day H + L + C)/3 with the standard derived S1/S2/R1/R2 levels; the levels do not change intraday and are used as bias filter (price above or below P) and as support/resistance references.
    Failure modes: Gap openings can start the day far beyond the pivot, making the levels irrelevant; quiet range days slice back and forth across P, generating noise.
    Misconceptions: That daily pivots are intraday reversal guarantees; they are reference levels whose reactions must be confirmed.
    Example: An index future closes at 5000 with a 5030 high and 4970 low; today's daily pivot set is P = 5000, R1 = 5030, S1 = 4970, watched on the 5-minute chart all session.
    P = (H_prev_day + L_prev_day + C_prev_day)/3; S1 = 2P - H; R1 = 2P - L; S2 = P - (H - L); R2 = P + (H - L).
    Aliases: daily pivot point

    Weekly pivot #

    A pivot point computed from the previous calendar week's high, low, and close, applied to the current week. On StockCharts' convention, pivots for 30- to 120-minute charts use the prior week's data and remain fixed for the entire week.
    Intuition: Last week's price action condenses into this week's bias line and outer reference levels.
    Mechanics: Computed at the week's start as P = (prior week H + L + C)/3 with derived S/R levels; fixed for the week and recalculated on the first trading day of the next week.
    Failure modes: Same structural limits as all pivots: strong weekly trends can leave the pivot behind on day one, and holiday-shortened weeks distort the inputs.
    Misconceptions: That weekly pivots are more 'reliable' than daily ones by nature; they are merely slower-moving references with the same heuristic status.
    Example: Last week a stock printed H 110, L 100, C 108: this week's weekly pivot is P = 106, with R1 = 112 and S1 = 102 on the 60-minute chart.
    P = (H_prev_week + L_prev_week + C_prev_week)/3; S1 = 2P - H; R1 = 2P - L; S2 = P - (H - L); R2 = P + (H - L).
    Aliases: weekly pivot point

    Monthly pivot #

    A pivot point computed from the previous month's high, low, and close, applied to the current month. On StockCharts' convention, pivots displayed on daily charts use the prior month's data and remain fixed for the whole month (weekly and monthly charts use the prior year's data).
    Intuition: Last month's range and close set this month's reference grid for where longer-term buyers and sellers may engage.
    Mechanics: Computed on the first trading day of the month as P = (prior month H + L + C)/3 with derived S/R levels; fixed until the next month begins, and used on daily charts as slower bias and support/resistance references.
    Failure modes: Monthly levels are coarse on volatile instruments, often sitting far from price and untouched for weeks; gaps between months can strand the grid entirely.
    Misconceptions: That monthly pivots carry institutional weight by construction; they are arithmetic averages of last month's range, watched only by those who choose to watch them.
    Example: A stock printed H 120, L 100, C 115 in May: June's monthly pivot is P = 111.67, plotted on the daily chart for all of June.
    P = (H_prev_month + L_prev_month + C_prev_month)/3; S1 = 2P - H; R1 = 2P - L; S2 = P - (H - L); R2 = P + (H - L).
    Aliases: monthly pivot point

    Market structure break #

    In price-structure analysis, a break of the swing sequence defining the current trend: in an uptrend, a close below the most recent higher low (or, in continuation terms, a close above the prior swing high that extends the structure). SMC/ICT usage distinguishes a trend-continuing break of structure from a counter-trend structure shift; the terminology is a discretionary convention without a single canonical authority.
    Intuition: The market's staircase pattern breaks a step — the rhythm of higher floors or lower ceilings is violated, so the structure may be changing.
    Mechanics: Map swing highs and lows; a trend-continuing BOS prints when price exceeds the last swing high in an uptrend (or undercuts the last swing low in a downtrend), while a structure break against the trend prints when the last higher low (or lower high) is taken out. Confirmation conventions vary: some require a close beyond the swing, others a wick.
    Failure modes: Marginal wick-throughs of a swing look like structure breaks but resolve as stop runs; and without a fixed swing-definition rule, two analysts mark different structures on the same chart.
    Misconceptions: That a single broken swing proves trend reversal; a wick through a level that closes back inside is a sweep, not a break — acceptance beyond the level is the distinguishing test.
    Example: An uptrend with higher lows at 50, 52, 54 breaks down when price closes below 54: the structure break flips bias from buying dips to selling rallies.
    Aliases: break of structure, BOS, MSB

    Change of character #

    In SMC/ICT vocabulary, the first structural break against the prevailing trend — e.g., in an uptrend, the first close below a higher low — read as an early signal that order flow may be shifting. It is the counter-trend counterpart of a trend-continuing break of structure; a discretionary convention with no canonical authority.
    Intuition: The first crack in the trend's rhythm: the market does something it hasn't been doing, hinting the character of the move is changing.
    Mechanics: After establishing the trend's swing sequence, mark the most recent higher low (uptrend) or lower high (downtrend); a decisive break of that pivot against the trend is the change of character. SMC models typically require a preceding liquidity sweep and subsequent displacement before treating it as tradable evidence.
    Failure modes: CHoCH signals proliferate on lower timeframes and most are noise within higher-timeframe trends; deep pullbacks routinely trigger counter-trend structure breaks without reversing the main trend.
    Misconceptions: That a CHoCH is a sell/buy signal by itself; within SMC it is context requiring confirmation (displacement, a return to an order block or gap), not an entry trigger.
    Example: After a sweep of the prior high, an uptrending pair closes below its last higher low on the 15-minute chart — a CHoCH that SMC traders take as the first evidence of a possible short-side shift.
    Aliases: CHoCH, ChoCh, market structure shift (MSS)

    Liquidity sweep #

    In SMC/ICT vocabulary, a run through a level where resting orders cluster — stop-losses and breakout entries just beyond an obvious high or low — followed by failure to hold beyond it: price trades through, fills those orders, and closes back inside the prior range. The same chart event is called a false breakout in classical TA; 'sweep' adds an interpretation (order collection) that a chart cannot prove.
    Intuition: Price pokes through an obvious level, triggers the crowd's stops, then snaps back — the level was the target of the move, not an obstacle in its way.
    Mechanics: Identification is a two-part check: mark pools where stops cluster (prior swing highs/lows, equal highs/lows, session extremes), then require the failure — a trade through the level (often on a wick) followed by a close back inside the old range. A close that holds beyond the level with continuation is a breakout, not a sweep.
    Failure modes: At the moment of the break a sweep and a genuine breakout are indistinguishable — only the close and follow-through separate them, so acting early means sometimes fading real breakouts.
    Misconceptions: That sweeps prove a market maker deliberately hunted retail stops; order-flow research documents stop clustering at predictable levels, but intent is narrative, not observable fact.
    Example: Price wicks above a prior swing high where buy stops rest, immediately rejects, and closes back inside the range; SMC traders read the level as swept and look for short setups on the retrace.
    Aliases: stop hunt, stop run, grab, purge, raid

    Stop run #

    A move that pushes price through a level where stop-loss orders are known or presumed to cluster, triggering them, often followed by a quick reversal. In SMC vocabulary this is the liquidity sweep; in classical terms it overlaps with the false breakout. The deliberate-targeting framing is an inference, not an observable fact.
    Intuition: Obvious levels have crowds of stops parked behind them; a push through sets off that cascade in one quick burst.
    Mechanics: Identified by the footprint: a fast trade through a well-watched swing high/low or round number, a burst of activity as stops execute, and (in the sweep reading) failure to hold beyond the level. Buy stops sit above highs, sell stops below lows, so the run direction names the pool being executed.
    Failure modes: Not every run through a level reverses — sometimes the stop cascade fuels a genuine breakout; distinguishing the two requires waiting for the close and follow-through.
    Misconceptions: That brokers or market makers visibly hunt individual retail stops; academic work documents stop clustering and unusual behavior at such levels, but the personalized stop-hunt story is contested.
    Example: A pair trades two ticks below an obvious equal-lows level, triggering clustered sell stops, then closes back above the level within the same bar — a classic stop-run footprint.
    Aliases: stop hunt, running the stops, stop raid
