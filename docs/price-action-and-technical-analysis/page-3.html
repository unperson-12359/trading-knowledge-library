    Supply zone reviewed #

    A price band marking the base from which a strong decline launched, drawn (in supply-and-demand methodology) around the small consolidation preceding the impulsive drop. It is treated as potential resistance on a return, on the hypothesis that unfilled sell interest remains there — a discretionary zone framework related to, but looser than, classical resistance.
    Intuition: Price paused, then fell out of bed; the pause zone is where sellers were last in control, so the first return there may meet them again.
    Mechanics: Identify an impulsive decline; step back to the small base candles just before it. Draw the zone around the base — proximal line at the edge nearest current price, distal line at the base's extreme — and extend it right. Reversal zones (rally-base-drop) form at swing turns; continuation zones (drop-base-drop) form mid-swing.
    Failure modes: Zones are re-drawn to fit hindsight, and price trades straight through many of them; continuation-type (RBR/DBD) zones are documented by practitioners as weaker than reversal-type zones.
    Misconceptions: That a supply zone is proof of institutional sell orders waiting there; the zone marks where price left a base impulsively, and the unfilled-orders story is an inference, not an observation.
    Example: EUR/USD pauses for three small candles at 1.0850-1.0860, then drops 100 pips; the base is drawn as a supply zone and watched for a short entry on the first return.
    Aliases: supply area, drop-base-drop / rally-base-drop zone (formation names)

    Demand zone reviewed #

    A price band marking the base from which a strong advance launched, drawn (in supply-and-demand methodology) around the small consolidation preceding the impulsive rally. It is treated as potential support on a return, on the hypothesis that unfilled buy interest remains there — a discretionary zone framework related to, but looser than, classical support.
    Intuition: Price paused, then took off upward; the pause zone is where buyers were last in control, so the first return there may attract them again.
    Mechanics: Identify an impulsive rally; step back to the small base candles just before it and box the base (proximal edge nearest current price, distal edge at the base extreme). Reversal zones (drop-base-rally) form at swing lows; continuation zones (rally-base-rally) form mid-swing and are regarded by practitioners as weaker.
    Failure modes: Zone boundaries are subjective and hindsight-fitted; price frequently slices through demand zones without reacting, especially continuation-type zones formed mid-swing.
    Misconceptions: That an untouched ('fresh') demand zone must hold on first test; freshness heuristics are practitioner rules of thumb without validated statistics.
    Example: GBP/JPY bases at 182.00-182.30, then rallies sharply; the base is drawn as a demand zone, and a later retracement into it produces a bullish engulfing entry.
    Aliases: demand area, rally-base-rally / drop-base-rally zone (formation names)

    Order block reviewed #

    In SMC/ICT vocabulary, the last opposite-direction candle (or consecutive cluster of them) before an impulsive move — the final down candle before a rally (bullish) or the final up candle before a decline (bearish) — marked as a zone that may be defended on a return. It is read as the footprint of institutional position-building; that reading is an interpretation, since the candle alone shows only price, not orders.
    Intuition: The last place price sat before the big move launched is treated as the move's origin — and origins are where defenders may remain.
    Mechanics: Find the displacement first (an impulsive leg that breaks structure or sweeps a swing, ideally leaving a fair value gap), then step back to the last opposite close and box its range — conventions include full high-low, body only, or the 50% mean threshold. A decisive close through the whole zone invalidates it; the failed zone is then watched as a 'breaker' from the other side.
    Failure modes: Candidate blocks are everywhere, so most are meaningless; price trades straight through a large share of them, and there is no reliable published hit rate for the concept.
    Misconceptions: That an order block visibly shows institutional orders; only the candle is observable, and the institutional-footprint narrative cannot be verified from the chart.
    Example: A pair sweeps a low, then rallies impulsively through the prior swing high; the last down-close candle before that leg is boxed as a bullish order block, watched for longs on the retrace (mitigation).
    Aliases: OB, bullish/bearish order block

    Fair value gap reviewed #

    In SMC/ICT vocabulary, a three-candle pattern in which the middle candle's range does not overlap with the wicks of its neighbors — the low of the third candle sits above the high of the first (bullish FVG), or the high of the third sits below the low of the first (bearish FVG) — leaving a span price crossed in a single fast pass. It is read as an inefficiency that price may later revisit; that expectation is a hypothesis, not an established regularity.
    Intuition: The middle candle moved so fast that a slice of prices was skipped; the gap marks the skipped slice.
    Mechanics: Scan consecutive three-candle sequences: for a bullish FVG, check whether candle 3's low exceeds candle 1's high — the gap is the void between them. Traders mark the span (sometimes refined to its midpoint, the 'consequent encroachment') and watch for a retracement into it as entry context, with invalidation beyond the gap's far edge.
    Failure modes: FVGs are extremely common, especially on low timeframes, so most carry no information; many are never filled, and 'price returns to balance inefficiencies' is an untested slogan rather than a documented tendency.
    Misconceptions: That an FVG must be filled; unlike session gaps (where 'gap fill' at least has a mechanical definition), there is no validated fill-rate for FVGs, and the concept repackages older notions of price vacuums.
    Example: Candle 1 tops at 100.20, candle 2 surges to close at 102, and candle 3 bottoms at 101.50: the 100.20-101.50 span is a bullish fair value gap watched for support on a retrace.
    Bullish FVG exists when Low(candle3) > High(candle1); gap span = [High(candle1), Low(candle3)]. Bearish FVG exists when High(candle3) < Low(candle1); span = [High(candle3), Low(candle1)].
    Aliases: FVG, imbalance, price inefficiency

    Imbalance zone reviewed #

    A chart region where price moved so quickly in one direction that little two-sided trading occurred — operationalized in SMC/ICT practice chiefly as the fair value gap, the three-candle non-overlap span. The zone is hypothesized to attract a later revisit ('rebalancing'); the hypothesis is discretionary and unproven.
    Intuition: A one-sided stampede leaves thin ice on the chart — a stretch where almost no business was done.
    Mechanics: Identified as the span between the first and third candles' non-overlapping wicks in a fast three-candle move (the FVG definition), or more loosely as any fast, one-directional price leg with minimal overlap. Marked as a zone and watched for retracement entries in the direction of the originating move.
    Failure modes: The 'imbalance gets rebalanced' expectation fails constantly; trends leave trails of unfilled imbalances behind them, and the term is vague enough to be fitted to almost any fast move.
    Misconceptions: That an imbalance is a market defect the price must repair; markets have no obligation to revisit thinly traded spans, and session gaps (a different phenomenon) are often conflated with this concept.
    Example: During a news-driven spike, three consecutive 5-minute candles leave a 15-point span untouched between the first candle's high and the third's low — an imbalance zone marked for a possible retrace entry.
    Aliases: imbalance, price imbalance, inefficiency zone

    Premium zone reviewed #

    In SMC/ICT vocabulary, the upper half of a 'dealing range' — the leg between a significant swing low and swing high — i.e., everything above the 50% midpoint. The convention frames longs taken in premium as poor value and shorts in premium as acceptable value; it is a positioning heuristic, not a valuation measure.
    Intuition: Split the range in half; buying in the top half means paying up relative to where the market has just been.
    Mechanics: Anchor the dealing range at a meaningful swing low and high; the midpoint is equilibrium and the region above it is premium. SMC models look for sell-side setups (bearish order blocks, bearish FVGs) located in premium, aligned with the higher-timeframe bias.
    Failure modes: In strong trends price lives in premium for long stretches, so 'expensive' stays expensive; the framework also depends entirely on which swing anchors are chosen.
    Misconceptions: That premium means overvalued in any fundamental sense; it is purely a relative position within one analyst-chosen range.
    Example: With a dealing range of 1800-1900, equilibrium sits at 1850; a bearish order block at 1875 lies in premium, qualifying it — under SMC convention — as a short-entry zone.
    Equilibrium = (range high + range low) / 2; premium zone = prices above that midpoint within the dealing range.
    Aliases: premium, premium array (contextual)

    Discount zone reviewed #

    In SMC/ICT vocabulary, the lower half of a 'dealing range' — the leg between a significant swing low and swing high — i.e., everything below the 50% midpoint. The convention frames longs taken in discount as acceptable value and shorts in discount as poor value; a positioning heuristic, not a valuation measure.
    Intuition: Split the range in half; buying in the bottom half means paying less than the range's typical price.
    Mechanics: Anchor the dealing range at a meaningful swing low and high; the midpoint is equilibrium and the region below it is discount. SMC models look for buy-side setups (bullish order blocks, bullish FVGs) located in discount, aligned with the higher-timeframe bias.
    Failure modes: In strong downtrends price keeps getting 'cheaper' without bouncing, so discount buys can average into a falling market; results hinge on discretionary anchor selection.
    Misconceptions: That discount means undervalued or due to rise; it only means 'below the midpoint of the chosen range', which says nothing about where price goes next.
    Example: With a dealing range of 1800-1900, a bullish order block at 1825 lies in discount (below the 1850 equilibrium), qualifying it — under SMC convention — as a long-entry zone.
    Equilibrium = (range high + range low) / 2; discount zone = prices below that midpoint within the dealing range.
    Aliases: discount, discount array (contextual)

    Equilibrium level reviewed #

    In SMC/ICT vocabulary, the 50% midpoint of a dealing range (a significant swing low to swing high), separating the premium half above from the discount half below. It serves as the reference for the methodology's value heuristic; outside SMC, a 50% midpoint is simply the midpoint of a range (also a Dow Theory retracement convention).
    Intuition: The range's center of gravity: above it price is relatively expensive, below it relatively cheap, within that range.
    Mechanics: Computed as (swing high + swing low)/2 of the chosen dealing range and drawn as a horizontal reference. SMC models use it to filter setups (longs below, shorts above) and as a mean-threshold reference on individual candles and gaps.
    Failure modes: The level moves whenever the analyst re-anchors the range, so it is unstable as a signal; price shows no documented obligation to react at a range midpoint.
    Misconceptions: That equilibrium is a supply-demand balance price the market returns to; it is an arithmetic midpoint of an arbitrarily chosen range, not an economic equilibrium.
    Example: A dealing range from 1800 to 1900 puts equilibrium at 1850; price stalls near 1850 on its way from the discount half toward the range high.
    Equilibrium = (range high + range low) / 2.
    Aliases: equilibrium, 50% level, consequent encroachment (when applied to a gap), dealing range midpoint
