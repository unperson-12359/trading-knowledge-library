    Trend #

    The general direction in which a security's price is traveling, inferred from the direction of successive peaks and troughs on a chart. Classical technical analysis, following Dow Theory, classifies trends as up, down, or sideways and by duration (primary, secondary, minor); it is an analytical framework for describing price behavior, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Prices rarely move in straight lines — they zigzag. The trend is simply the direction in which those zigzags lean.
    Mechanics: Identified from price structure: ascending peaks and troughs indicate an uptrend, descending peaks and troughs a downtrend, and roughly equal peaks and troughs a sideways trend. Analysts commonly visualize trends with trendlines connecting lows or highs, or with moving averages, and classify them by length (primary: ~1 year or more; secondary: weeks to months; minor: under a month).
    Failure modes: A trend's turning points are only unambiguous in hindsight, so trend identification always lags; the same security can simultaneously be in an uptrend on one timeframe and a downtrend on another, making the label timeframe-dependent.
    Misconceptions: That a trend is a force that must continue ('the trend is your friend' taken literally). Apparent trends can arise in random price paths, and trend labels describe the past rather than guarantee the future.
    Example: A stock rises from 50 to 55, dips to 52, then rises to 58: the ascending peaks (55, 58) and troughs (50, 52) define an uptrend.
    Aliases: market trend, price trend

    Uptrend #

    A trend characterized by a series of ascending peaks and troughs — successively higher highs and higher lows. It is the classical chart-based description of sustained demand exceeding supply; an analytical construct, not evidence that prices must keep rising.
    Intuition: Each rally carries price further than the last, and each dip stops at a higher floor — a staircase climbing upward.
    Mechanics: Identified when each successive swing high exceeds the prior swing high and each successive swing low holds above the prior swing low. Often visualized with an up trendline connecting two or more rising lows; the uptrend is conventionally considered intact while price stays above that line or the last higher low.
    Failure modes: The structure breaks the moment a lower low prints, but that break is only confirmable after it happens; sharp news-driven declines can invalidate an uptrend without warning.
    Misconceptions: That every dip in an uptrend is a buying opportunity; any pullback can deepen into a full reversal, and the difference is clear only in retrospect.
    Example: A stock rises 50 to 55, pulls back to 52, rises to 58, then pulls back to 54: higher highs (55, 58) and higher lows (52, 54) mark an uptrend.
    Aliases: bullish trend, rising trend

    Downtrend #

    A trend characterized by a series of descending peaks and troughs — successively lower highs and lower lows. It is the classical chart-based description of sustained supply exceeding demand; an analytical construct, not evidence that prices must keep falling.
    Intuition: Each rally stalls lower than the last and each drop breaks to a new floor — a staircase descending.
    Mechanics: Identified when each successive swing high forms below the prior swing high and each successive swing low breaks below the prior swing low. Often visualized with a down trendline connecting two or more falling highs; the downtrend is conventionally considered intact while price stays below that line or the last lower high.
    Failure modes: Counter-trend rallies within downtrends can be sharp and prolonged, and are indistinguishable from genuine reversals until the structure confirms one way or the other.
    Misconceptions: That a downtrend means every bounce should be shorted immediately; bear-market rallies can retrace large portions of a decline before the downtrend resumes.
    Example: A stock falls from 100 to 90, bounces to 95, then falls to 88: lower highs (100, 95) and lower lows (90, 88) mark a downtrend.
    Aliases: bearish trend, falling trend

    Sideways market #

    A market state in which prices oscillate within a horizontal band with roughly equal peaks and troughs, reflecting a balance between supply and demand rather than directional movement. Also described as consolidation or a ranging market; a descriptive label for price behavior, not a forecast.
    Intuition: Buyers and sellers are evenly matched, so price ping-pongs between an upper and a lower boundary.
    Mechanics: Identified when swing highs cluster near a common resistance level and swing lows near a common support level with no sustained progression in either direction. The state is considered resolved when price breaks decisively out of the band.
    Failure modes: Range boundaries are approximate, so price frequently pokes through them intraday before returning, whipsawing traders who treat every touch of the edge as meaningful.
    Misconceptions: That sideways markets are low-risk because they are quiet; ranges often precede sharp directional moves, and volatility can expand abruptly.
    Example: A stock bounces between $70 support and $75 resistance for several weeks without closing outside the band.
    Aliases: ranging market, horizontal trend, flat market

    Trading range #

    A relatively tight horizontal price band, bounded by support below and resistance above, within which prices move while supply and demand are roughly balanced. In classical chart analysis a break of either boundary is taken as evidence that one side has gained control — a heuristic, not a law.
    Intuition: A tug-of-war zone: neither bulls nor bears can push price out of the box, until suddenly one side wins.
    Mechanics: Marked by drawing horizontal lines or zones at the range's reaction highs (resistance) and reaction lows (support); ranges can persist for weeks to months. Resolution is read from a decisive break of a boundary, ideally confirmed by expanding volume.
    Failure modes: False breakouts at range boundaries are common; in wide or long-lived ranges the 'exact' levels are fuzzy enough that zone-based marking is more honest than single-price lines.
    Misconceptions: That merely touching a range boundary is a tradable signal; without confirmation, boundary touches and marginal breaks fail frequently.
    Example: ChartSchool documents WorldCom oscillating between support at 55 and resistance at 63 for about five months in 1999, including a false breakout above 62, before the range broke down through 55.
    Aliases: congestion area, rectangle range

    Swing high #

    A local price peak — a high flanked by lower highs on either side — marking the point where an advance stalled and turned down. Swing highs (peaks) and swing lows (troughs) are the basic building blocks from which trend direction is read in classical technical analysis.
    Intuition: The top of one 'zig' before the market 'zags' back down.
    Mechanics: Discretionary analysts mark visible reaction peaks on the chart; rule-based definitions require a bar whose high exceeds the highs of a fixed number of bars on each side (e.g., two). Successive swing highs are compared — higher, lower, or equal — to classify trend structure, and old swing highs often serve as reference resistance.
    Failure modes: The lookback that defines a swing is arbitrary, so different analysts mark different swings; minor swing highs are constantly exceeded and re-marked, making the concept scale-dependent.
    Misconceptions: That a swing high is a precise resistance price; it is better treated as an approximate zone, since technical analysis is not an exact science.
    Example: A stock rallies to 55, declines to 52, and rallies again: 55 is a swing high that later acts as a reference resistance level.
    Aliases: reaction high, peak, pivot high

    Swing low #

    A local price trough — a low flanked by higher lows on either side — marking the point where a decline stalled and turned up. Swing lows (troughs), together with swing highs, form the price structure from which trend direction is read.
    Intuition: The bottom of one 'zag' before the market 'zigs' back up.
    Mechanics: Discretionary analysts mark visible reaction lows; rule-based definitions require a bar whose low is below the lows of a fixed number of bars on each side. Successive swing lows are compared to classify structure, and prior swing lows often serve as reference support or as stop-placement references.
    Failure modes: As with swing highs, the defining lookback is arbitrary and the label is scale-dependent; marginal breaks of a swing low can be noise rather than structure change.
    Misconceptions: That a swing low must hold as support; prior lows are reference zones, not floors, and are routinely undercut before price reverses.
    Example: A stock drops to 48, rebounds to 52, then dips to 50: 48 is a swing low anchoring the emerging structure.
    Aliases: reaction low, trough, pivot low

    Higher high #

    A swing high that exceeds the previous swing high. A sequence of higher highs combined with higher lows is the classical structural definition of an uptrend.
    Intuition: Each upward push reaches new territory, showing buyers keep gaining ground.
    Mechanics: Identified by comparing successive reaction peaks: when the latest peak trades or closes above the prior peak, a higher high is printed. Analysts track the sequence of highs and lows, rather than any single point, to judge whether trend structure remains intact.
    Failure modes: A single higher high can be an exhaustion spike; without confirming higher lows the apparent bullish structure can invert immediately.
    Misconceptions: That printing one higher high confirms an uptrend; structure is a property of a sequence, and one peak in isolation means little.
    Example: After a pullback, a stock rallies past its old 55 peak to 58, printing a higher high that keeps the uptrend structure intact.
    Aliases: HH

    Higher low #

    A swing low that forms above the previous swing low. Together with higher highs, a sequence of higher lows constitutes the structural definition of an uptrend and shows declining selling pressure on pullbacks.
    Intuition: Each dip finds buyers sooner than the last — the floor keeps rising.
    Mechanics: Identified by comparing successive reaction troughs: when the latest pullback low holds above the prior low, a higher low is printed. A break below the most recent higher low is conventionally treated as a warning that the uptrend structure may be changing.
    Failure modes: Higher lows fail routinely as support on retest; a marginal undercut of the prior low can be stop-driven noise rather than a genuine structure break.
    Misconceptions: That a higher low is a safe, low-risk entry point by itself; it is a structural observation, not a signal with defined risk.
    Example: A stock pulls back to 54 after previously bottoming at 52, then rallies: the higher low at 54 preserves the uptrend structure.
    Aliases: HL

    Lower high #

    A swing high that forms below the previous swing high. Together with lower lows, a sequence of lower highs constitutes the structural definition of a downtrend and shows weakening buying pressure on rallies.
    Intuition: Each bounce runs out of energy sooner than the last — the ceiling keeps sinking.
    Mechanics: Identified by comparing successive reaction peaks: when the latest rally high stalls below the prior peak, a lower high is printed. A break above the most recent lower high is conventionally treated as a warning that the downtrend structure may be changing.
    Failure modes: Lower highs are frequently exceeded in bear-market rallies, which can retrace large portions of a decline before the downtrend resumes.
    Misconceptions: That a lower high guarantees further declines; it is one observation in a sequence and says nothing certain about the next move.
    Example: A stock in a downtrend bounces to 95 after previously peaking at 100, then rolls over: the lower high at 95 confirms sellers remain in control.
    Aliases: LH

    Lower low #

    A swing low that breaks below the previous swing low. Together with lower highs, a sequence of lower lows constitutes the structural definition of a downtrend.
    Intuition: Each drop undercuts the last floor, showing sellers keep gaining ground.
    Mechanics: Identified by comparing successive reaction troughs: when the latest decline trades or closes below the prior trough, a lower low is printed. In an uptrend, a newly printed lower low is conventionally read as the first structural warning of a possible trend change.
    Failure modes: Lower lows are often produced by stop-driven flushes that immediately recover; a marginal undercut does not by itself confirm a downtrend.
    Misconceptions: That a lower low in an uptrend proves the trend has reversed; it is a warning sign within a sequence, not a verdict.
    Example: A stock falls to 88 after previously bottoming at 90, printing a lower low that extends the downtrend's structure.

    Support #

    A price level or zone at which demand is thought to be strong enough to prevent the price from declining further. It is a core hypothesis of technical analysis about where buying interest concentrates, not a guaranteed floor.
    Intuition: As price gets cheaper, buyers become more willing to buy and sellers less willing to sell, so declines tend to pause there.
    Mechanics: Commonly established at previous reaction lows, broken resistance levels (polarity), round numbers, or moving averages. Because TA is not an exact science, practitioners often mark support as a zone rather than an exact price; a decisive break below is read as supply overcoming demand.
    Failure modes: Support breaks happen regularly, especially in high volatility or around news; once broken, the old level can invert into resistance, trapping buyers who assumed it would hold.
    Misconceptions: That support is a precise price that must hold; brief dips below a level are common and do not necessarily invalidate it.
    Example: ChartSchool cites Halliburton returning repeatedly to support in the low 30s (October 1999 low near 31, December near 33, February near 32.5) before the level was tested again.
    Aliases: support level, support zone

    Resistance #

    A price level or zone at which selling is thought to be strong enough to prevent the price from rising further. It is a core hypothesis of technical analysis about where supply concentrates, not a guaranteed ceiling.
    Intuition: As price gets more expensive, sellers become more willing to sell and buyers less willing to buy, so rallies tend to stall there.
    Mechanics: Commonly established at previous reaction highs or at broken support levels (polarity principle: broken support can turn into resistance and vice versa, often because trapped holders sell at breakeven on the return). A decisive break above is read as demand overcoming supply.
    Failure modes: Resistance breaks happen regularly; price can also briefly exceed the level intraday and fall back, so marginal penetrations are unreliable as signals.
    Misconceptions: That resistance must repel price on the first approach; levels are zones of probable supply, and repeated tests often weaken them.
    Example: ChartSchool documents Halliburton's broken support at 42.5 turning into resistance: the stock rallied back to 42.5 three times and failed each time.
    Aliases: resistance level, resistance zone

    Breakout #

    A price move above an established resistance level, range boundary, or chart-pattern upper boundary. In classical chart analysis a breakout is interpreted as evidence that demand has overcome supply at that level; it is an analytical reading, and a meaningful fraction of breakouts fail.
    Intuition: Price finally escapes the ceiling that had been capping it, suggesting buyers are now willing to pay more than before.
    Mechanics: Identified when price closes (or trades, depending on convention) above the watched level; confirmation heuristics include expanding volume, follow-through in subsequent sessions, and a successful retest of the broken level as new support.
    Failure modes: False breakouts are frequent, especially on low volume; price pokes above the level, draws in breakout buyers, then closes back inside the old range.
    Misconceptions: That every break of resistance is the start of a new trend; without confirmation, single-bar breaks are routinely reversed.
    Example: ChartSchool documents WorldCom briefly poking above its 62 range resistance in mid-June 1999 — a breakout that failed within days and was nullified by a gap down.
    Aliases: upside breakout

    Breakdown #

    A price move below an established support level, range boundary, or chart-pattern lower boundary. In classical chart analysis a breakdown is interpreted as evidence that supply has overcome demand at that level; an analytical reading, not a certainty of further declines.
    Intuition: The floor that had been holding price up gives way, showing sellers are now willing to accept lower prices.
    Mechanics: Identified when price closes (or trades, by convention) below the watched support; a broken support level can subsequently act as resistance on rebounds (the polarity principle), and volume expansion is treated as confirmation.
    Failure modes: Bear-trap breakdowns occur when price dips below support, triggers stops, and then reclaims the level; breakdowns on thin volume are especially unreliable.
    Misconceptions: That a support break must be followed by immediate collapse; markets frequently retest the broken level from below, offering a second exit before any sustained decline.
    Example: ChartSchool documents Lucent breaking support at 60 in late 1999 and falling so fast that exit above 44 was impossible — an illustration that breakdowns can gap through stops.
    Aliases: downside break, support break

    False breakout #

    A breakout (or breakdown) that fails to hold: price moves beyond a watched level but cannot sustain itself there and closes back inside the prior range. It is the classical-TA label for a failed break; Smart Money Concepts frames the same event as a liquidity sweep.
    Intuition: Price teases a move out of the box, luring in breakout traders, then snaps back inside — trapping them on the wrong side.
    Mechanics: Identified only in retrospect: price trades beyond the level, then fails the follow-through test by closing back within the old range. Traders reduce exposure to false breaks by requiring closes beyond the level, volume confirmation, or a successful retest before acting.
    Failure modes: The concept is inherently after-the-fact: at the moment of the break, a genuine breakout and a false breakout look identical, so filters that screen out false breaks also delay entry on real ones.
    Misconceptions: That false breakouts are always deliberate manipulation; the footprint is real, but intent cannot be proven from a chart.
    Example: ChartSchool's WorldCom example: price briefly poked above 62 resistance in mid-June 1999, then gapped back down days later, nullifying the breakout.
    Aliases: failed breakout, bull trap, bear trap, fakeout

    Retest #

    The return of price to a level it has just broken, testing whether the old barrier now acts as the opposite kind — broken resistance as new support, or broken support as new resistance (the polarity principle). A successful retest is treated as confirmation of the break; a failed one warns of a false breakout.
    Intuition: After escaping the box, price comes back to check whether the old wall now works as a floor (or ceiling) from the other side.
    Mechanics: Observed when, following a break, price returns to the broken level and either respects it (holds beyond it, then resumes in the break direction) or fails (re-enters the old range). Many traders treat the retest, rather than the break itself, as the tradeable moment because it defines risk more tightly.
    Failure modes: Retests do not always occur — strong moves may never look back; and a retest that holds can still fail later, since the polarity principle is a heuristic.
    Misconceptions: That a retest must touch the exact broken price; retests are zone affairs and may fall short or marginally overshoot the level.
    Example: ChartSchool documents the NASDAQ 100 breaking above 935 resistance in May 1997, then falling back to test 935 as support twice before advancing to 1150.
    Aliases: throwback, pullback to broken level

    Pullback #

    A temporary counter-move against the prevailing trend — typically shallow and short-lived — after which the prior trend resumes. It is an interpretive label: whether a counter-move is a pullback or the start of a reversal is knowable only in hindsight.
    Intuition: The trend takes a breather: some traders take profits, price slips a little, then the main move continues.
    Mechanics: Identified as a counter-trend decline in an uptrend (or rally in a downtrend) that stalls at logical areas such as prior support/resistance, moving averages, or Fibonacci retracement zones (often 38.2%-61.8%), and is followed by resumption of the trend. Volume commonly contracts during the pullback and expands on resumption.
    Failure modes: Any pullback can deepen into a correction or full reversal; entering on the assumption of resumption fails precisely when the trend is ending.
    Misconceptions: That pullbacks are safe entries because 'the trend is intact'; trend integrity is only confirmed after the pullback resolves.
    Example: In an uptrend from 50 to 58, price dips back to 54 (near the 50% retracement and prior breakout level), forms a bullish candle, and then rallies to new highs.
    Aliases: dip

    Rally #

    A period of sustained price increases over days to months. Rallies occur within both bull and bear markets; a rise within a dominant downtrend is a bear market rally — a counter-trend advance, not necessarily the start of a recovery.
    Intuition: A stretch where buyers dominate and prices climb, whether as the main trend or as a pause inside a larger decline.
    Mechanics: Identified simply as a sustained sequence of rising prices; context is what classifies it — a rally that keeps printing higher highs and higher lows within an uptrend is trend continuation, while a sharp multi-week rise under a falling primary trend is read as a potential bear market rally driven by short covering and oversold conditions.
    Failure modes: Bear market rallies routinely suck in buyers and then fail at resistance, resuming the decline; only hindsight distinguishes them from new bull legs.
    Misconceptions: That a strong multi-week rally proves the bottom is in; 2022 produced several large bear market rallies that each resolved to new lows.
    Example: During the 2022 bear market, the S&P 500 staged several multi-week rallies amid Fed rate hikes; each faded and the downtrend resumed.
    Aliases: advance, bear market rally (contextual)

    Correction #

    A counter-trend decline within a prevailing uptrend, conventionally defined at the index level as a fall of 10% or more from the recent peak (but less than the 20% associated with a bear market). It is a descriptive, magnitude-based label applied to price action, not a forecast of what follows.
    Intuition: The market takes a meaningful step back inside a larger climb — deeper than a pullback, but short of a bear market.
    Mechanics: Measured as the percentage decline from the recent high: a drop of roughly 10% earns the label 'correction' by market convention; corrections are typically shorter in duration than bear markets and, in an intact bull trend, are followed by recovery to new highs.
    Failure modes: The 10% threshold is arbitrary, and a correction can always deepen into a bear market; buying 'the correction' fails when the decline keeps going.
    Misconceptions: That a correction 'must' end near 10%; the label is retrospective, and nothing bounds the eventual depth at the time it is occurring.
    Example: The 2023-2024 bull market saw several ~10% declines (banking stress, AI concerns) that each recovered to new highs — textbook corrections within an uptrend.
    Correction depth (%) = (peak price - trough price) / peak price x 100; >= 10% is the conventional correction threshold, >= 20% the conventional bear market threshold.
    Aliases: market correction

    Retracement #

    A temporary reversal within a larger trend that gives back only part of the prior move before the trend resumes. The term is magnitude-focused — how much of the prior leg was retraced — and is usually measured in percentages (e.g., Fibonacci levels); it describes structure, not a prediction of resumption.
    Intuition: The market gives back a slice of what it just gained, then carries on — the question is always how big the slice will be.
    Mechanics: Measured as a percentage of the prior swing: retracement level = pivot high - (retracement % x (pivot high - pivot low)) for a pullback in an up-leg. Shallow retracements (~38.2%) are read as trend strength, deep ones (~61.8%-78.6%) as potential weakness or reversal risk.
    Failure modes: There is no level a retracement must respect; a 'retracement' that exceeds 100% of the prior leg was in fact a reversal, which is only clear afterward.
    Misconceptions: That price is likely to stop at a particular retracement percentage; the percentages are observation conventions, and prices break through them regularly.
    Example: After a rally from 5809 to 6162 in S&P 500 futures, a decline to 5944 is a 61.8% retracement of the up-leg (6162.25 - 0.618 x 353.25 = 5944).
    Retracement level = High - r x (High - Low) for an up-leg, where r is the retracement fraction (e.g., 0.382, 0.5, 0.618); symmetrically Low + r x (High - Low) for a down-leg.

    Reversal #

    A durable change in the direction of the prevailing trend — an uptrend turning into a downtrend or vice versa — as opposed to a temporary counter-move. In chart analysis, reversals are confirmed through structure (broken swing sequences, broken trendlines, completed reversal patterns), and only after the fact with certainty.
    Intuition: The tide actually turns: what was a staircase up becomes a staircase down.
    Mechanics: Read from structure shifts: in an uptrend, a lower high followed by a lower low (breaking the last higher low) signals a possible reversal; trendline breaks, support/resistance breaks, and classical reversal patterns (double tops/bottoms, head and shoulders) provide corroboration. Depth and duration distinguish reversal from pullback or consolidation.
    Failure modes: Early reversal signals frequently turn out to be deep pullbacks; acting on the first structural break exposes the trader to trend resumption.
    Misconceptions: That reversals can be called at the turn; every real-time 'reversal call' is a probabilistic bet, and counter-trend calling is a classic account destroyer.
    Example: An uptrend printing 55, 58 highs and 52, 54 lows breaks down through 54, then rallies only to 56 (lower high) and falls under 52: the reversed sequence confirms a trend change.

    Continuation #

    The resumption of the prevailing trend after a pause, pullback, or consolidation. In classical chart analysis, continuation is the default expectation associated with congestion patterns and mid-trend gaps — a probabilistic heuristic, not a law.
    Intuition: The trend catches its breath and then keeps going in the same direction.
    Mechanics: Identified when a counter-move or sideways congestion resolves with a break in the direction of the prior trend — e.g., a consolidation range breaking with the trend, or a runaway (continuation) gap appearing mid-trend on rising volume.
    Failure modes: Not every pause continues the trend — some consolidations are distribution or accumulation that precede reversals, so continuation assumptions fail at trend turning points.
    Misconceptions: That a pause in a trend guarantees continuation; continuation is the base-rate expectation, but each individual congestion can resolve either way.
    Example: A stock trending up pauses in a two-week sideways range, then gaps higher on increased volume — a continuation resolution of the congestion.

    Consolidation #

    A period during which price moves sideways within a relatively contained range after a prior move, reflecting a temporary balance between buyers and sellers. Consolidations are read as pauses that can resolve into either continuation or reversal; the label describes the pause, not its outcome.
    Intuition: After running, the market rests — price churns in a band while both sides regroup.
    Mechanics: Identified as a stretch of overlapping bars bounded by approximate horizontal support and resistance, often with contracting volume and range. Resolution is read from the direction of the eventual range break, ideally confirmed by volume expansion.
    Failure modes: Consolidations produce repeated false breaks at their edges before resolving; the longer the range, the fuzzier its exact boundaries.
    Misconceptions: That consolidation means the trend will continue ('a pause that refreshes'); it can equally mark distribution before a reversal.
    Example: After rallying from 27 to 64, WorldCom consolidated between 55 and 63 for about five months in 1999 before breaking down through 55 (ChartSchool case study).

    Compression #

    A progressive narrowing of price swings and trading ranges, reflecting falling volatility as buyers and sellers reach a temporary equilibrium. In volatility-based frameworks (e.g., Bollinger Bands), compression is associated with the 'squeeze' state that often — but not reliably — precedes a directional expansion.
    Intuition: Price action coils like a spring: swings get smaller and smaller until the stored energy releases in a sharp move.
    Mechanics: Observed as successively smaller bar ranges and converging swing highs/lows; quantified with volatility measures such as narrowing Bollinger Bands (which contract as the standard deviation of price falls) or falling ATR/BandWidth readings.
    Failure modes: Compression says nothing about direction, and squeezes can persist far longer than expected or resolve in a fakeout before the true move.
    Misconceptions: That compression must resolve in a large move; low-volatility regimes can persist indefinitely, and the eventual break can be small.
    Example: A stock's daily ranges shrink over three weeks while its Bollinger Bands narrow to their tightest width in months, preceding a sharp breakout.
    Aliases: volatility squeeze, range contraction
