    Weekly review reviewed #

    A weekly aggregation and analysis of the trading journal - process metrics, outcome statistics by setup, recurring errors, and psychological patterns - used to adjust the playbook and plan on a sample less noisy than daily results. It produces a small number of concrete, testable adjustments rather than wholesale rewrites.
    Intuition: Days are noise; the week is the first window where your actual habits become visible as data.
    Mechanics: Compiles the week's trades: adherence rate, expectancy and R-distribution per setup, error counts by type, best and worst conditions; conclusions become at most a few documented changes (drop a setup, cap a time window, adjust a review step), which are then tracked for effect in subsequent weeks.
    Failure modes: A week is still a small sample - structural strategy changes on five days of data are overfitting to noise. Reviews without structured journal data become mood summaries rather than analysis.
    Misconceptions: Weekly review is not a performance verdict; it is hypothesis generation for the plan. Nor is more analysis always better - the output should be a short list of testable adjustments.
    Example: Friday's review shows all of the week's losses came from opening-range trades taken before 9:45; the plan is amended to exclude that window and the change is re-evaluated the following week.

    Playbook reviewed #

    A trader's curated, documented collection of proven setups - each with context conditions, entry, exit, sizing, and a worked example - built from personal trading results and reviewed regularly. Popularized by Mike Bellafiore (One Good Trade; The PlayBook), it turns scattered experience into an explicit library of tradable patterns.
    Intuition: Instead of trading everything the market shows you, you trade only the handful of patterns you have written down, tested, and own.
    Mechanics: Each playbook entry documents one setup: market context, trigger, invalidation, targets, sizing rule, and annotated screenshots from real trades; entries are added from review of one's own best trades and retired when tracked expectancy decays. Setup-quality grading (A/B/C) is applied against playbook definitions.
    Failure modes: Playbooks ossify: setups fitted to one market regime keep being traded after the regime ends, so each entry needs ongoing expectancy tracking. Copying another trader's playbook without one's own screen time and data usually fails.
    Misconceptions: A playbook is not a list of chart patterns from a book; it is a personal, evidence-backed subset of what that trader executes best. More setups is not better - depth in a few beats breadth.
    Example: A trader's playbook holds four setups; when his quarterly review shows one has been negative for 60 trades, it is retired and replaced by a pattern his journal shows emerging.

    Trading routine reviewed #

    The fixed daily and weekly structure around trading - pre-market preparation, defined session behaviors, post-market review - that standardizes decision quality and conserves willpower. Routines externalize discipline into schedule and environment instead of relying on in-the-moment self-control.
    Intuition: You cannot will yourself into consistency every day; you build a routine so the consistent behavior happens by default.
    Mechanics: Typical elements: pre-market plan (levels, catalysts, scenarios), warm-up and state check, session rules (trading windows, breaks, loss limits), a shutdown ritual, daily review, and sleep and exercise management. Trading-psychology work such as Steenbarger's emphasizes structured practice and self-coaching routines as the mechanism of trader development.
    Failure modes: Routines decay under travel, stress, or overconfidence after good stretches, usually starting with skipped preparation. An overly rigid routine also breaks when market conditions shift the session structure, as on event days.
    Misconceptions: A routine is not superstition or comfort ritual; its components exist to control preparation quality, arousal, and exposure. Watching screens longer is not a routine - it is often the absence of one.
    Example: A trader's day: 7:00 review of overnight markets and scenarios, 9:20 checklist and state check, trade only 9:30-11:00, hard daily stop at -2R, journal and review by 16:30 - the same sequence every session.

    Mental stop reviewed #

    A stop-loss level held in the trader's mind rather than placed as an order with the broker: the trader commits to exit manually if price reaches the level. It preserves flexibility and avoids order visibility but depends entirely on real-time discipline under stress.
    Intuition: A mental stop is a promise to yourself; the market will test exactly your willingness to keep it.
    Mechanics: The invalidation level is defined at entry but no resting order is working; when price trades there, the trader must execute the exit manually, which introduces hesitation risk, delay slippage, and the temptation to renegotiate. It is the discretionary counterpart of the hard stop, which is an actual stop or stop-limit order resting at the venue.
    Failure modes: It fails precisely when needed most: fast markets, news spikes, and tilt states, where the trader freezes, moves the level, or averages down instead. Without a working order, gaps and connectivity failures leave the position unprotected.
    Misconceptions: Mental stops are not 'more professional'; they are appropriate mainly for experienced traders in liquid markets who watch the position continuously. Honoring a mental stop nine times out of ten still leaves the tenth - often the catastrophic one.
    Example: Long from 50 with a mental stop at 48.50; a surprise headline dumps the stock through the level in seconds and the trader, hesitating, exits at 47.90 - triple the planned slippage.
    Aliases: Soft stop

    Hard stop reviewed #

    A stop-loss instruction resting with the broker or exchange as an actual order (stop or stop-limit), which triggers automatically when price reaches the stop level. It enforces the invalidation point mechanically, removing real-time discretion - at the cost of possible slippage or non-fill in fast markets.
    Intuition: A hard stop is the exit decision executed by the machine, so the future, emotional version of you does not get a vote.
    Mechanics: A sell-stop sits below the market (a buy-stop above it, for shorts); when the stop price trades, a stop order becomes a market order - execution guaranteed, price not - while a stop-limit becomes a limit order - price capped, execution not. Trailing-stop variants ratchet the stop with favorable price movement.
    Failure modes: In gaps and fast markets a stop-market fills far through the stop price, and a stop-limit may not fill at all; obvious stop levels can be run by short-term liquidity hunts before the move resumes. A hard stop does not cap gap risk.
    Misconceptions: A stop-loss does not guarantee the exit price - 'stop' is a trigger, not a fill price. Nor are hard stops only for the undisciplined; they are structural risk control that survives distraction, outages, and emotional episodes.
    Example: Long 250 shares at 50 with a sell-stop at 48 (planned -$500 risk); an overnight gap opens at 47.20 and the stop market-fills at 47.25 - more than planned, but bounded and automatic.
    Aliases: Stop order, Stop-loss order
