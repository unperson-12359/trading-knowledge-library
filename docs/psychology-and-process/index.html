    Trading psychology #

    The study of how emotions, cognitive biases, and mental processes affect trading decisions and performance, and the applied discipline of managing those factors. It is a core topic of behavioral finance, which documents systematic, predictable departures from the rational-agent model of traditional finance.
    Intuition: Two traders with the same strategy get different results because fear, greed, and bias change what they actually do. Trading psychology is about understanding and managing that human layer.
    Mechanics: Draws on behavioral finance and decision science to catalog biases (loss aversion, overconfidence, herding) and emotional states (fear, euphoria, tilt) that distort trade selection, sizing, and exit timing; practitioners counter them with plans, checklists, journals, and review routines.
    Failure modes: Awareness of psychological pitfalls does not by itself eliminate them; under real-time stress, knowledge of biases often fails to change behavior. It also cannot compensate for a strategy with no genuine edge.
    Misconceptions: The belief that mastering psychology alone makes trading profitable; psychology governs execution quality, not whether the underlying method has positive expectancy. Another misconception is that emotions should be eliminated rather than recognized and managed.
    Example: A trader who knows her edge but repeatedly exits winners early out of fear studies her journal, finds the pattern, and adopts pre-committed exit rules to close the gap between plan and action.
    Aliases: Investor psychology, Behavioral trading
    Related:

    Loss aversion #

    The empirically documented tendency for losses to be psychologically weighted more heavily than equivalent gains relative to a reference point; in Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory the value function is steeper for losses than for gains. It is a component of prospect theory and is distinct from rational risk aversion.
    Intuition: Losing $100 hurts roughly twice as much as winning $100 feels good, so people go to disproportionate lengths to avoid realizing losses.
    Mechanics: Prospect theory's value function v(x) is concave for gains, convex for losses, and kinked at the reference point, with the loss limb about twice as steep as the gain limb (loss-aversion coefficient lambda near 2 in many estimates), producing risk aversion over gains and risk seeking over losses.
    Failure modes: Its magnitude and even existence at small stakes are debated in the replication literature, and 'loss attention' accounts argue losses attract attention without extra subjective weight. The reference point shifts with framing and expectations, so predictions can move.
    Misconceptions: Loss aversion is not the same as risk aversion: it is a gain/loss asymmetry around a reference point, not a general preference for certainty. It is also not uniform across stake sizes, individuals, or domains.
    Example: An investor refuses to sell a stock down 20% because realizing the loss feels far worse than the equivalent gain felt good, yet sells a stock up 20% quickly to lock in the win.
    v(x) = x^alpha for x >= 0; v(x) = -lambda * (-x)^beta for x < 0, with 0 < alpha, beta < 1 and lambda > 1 (empirically lambda is often estimated near 2); x is the gain or loss relative to a reference point.
    Aliases: Loss/gain asymmetry
    Related:

    Prospect theory #

    A descriptive model of decision making under risk developed by Kahneman and Tversky (1979) as a critique of expected utility theory, in which people evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point and weight probabilities by a subjective decision-weighting function. It predicts risk aversion over gains, risk seeking over losses (the reflection effect), and overweighting of small probabilities.
    Intuition: People do not think in terms of final wealth; they think in wins and losses from where they stand now, and they treat 'almost certain' very differently from 'certain'.
    Mechanics: Choice proceeds in an editing phase (coding outcomes as gains/losses, combining and cancelling components) and an evaluation phase that computes V as the sum of decision weights pi(p_i) times values v(x_i), where v is S-shaped and steeper for losses, and pi overweights small probabilities and underweights moderate-to-large ones.
    Failure modes: It is a descriptive, not normative, model, and its parameters vary across individuals and contexts; the original formulation allowed violations of stochastic dominance, fixed in cumulative prospect theory (1992). It is largely silent on how reference points are selected in a given situation.
    Misconceptions: Prospect theory does not claim people are simply irrational or always risk-averse; it predicts risk seeking in the loss domain. It is also a theory of individual choice, not by itself a theory of market prices.
    Example: Offered a sure $3,000 or an 80% chance of $4,000, most people take the sure gain; offered a sure loss of $3,000 or an 80% chance of losing $4,000, most take the gamble - the mirror-image risk attitudes documented in the 1979 paper.
    V = sum_i pi(p_i) * v(x_i); v(x) = x^alpha if x >= 0, v(x) = -lambda * (-x)^beta if x < 0; x_i = outcome relative to reference point, pi = decision-weight function, alpha, beta in (0,1), lambda > 1.
    Aliases: Cumulative prospect theory (1992 extension)

    Disposition effect #

    The empirically documented tendency of investors to sell winning positions too early and hold losing positions too long, named by Shefrin and Statman (1985) and confirmed in brokerage-account data by Odean (1998). It is a descriptive behavioral pattern, explained in part by prospect theory, mental accounting, and regret avoidance.
    Intuition: Traders cash in winners quickly to enjoy the win, but let losers run because selling would mean admitting a mistake.
    Mechanics: Odean measured it as the gap between the proportion of gains realized (PGR) and the proportion of losses realized (PLR) in account data; PGR exceeded PLR in all months except December, when tax-loss selling reverses it. Prospect theory supplies the mechanism: risk seeking in the loss domain (ride losers) and risk aversion in the gain domain (sell winners).
    Failure modes: The effect weakens or reverses with tax-loss selling, in panics (high volatility), and among some professional groups; belief-driven trading can mimic or mask the preference-based effect. It is a population tendency, not a rule about any individual position.
    Misconceptions: It is not a claim that holding losers is always wrong - rebalancing, mean reversion, or tax logic can justify it - nor that prospect theory alone explains it; belief revisions also contribute. It also does not imply winners should never be sold.
    Example: In Odean's sample of 10,000 discount-brokerage accounts, a stock trading above its purchase price was about 1.5 times as likely to be sold as one trading below it, and the winners sold went on to outperform the losers kept.
    PGR = Realized Gains / (Realized Gains + Paper Gains); PLR = Realized Losses / (Realized Losses + Paper Losses); disposition effect present when PGR - PLR > 0 (Odean 1998).

    Overconfidence bias #

    A cognitive bias in which subjective confidence in one's judgments reliably exceeds their objective accuracy, appearing as overestimation of one's own performance, overplacement relative to others, or overprecision of beliefs. In markets it is linked empirically to excessive trading volume and to the underperformance of the most active individual traders.
    Intuition: Traders systematically think their forecasts are better than they are - when they say 90% sure, they are right far less than 90% of the time.
    Mechanics: Measured by calibration studies (confidence versus hit rate), better-than-average surveys, and confidence-interval tasks in which 90% intervals capture the truth only about half the time. Barber and Odean linked overconfidence to trading frequency and showed that the most active individual investors earned the lowest net returns.
    Failure modes: Overplacement reverses on hard tasks (people then rate themselves below average), and some better-than-average findings reflect event base rates rather than bias. Experts in high-validity environments with rapid feedback can be well calibrated, so the bias is context-dependent.
    Misconceptions: Overconfidence is not simply optimism or high self-esteem; it is a calibration error between confidence and accuracy. Nor is more information a cure - in Oskamp's study confidence rose with information while accuracy stayed flat.
    Example: Barber and Odean (2000, summarized in the SEC-commissioned LOC report) found that the most actively trading individual investors underperformed the market, consistent with overconfidence-driven overtrading.
    Aliases: Overconfidence effect, Miscalibration

    Confirmation bias #

    The tendency to search for, interpret, favor, and recall information in ways that confirm one's preexisting beliefs or hypotheses while discounting disconfirming evidence. Documented experimentally since Wason's 1960s work, it operates through biased search, biased interpretation, and biased recall, and is strongest for emotionally charged or deeply held beliefs.
    Intuition: Once a trader decides the market is going up, every headline starts looking bullish and bearish evidence gets explained away.
    Mechanics: People prefer 'positive tests' consistent with their working hypothesis, apply stricter evidentiary standards to disconfirming data (disconfirmation bias), and remember congruent evidence more easily; in finance it sustains losing theses and inflates confidence beyond what the evidence supports.
    Failure modes: The classic falsification reading has been qualified: a positive-test strategy can be informationally reasonable in some environments (Klayman and Ha), so not every confirmatory search is an error. Belief perseverance can persist even after the original evidence is discredited, corrupting post-trade review.
    Misconceptions: It is not deliberate dishonesty; the search and interpretation biases are largely automatic. Nor does intelligence protect against it - myside bias appears at all cognitive levels.
    Example: A long-biased trader reads only bullish analyst notes, treats a revenue miss as already priced in, and recalls only the times the stock rallied on bad news - deepening a losing position.
    Aliases: Confirmatory bias, Myside bias

    Recency bias #

    A cognitive bias that assigns disproportionate weight to recent events over older ones when forming judgments or forecasts, related to the recency effect in memory research. In markets it manifests as extrapolating the latest trend, returns, or volatility into the future at the expense of long-run base rates.
    Intuition: After three green months it feels like the market only goes up; after a crash it feels like it will never recover.
    Mechanics: Recent observations are more available in memory and receive excessive weight, so expectations, allocations, and risk perception track the recent past rather than the full sample; investor-behavior commentary documents its role in chasing recent performance and abandoning assets after recent declines.
    Failure modes: As an implicit forecasting rule it fails worst at regime changes, which cluster exactly where recency-weighted expectations are most extreme. It should not be confused with the distinct primacy effect, nor with rational updating when trends are genuinely persistent.
    Misconceptions: Weighting recent data more heavily is not always irrational - in non-stationary markets some recency weighting is optimal; the bias is overweighting beyond what the evidence supports. It is also not the same as price momentum, an empirical return pattern rather than a judgment error.
    Example: An investor who held a stock through a long rally assumes the uptrend will continue and adds near the top, then refuses to buy during a recession because recent price action feels predictive.
    Aliases: Recency effect (memory analogue)

    Anchoring bias #

    The tendency for judgments and estimates to be biased toward an initially encountered reference value (the anchor), even when it is arbitrary or irrelevant, introduced as a heuristic by Tversky and Kahneman (1974). Adjustments away from the anchor are typically insufficient, so final estimates cluster near it.
    Intuition: The first number you hear - a purchase price, a 52-week high, an analyst target - quietly drags every later estimate toward itself.
    Mechanics: In the classic experiment, a rigged wheel stopping at 10 versus 65 moved estimates of African UN membership from 25% to 45%. Explained by anchoring-and-insufficient-adjustment and by selective accessibility of anchor-consistent information; in markets, purchase prices, round numbers, and historical highs and lows act as anchors.
    Failure modes: Extremely transparent or implausible anchors sometimes fail to replicate the effect, and self-generated anchors behave differently from provided ones. The bias is notably resistant to debiasing: explicit warnings and monetary incentives mostly fail to remove it.
    Misconceptions: Anchors need not be plausible or consciously accepted to work, and experts (for example real-estate agents given listing prices) are affected while denying it. Using a genuinely informative reference price is not anchoring bias.
    Example: A trader anchors on the $150 price at which she bought a stock and reads $120 as 'cheap' relative to it, instead of evaluating the business at its current price.
    Aliases: Anchoring effect, Anchoring and adjustment

    Availability heuristic #

    A mental shortcut, identified by Tversky and Kahneman (1973), in which the judged frequency or probability of an event depends on how easily instances come to mind rather than on actual base rates. Ease of recall - driven by recency, vividness, emotion, and media exposure - substitutes for statistical reasoning.
    Intuition: If you can picture it quickly, you think it is common; after a week of crash headlines, a crash feels imminent.
    Mechanics: Frequency judgments are made from the number or experienced ease of retrieved examples, as in the classic 'words starting with K' experiment; Schwarz and colleagues showed the felt ease of retrieval itself carries the effect. In markets, vivid recent events such as crashes or hot IPOs are overweighted relative to their base rates.
    Failure modes: It misleads exactly where media coverage or personal experience is unrepresentative - vivid but rare risks are overweighted while common, dull ones are ignored. Later critiques note ambiguity in whether ease or content of recall drives the effect, complicating measurement.
    Misconceptions: Availability is not simple recency; vividness, emotion, and repetition also drive it. Nor is the heuristic always wrong - availability often correlates with true frequency, which is why it persists; the bias lies in its systematic distortions.
    Example: After weeks of coverage of a crypto collapse, a trader overestimates the probability of an imminent broad-market crash and buys expensive hedges, though long-run base rates are unchanged.
    Aliases: Availability bias

    Hindsight bias #

    The tendency, after an outcome is known, to perceive the event as having been more predictable than it actually was, first demonstrated experimentally by Fischhoff (1975). It distorts memory of prior beliefs, inflates confidence in one's forecasting ability, and degrades the quality of performance review.
    Intuition: After the market sells off, everyone saw it coming - but almost nobody acted on it beforehand.
    Mechanics: Outcome knowledge reconstructs memory: people recall prior probability estimates as closer to the realized outcome than they were (memory distortion), perceive the outcome as inevitable, and believe they personally foresaw it. Biais and Weber found hindsight-prone investors underestimate volatility and show worse investment performance; contemporaneous decision diaries are the standard countermeasure.
    Failure modes: It corrupts strategy evaluation: past trades look obviously right or wrong, so lessons are distorted and the process becomes unfalsifiable. Warning people about the bias barely reduces it; deliberately considering alternative outcomes helps only somewhat.
    Misconceptions: Hindsight bias is not lying about what you knew - the memory distortion is unconscious. It also differs from outcome bias, which judges decision quality by results rather than distorting remembered predictions.
    Example: A trader who assigned 30% odds to a rate hike recalls afterward that he was 'pretty sure' it was coming, and carries inflated confidence into the next meeting.
    Aliases: Knew-it-all-along phenomenon, Creeping determinism

    Outcome bias #

    The error of evaluating the quality of a decision by its realized outcome rather than by the information and reasoning available when the decision was made, demonstrated by Baron and Hershey (1988). Identical decisions are rated better when they happen to end well and worse when they end badly, even when outcomes are driven by chance.
    Intuition: A reckless trade that made money gets praised; a disciplined trade that lost gets criticized - same process, different luck.
    Mechanics: Evaluators weight the known result more heavily than ex-ante reasoning; experiments show the decisions of surgeons, coaches, and managers are judged worse after bad luck and better after good luck. In trading it rewards hidden risk-taking that happened to pay off and punishes sound process during normal drawdowns.
    Failure modes: It destroys learning: lucky gambles are reinforced and sound processes abandoned after ordinary variance. In performance evaluation it systematically encourages concealed tail risk, since only the realized branch of the distribution is judged.
    Misconceptions: A good outcome does not validate a decision, and a bad outcome does not indict it - only ex-ante expected value and process quality do. Outcome bias is also distinct from hindsight bias, which concerns remembered predictability rather than evaluation of decisions.
    Example: A desk praises a trader who doubled his account on an oversized punt and criticizes one who followed the risk plan into a losing streak; the punter blows up the following quarter.

    Gambler's fallacy #

    The mistaken belief that, for independent random events, a run of one outcome makes the opposite outcome more likely next - as if chance had a memory and streaks must even out. Tversky and Kahneman traced it to the representativeness heuristic and the erroneous 'law of small numbers'.
    Intuition: After five reds at roulette, black feels due - but the wheel has no memory and the odds are unchanged.
    Mechanics: People expect short sequences to mirror long-run proportions, so they predict reversals after streaks in genuinely independent processes (negative recency). In trading it appears as expecting a rally purely because of a string of down days; it is the mirror image of the hot-hand fallacy.
    Failure modes: It is misapplied where events are actually dependent (cards drawn without replacement) or where a streak legitimately signals a biased process; inferring a loaded coin from twenty heads is Bayesian, not fallacious. In markets, streaks can reflect real regimes, which blurs the line between the fallacy and inference.
    Misconceptions: Believing in long-run averages is not the fallacy; the fallacy is believing nature enforces them over the next few trials. Mean reversion in prices is a separate empirical claim, not a justification for the fallacy.
    Example: A trader shorts an index after six consecutive up days, reasoning it is overdue for a down day, even though daily returns are close to independent and the streak carries no predictive information.
    Aliases: Monte Carlo fallacy, Fallacy of the maturity of chances

    Hot-hand fallacy #

    The belief that a person or process enjoying a run of successes has an elevated probability of continued success - the 'hot hand'. Gilovich, Tversky and Vallone (1985) found basketball shooting streaks consistent with chance, but Miller and Sanjurjo later showed the original statistical test was itself biased, so the existence of genuine streaks remains contested.
    Intuition: After three winning trades in a row a trader feels in the zone and sizes up - as if success itself were contagious.
    Mechanics: It is positive recency: expecting a streak to continue. GTV found shot outcomes statistically independent; Miller and Sanjurjo (2018) showed that in finite sequences the measured hit rate after streaks is biased downward, so equal rates can actually indicate a real hot hand. In trading the fallacy is attributing a winning streak to skill or a hot state without statistical evidence.
    Failure modes: The fallacy label can itself be wrong: genuine streaks exist in some domains, and small samples make streak detection statistically fragile. Belief in a hot hand can also become self-reinforcing through confidence and increased risk-taking.
    Misconceptions: The 1985 study did not prove streaks never exist - it showed observed patterns were consistent with randomness, and its method was later shown to be biased against finding streaks. Winning streaks in trading are nonetheless mostly consistent with luck plus variance.
    Example: A day trader doubles his size after four straight winning days, believing he is hot; his win rate over the next hundred trades regresses to his long-run average.
    Aliases: Hot hand phenomenon

    Sunk-cost fallacy #

    The tendency to continue an endeavor because of previously invested, unrecoverable resources - money, time, or effort - even when prospective costs exceed prospective benefits. Rational choice requires ignoring sunk costs (the bygones principle); Arkes and Blumer (1985) documented the systematic violation.
    Intuition: 'I have already lost so much on this trade that I cannot sell now' - but the money is gone regardless of what you do next.
    Mechanics: Past investment is treated as justification for future commitment, driven by loss framing (selling realizes a loss), personal responsibility for the initial decision, and the desire not to appear wasteful. In trading it appears as adding to losers or holding positions past their invalidation point.
    Failure modes: Not all persistence is fallacious: abandoning a position too early can be its own error, and outsiders may lack the decision-maker's information. The label is warranted only when forward-looking expected value no longer justifies continued exposure.
    Misconceptions: Cutting a position because it is a sunk cost is rational only when the forward outlook no longer justifies holding; the fallacy is letting the past loss, rather than the future expectation, drive the decision. Sunk costs are also not the same as fixed costs.
    Example: A trader doubles down on a losing position to get back to breakeven, increasing exposure to a trade whose original thesis has already failed.
    Aliases: Sunk cost effect, Concorde fallacy

    Endowment effect #

    The finding that people value a good more highly once they own it: the minimum they will accept to give it up substantially exceeds the maximum they would pay to acquire it. Named by Thaler (1980) and demonstrated experimentally by Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler (1990), it is commonly explained by loss aversion around an ownership reference point.
    Intuition: The moment a position is in your account, it feels worth more than the identical position you do not own.
    Mechanics: Ownership shifts the reference point so that selling is coded as a loss; in the classic Cornell mug experiments, sellers demanded roughly twice what buyers offered. Competing explanations include psychological ownership, reference-price asymmetries, and experimental artifacts (Plott and Zeiler).
    Failure modes: The effect weakens for experienced traders of a good, for goods with close substitutes, and under some experimental designs, and its interpretation as pure loss aversion is disputed. It cannot justify holding a position against contrary evidence.
    Misconceptions: It is not mere sentimental attachment - it appears within minutes of randomly assigned ownership. Nor does it prove markets are irrational: induced-value markets in the same experiments cleared efficiently.
    Example: An investor refuses to sell a stock at $80 even though, if she did not already own it, she would not pay more than $60 for it today.
    Aliases: Divestiture aversion, WTA-WTP gap
    Related:

    • Disposition effect

    Status quo bias #

    A preference for maintaining the current state of affairs, such that an option is disproportionately favored when it is designated as the status quo or default. Documented by Samuelson and Zeckhauser (1988), it is linked to loss aversion, regret avoidance, and the endowment effect, and is distinct from a rational preference for the current state.
    Intuition: Whatever allocation or position you already have feels safer than any change, so portfolios drift and stale positions linger.
    Mechanics: In choice experiments, labeling an option as the status quo significantly raises the share choosing it, and the effect grows with the number of alternatives; real-world defaults (retirement-plan enrollment, the New Jersey versus Pennsylvania auto-insurance natural experiment) produced large participation differences. Switching away is coded as a risky loss relative to the reference state.
    Failure modes: It can be rational under transaction costs, information limits, or genuinely superior current states, so observed inertia is not automatically bias. It also interacts with omission bias, making the two hard to separate empirically.
    Misconceptions: Status quo bias is not laziness alone, and loss aversion does not fully explain it - the bias appears even without gain/loss framing. In trading, doing nothing is itself a decision with risk consequences.
    Example: A trader keeps a stale position and outdated stop levels for months because changing them feels like an active risk, while an identical fresh setup would never pass his entry rules.
    Aliases: Default bias
    Related:

    Herding #

    The tendency of individuals to suppress their own judgment and conform to the behavior of the group; in financial markets, the clustering of investors into the same trades, driven by conformity, information cascades, and reputational concerns. It is a documented behavioral pattern associated with bubbles, panics, and momentum, not a reliable signal in itself.
    Intuition: When everyone is buying, not buying feels wrong; the crowd feels like information even when it is just a crowd.
    Mechanics: Conformity experiments (Asch) show roughly a third of subjects following an obviously wrong majority; in markets, Keynes's 'better to fail conventionally' logic, information cascades, and social proof produce correlated buying and selling that amplifies trends and crashes. The SEC-commissioned Library of Congress report lists following the herd among the systematic mistakes of U.S. investors.
    Failure modes: Herding can be individually rational (career risk, genuine information in others' actions), so correlated trades are not automatically irrational. Contrarian strategies built against herding can lose for long stretches while the crowd is right.
    Misconceptions: Herding is not simply copying smart money - cascades form even when most participants privately doubt the consensus. Nor is every correlated market move herding; common reactions to public news are a different phenomenon.
    Example: During a meme-stock spike, retail traders buy because everyone else is buying and posting gains, pushing the price far beyond any fundamental anchor until the cascade reverses.
    Aliases: Herd mentality, Herd behavior

    Narrative bias #

    The tendency to impose coherent stories on sequences of events and to treat a plausible narrative as explanation or evidence, even when outcomes were driven substantially by chance. Taleb calls this the narrative fallacy: we compress complex, random reality into memorable stories and then overtrust them.
    Intuition: The market rallies and someone supplies a reason; the reason feels like knowledge, but the same story machine would have explained a sell-off just as smoothly.
    Mechanics: Stories reduce dimensionality and aid memory, so causally tidy accounts crowd out base rates, randomness, and alternative explanations; post-hoc narratives about past moves are then projected forward as if they had predictive force. In trading it corrupts both real-time judgment and post-trade review.
    Failure modes: It misleads most in noisy, low-signal domains such as short-term price moves, where a compelling story can be constructed for any outcome. It also makes review unfalsifiable, since every result can be retrofitted with a satisfying cause.
    Misconceptions: Not all narratives are false - the bias is mistaking a story's coherence for evidence of its truth and underestimating the role of luck. Narratives are indispensable for communication; the error is treating them as analysis.
    Example: A trader explains yesterday's rally as rotation into quality on rate expectations; when the market falls the next day, the same factors are cited to explain the drop.
    Aliases: Narrative fallacy, Story bias

    Self-attribution bias #

    The tendency to attribute successes to one's own skill and failures to external factors such as luck, market conditions, or other people. A self-serving attribution pattern studied since Miller and Ross (1975), it is listed among the behavioral patterns that distort investor decision-making in the SEC-commissioned Library of Congress review.
    Intuition: Winning trades prove you are skilled; losing trades prove the market was rigged - so the mental scoreboard always flatters you.
    Mechanics: Outcomes consistent with expectations are internalized (skill), inconsistent ones externalized (luck or manipulation), protecting self-esteem but corrupting the feedback loop needed for learning. In behavioral finance it feeds overconfidence: traders who credit wins to themselves update self-assessed skill too quickly and trade more.
    Failure modes: It blocks error correction: if losses are always someone else's fault, process flaws are never found. Miller and Ross cautioned that some apparently self-serving attributions reflect rational expectation-based inference rather than ego defense, complicating diagnosis.
    Misconceptions: It is not deliberate dishonesty - the attribution pattern is largely automatic. Nor is every internal attribution of success biased; sometimes the trader genuinely was skilled, which is why contemporaneous process records are needed to tell the difference.
    Example: After a profitable quarter a trader concludes his stock-picking is elite and doubles his size; after a losing quarter he blames manipulation and changes nothing.
    Aliases: Self-serving bias, Self-serving attribution bias

    Illusion of control #

    The tendency to overestimate one's ability to control or influence outcomes that are partly or wholly determined by chance, named by Ellen Langer (1975). It is strengthened by 'skill cues' such as personal choice, competition, and familiarity - all abundant in trading environments.
    Intuition: Clicking the buy button yourself, watching the screen, and having a system all feel like control over an outcome that is mostly random.
    Mechanics: In Langer's lottery and chance-task experiments, personal choice and early success inflated perceived control over random outcomes. Fenton-O'Creevy and colleagues (2003) found that City of London traders most prone to the illusion scored significantly lower on analysis, risk management, and profit contribution.
    Failure modes: It inflates risk-taking because subjective risk feels lower than objective risk, and it creates insensitivity to negative feedback, impeding learning. Calibration research adds a caveat: people can also underestimate control they genuinely have.
    Misconceptions: Feeling in control is not evidence of control; the question is whether outcomes are actually conditional on one's actions. Elaborate tooling and fast execution are skill cues that do not change the stochastic nature of returns.
    Example: A scalper believes his rapid order flow works the market in his favor; over a year his results match a random-entry benchmark with the same risk parameters.

    Fear of missing out #

    The apprehension that others are having rewarding experiences or opportunities one is absent from, coupled with a drive to stay continually connected; the term was popularized by Patrick McGinnis in 2004 and studied psychologically by Przybylski and colleagues (2013). In trading it manifests as chasing fast-moving assets to avoid the regret of missing gains, a dynamic exploited in pump-and-dump schemes.
    Intuition: Watching an asset triple while strangers post profits online creates an itch to buy at any price - the pain of missing out outweighs the fear of loss.
    Mechanics: FOMO is linked to unmet psychological needs and amplified by social media's real-time display of others' gains; in markets it drives late entries into extended moves, size inflation, and abandonment of entry criteria, and it is deliberately triggered by promoters running coordinated pumps.
    Failure modes: It systematically produces buying near local tops, because emotional pressure peaks exactly when a move is most extended and most visible. It also degrades selectivity, replacing setup quality with urgency.
    Misconceptions: FOMO is not a rational response to genuine opportunity; the feeling scales with the social visibility of others' gains, not with expected value. Sitting out a move in which one had no edge is not a loss.
    Example: After a stock doubles in a week and dominates his feed, a trader with no prior thesis buys at the high; the stock mean-reverts 30% the following week.
    Aliases: FOMO

    Revenge trading #

    A practitioner term for impulsive trading driven by anger or the urge to win back money immediately after a loss: re-entering the market without a valid setup, often with increased size, to get even. It is treated in the trading-psychology literature as an emotional-control failure that compounds losses rather than recovers them.
    Intuition: After a stinging loss, the next trade is not analysis - it is retaliation against the market.
    Mechanics: A significant loss triggers frustration and urgency to repair the P&L, shortening the time between trades and overriding entry criteria and risk limits; position size frequently increases, so the loss spiral accelerates. It commonly co-occurs with tilt and overtrading.
    Failure modes: It converts one manageable loss into a cluster of undisciplined trades taken in the worst emotional state, often breaching daily loss limits and causing account-level drawdowns. Attempts to trade through it typically deepen rather than repair the damage.
    Misconceptions: The problem is not trading again soon after a loss - valid setups can occur immediately - but trading from the emotional need to recover rather than from an edge. The market owes the trader nothing; 'winning it back' is not a market state.
    Example: Stopped out for -1R, a trader immediately re-enters short with double size; two more impulsive losses turn a controlled -1R day into a -6R day.

    Overtrading #

    Trading more frequently or in greater aggregate volume than one's strategy, edge, or costs justify. Brokerage-account research by Barber and Odean shows individual investors' net returns decline with trading frequency, and the SEC-commissioned Library of Congress report lists active trading among the most damaging investor mistakes.
    Intuition: Every trade pays the spread, fees, and slippage - if the edge per trade is thin or absent, more trades just means more cost and more noise.
    Mechanics: Overconfidence and the desire for action push trade count beyond the arrival rate of valid setups; each marginal trade carries transaction costs and roughly zero or negative expectancy, so aggregate performance erodes with activity. Barber and Odean found the most active quintile of individual traders significantly underperformed the least active.
    Failure modes: Diagnosing overtrading requires knowing the strategy's true edge and cost structure; what looks like overtrading for one system is correct throughput for a genuine high-frequency edge. Boredom-driven or P&L-driven spikes in trade count are the practical signature.
    Misconceptions: High activity is not inherently wrong - market makers and statistical arbitrageurs trade constantly with positive expectancy; overtrading means trading beyond one's validated edge, not merely trading a lot. Nor does more screen time create more opportunity.
    Example: A swing trader whose backtest supports 40 trades a year takes 300, mostly marginal intraday punts; commissions and slippage consume the entire simulated edge.
    Aliases: Excessive trading

    Undertrading #

    Habitually failing to take valid trades that meet one's own criteria - hesitating, freezing, or skipping signals out of fear, recent losses, or excessive doubt. At the investor-population level its analogue is documented non-participation: the SEC-commissioned Library of Congress report describes widespread reluctance to invest at all despite suitable options.
    Intuition: The plan says buy, the setup is there, but fear of another loss keeps the finger off the button - and the missed winners never appear in the P&L to be noticed.
    Mechanics: Loss aversion, recent drawdowns, and performance anxiety raise the effective threshold for action, so qualifying setups are passed; because missed opportunities leave no trade record, the cost is invisible unless planned-but-skipped signals are journaled alongside executed trades.
    Failure modes: Chronically passing valid setups silently destroys a strategy's realized expectancy even though the visible trade record looks disciplined. The mirror failure is using patience as a rationalization for fear.
    Misconceptions: Undertrading is not selectivity: selectivity applies criteria, undertrading violates them. Nor is every skipped signal an error - only skips driven by emotion rather than by rules.
    Example: After three losing weeks, a trader skips the next five A-grade signals; four of them would have been winners, and his equity curve diverges from the system's backtest.
    Aliases: Hesitation, Freezing on signals

    Tilt #

    A state of emotional confusion or frustration - the term originates in poker - in which a person abandons sound strategy for suboptimal, usually overly aggressive play. In trading it denotes the degraded decision-making state that follows losses, humiliating outcomes, or provocation, and it also has a 'winner's tilt' variant after unexpected success.
    Intuition: Tilt is the moment the plan leaves the room: you are no longer trading your system, you are trading your anger.
    Mechanics: Frustration (typically a loss or bad beat) shifts behavior toward aggression, impulsivity, and larger risk; the standard countermeasures in the poker-derived literature are stopping play, lowering variance, and returning only after emotions subside. Strong positive emotions can produce the same degradation (winner's tilt).
    Failure modes: On tilt, risk limits and edge estimates are effectively suspended, so loss severity compounds; because the state feels like urgency and conviction rather than impairment, self-detection usually lags the behavior.
    Misconceptions: Tilt is not just being upset - it is an observable shift to a worse strategy. It is also not confined to losses: euphoric overconfidence after wins produces the same recklessness.
    Example: After a stop-out seconds before the market reversed, a trader fires off five marginal trades in twenty minutes, losing more than in the prior two weeks combined.
    Aliases: Steaming, On tilt
