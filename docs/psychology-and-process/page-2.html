    Performance anxiety reviewed #

    Anxiety triggered by the requirement to perform under evaluation or pressure, activating the sympathetic fight-or-flight response (racing heart, tremor, dry mouth) and degrading execution. In trading it appears around real-money exposure, position sizing, drawdowns, or being observed, and is related to choking under pressure in skilled performance.
    Intuition: The setup is the same one you nailed in simulation a hundred times, but with real money and people watching, your hands shake and you cannot pull the trigger.
    Mechanics: Anticipation of evaluation raises arousal beyond the zone of optimal performance, narrowing attention and disrupting well-learned decision routines; it affects even highly experienced performers and can begin long before the event. Standard mitigations are preparation, breathing and attentional routines, graded exposure, and self-monitoring.
    Failure modes: It corrupts execution precisely on high-stakes trades - hesitation on entries, premature exits, refusal to size per plan - and can generalize into chronic avoidance of valid risk. Suppressing symptoms without addressing sizing or process rarely works.
    Misconceptions: Performance anxiety is not a sign of incompetence; elite performers across domains report it. Nor is all arousal harmful - moderate arousal can aid performance; the problem is excessive evaluative pressure.
    Example: A trader who executes flawlessly in a demo account repeatedly exits funded positions minutes after entry, well before either stop or target, whenever the daily P&L is visible.
    Aliases: Stage fright, Choking under pressure

    Risk tolerance reviewed #

    The amount of risk an individual or organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives. In the ISO 31000 risk vocabulary, willingness (appetite/tolerance in the willingness sense) is distinguished from capacity - what the entity is able to bear - and from the risk threshold, the point at which action to reduce risk is required.
    Intuition: Risk tolerance is how much drawdown you can stomach without abandoning the plan - a psychological willingness number, not a financial one.
    Mechanics: Assessed through questionnaires, drawdown history, and observed behavior under loss, then translated into concrete constraints: per-trade risk, position-size limits, and portfolio volatility targets. It is meant to be set in advance and revisited on a schedule, not discovered mid-drawdown.
    Failure modes: Stated tolerance is systematically overestimated in bull markets and after wins, and is revealed only in drawdowns - willingness expressed at the top rarely survives contact with a real loss. Questionnaires also conflate tolerance with capacity and with recent experience.
    Misconceptions: Risk tolerance is not fixed by personality alone, and it is not the same as risk capacity: a wealthy investor can have low tolerance, while a leveraged investor can report high tolerance yet have almost no capacity. Feeling comfortable with risk is not evidence you can afford it.
    Example: A trader states he can tolerate a 30% drawdown but starts breaking rules at -12%; his revealed tolerance, not the questionnaire answer, should drive position sizing.
    Aliases: Risk appetite (willingness sense)

    Risk capacity reviewed #

    The maximum loss an individual or organization can financially absorb without jeopardizing essential goals or solvency - what one is able to risk, as opposed to risk tolerance, which is what one is willing to risk. In the ISO-derived framing, capability bounds willingness: the accepted level of risk should never exceed capacity.
    Intuition: Tolerance is about nerves; capacity is about math - the rent money has no risk capacity no matter how brave you feel.
    Mechanics: Computed from objective constraints: income stability, time horizon, liquidity needs, leverage, dependents, and obligations. Prudent practice sizes risk to the lesser of capacity and tolerance; exceeding capacity risks ruin regardless of psychological comfort.
    Failure modes: Capacity is often misjudged by ignoring correlated obligations (job loss plus drawdown, margin calls plus illiquidity) and it shrinks abruptly in crises exactly when positions are largest. Using tolerance rather than capacity for sizing is a classic path to ruin.
    Misconceptions: High risk tolerance does not create risk capacity, and neither youth nor confidence substitutes for reserves. Capacity is also dynamic - it changes with wealth, income, and liabilities, so it must be re-estimated.
    Example: A trader with three months of living expenses saved and a variable income has near-zero capacity for a $10,000 drawdown, whatever his appetite, so per-trade risk must be sized to capacity.
    Aliases: Ability to bear risk

    Trading discipline reviewed #

    The consistent execution of a predefined trading process - entries, exits, sizing, and risk limits - regardless of emotional state or short-term outcomes. In the trading-psychology literature it is treated as a trainable skill of aligning behavior with plan, countering the documented biases that push traders off process.
    Intuition: Discipline is doing the boring, correct thing - taking the stop, skipping the marginal setup - especially on the days you least want to.
    Mechanics: Built through explicit written rules, checklists, pre-committed exits, position-sizing formulas, and review routines that convert intentions into habits; it is measured by rule-adherence rates rather than by P&L. Steenbarger frames discipline as self-coaching: structured practice, rehearsal, and feedback.
    Failure modes: Discipline cannot rescue a strategy without edge - flawless execution of a losing system produces consistent losses. It also degrades under fatigue, tilt, and oversized positions, because emotional arousal overwhelms willpower-based control.
    Misconceptions: Discipline is not stubbornness: holding a loser past its invalidation is not conviction but indiscipline. Nor is it a fixed personality trait; it is largely a property of well-designed routines and constraints.
    Example: A trader's stop is hit during a news spike; she exits per plan instead of widening it, and her month-end review shows 98% rule adherence even in a losing month.

    Process orientation reviewed #

    An evaluative stance that judges trading performance by the quality of decisions and adherence to process rather than by individual trade outcomes. It is the deliberate correction for outcome bias, on the principle that in probabilistic domains good processes produce good results only on average, not on any single trial.
    Intuition: You cannot control whether this trade wins; you can control whether it was a good trade - so grade the decision, not the dollar result.
    Mechanics: Operationalized by defining controllable process elements (setup selection, entry timing, sizing, exit execution, review), scoring each trade on those elements, and aggregating process metrics separately from P&L. Bellafiore's 'one good trade' framework institutionalizes the stance: a trade is good if it followed the rules, whatever its result.
    Failure modes: Taken too far it can immunize a failing strategy from evidence: 'process was fine' must eventually reconcile with long-run expectancy or the process definition is wrong. Process scores are also themselves vulnerable to self-serving grading.
    Misconceptions: Process orientation does not mean outcomes are irrelevant - aggregate outcomes are the test of the process. It is also not positive thinking; it is measurement discipline applied to decisions under uncertainty.
    Example: A trader loses on three consecutive well-executed A-setups and changes nothing; the next month the same process recovers, confirming the losses were variance, not error.
    Aliases: Process-over-outcome

    Trading plan reviewed #

    A written document specifying in advance what will be traded, under which setups and conditions, with what entry, exit, sizing, and risk rules, and how performance will be reviewed. It converts trading from ad hoc reaction into an explicit, testable procedure and is a foundational tool of trading discipline.
    Intuition: The plan is the contract you sign with yourself before emotions show up: it answers every 'what now?' question before the market asks it.
    Mechanics: Typically contains instruments and sessions traded, setup definitions with entry and exit criteria, position-sizing and risk limits (per-trade, daily, total), invalidation conditions, and a review cadence. Its function is pre-commitment: moving decisions to a calm design phase so that in-session behavior is execution, not improvisation.
    Failure modes: Plans fail when they are vague ('buy strength'), when they encode no real edge, or when they are rewritten mid-session under stress. Over-specified plans also break at regime changes they did not anticipate.
    Misconceptions: A plan is not a prediction and need not forecast the market; it is a decision procedure. Nor is having ideas the same as having a plan - unwritten, untested rules cannot be followed or evaluated.
    Example: A trader's plan says: only momentum setups on gapping large-caps, risk 0.5% per trade, stop at the premarket low, maximum three trades daily - so a midday FOMO urge to buy a random mover is answered by the document, not the mood.

    Pre-trade checklist reviewed #

    A short list of conditions verified immediately before entering a trade - setup validity, thesis, risk size, stop location, catalysts, and emotional state - designed to filter impulsive trades and enforce the trading plan at the moment of execution.
    Intuition: Pilots do not take off on memory and gut feel; the checklist is the ten seconds that stops a FOMO click from becoming a trade.
    Mechanics: Each item is a binary gate (for example: Is this a defined playbook setup? Is the stop placed? Is risk within limits? Am I calm?); any failed item blocks or defers the trade. Its value is forcing deliberation at the point where arousal and urgency peak.
    Failure modes: Checklists degrade into box-ticking when items are vague or when marginal answers are rationalized under urgency. They also add latency, which matters for very short-horizon strategies unless the checklist is rehearsed to automaticity.
    Misconceptions: A checklist is not bureaucracy for beginners only; it exists precisely because expert intuition is least reliable under time pressure and arousal. Passing the checklist does not make the trade good - it only enforces minimum standards.
    Example: Before buying a breakout, a trader confirms: setup in playbook, volume above threshold, stop at the structure low risking 0.4R of the daily limit, no major news in the next hour - then enters.

    Post-trade review reviewed #

    The structured analysis of completed trades - thesis versus execution, rule adherence, and outcome in R-multiples - conducted after the trade to extract lessons and feed them back into the playbook. Sound review evaluates decisions by ex-ante quality, not merely by whether the trade made money.
    Intuition: The trade is not over when you exit; it is over when you have written down what the market just taught you.
    Mechanics: Each closed trade is logged with screenshots, entry and exit rationale, and rule-adherence scoring; patterns across trades (which setups, times of day, or error types drive results) are aggregated in daily and weekly reviews. Review explicitly separates process grades from outcomes to avoid outcome bias.
    Failure modes: Review fails when small samples are over-interpreted, when only losers are reviewed, or when hindsight bias rewrites the original rationale. Reviews without recorded pre-trade reasoning degrade into storytelling.
    Misconceptions: Reviewing only P&L is not review; the unit of analysis is the decision. Nor is review an occasion for self-punishment - its function is system improvement, not emotional accounting.
    Example: A trader tags all trades by setup in her journal and finds that 80% of losses come from afternoon counter-trend trades, which she then removes from her plan.
    Aliases: Trade review, Trade debrief

    Trading journal reviewed #

    A systematic record of trades and the reasoning behind them - entries, exits, size, thesis, emotional state, and lessons - used to create an objective feedback loop for performance. Research on hindsight bias specifically recommends contemporaneous decision diaries because memory of past reasoning is unreliable.
    Intuition: Your memory says you are a disciplined genius; the journal shows the trades you actually took.
    Mechanics: Trades are logged at or near decision time with rationale and context, then analyzed for statistics (win rate, expectancy, R-distribution by setup) and behavioral patterns (rule breaks, tilt episodes). Written contemporaneous records are the principal defense against 'knew-it-all-along' memory distortion.
    Failure modes: Journals fail when entries are written after outcomes are known (hindsight contamination), when only narrative and no structured data is kept, or when data is collected but never analyzed. Inconsistent logging produces a database that cannot support conclusions.
    Misconceptions: A journal is neither a diary of feelings alone nor a bare trade blotter; it is the combination of decision records plus outcomes that enables calibration. Keeping one does not automatically improve trading - only analyzed journals do.
    Example: Three months of journaled trades reveal that a trader's average winner is 2.1R but his average loser is 1.4R because he overrides stops - a fact invisible in his memory.
    Aliases: Trade log

    Decision journal reviewed #

    A contemporaneous record of the reasoning, expectations, and estimated probabilities behind significant decisions, written before outcomes are known. Its purpose is to make judgment auditable: it counters hindsight bias, enables calibration of forecasts, and separates decision quality from luck.
    Intuition: Write down what you expect and why before the market answers; later you get to grade your thinking instead of your self-image.
    Mechanics: For each material decision the trader records the thesis, key assumptions, what would change his mind, and a probability or scenario sketch; at review, predictions are compared with outcomes and with what was actually known at the time. It generalizes the trade-thesis log to all consequential decisions, not only trades.
    Failure modes: It is undermined by vague, unfalsifiable entries ('feels bullish') that any outcome can confirm, and by recording probabilities without ever scoring them. The writing cost is real, so it is usually reserved for significant decisions.
    Misconceptions: A decision journal is not a prediction contest; its value is exposing systematic reasoning errors, not maximizing hit rate. Nor is it only for losing decisions - winning decisions made for bad reasons are the most dangerous to leave unexamined.
    Example: Before a CPI release a trader logs: '70% odds of a hawkish print; if wrong, expect index -1%; my long is invalid below 5,480.' Post-release review tests both the forecast and the sizing logic.
    Aliases: Decision diary

    Trade thesis reviewed #

    A concise, falsifiable statement of why a trade should work: the setup, the edge being exploited, the conditions required, the expected path, and what would prove the idea wrong. It is the pre-committed reasoning against which execution and outcome are later reviewed.
    Intuition: If you cannot say in two sentences why the trade exists and what kills it, you do not have a trade - you have a hope.
    Mechanics: Written before entry: context (trend, level, catalyst), the playbook setup, entry trigger, invalidation, target logic, and time frame. It defines in advance the evidence that would disconfirm the idea, anchoring exits to information rather than emotion.
    Failure modes: Theses fail when they are unfalsifiable narratives rather than testable claims, or when they are quietly edited after entry as price moves against the position. A coherent story is not an edge - narrative coherence is cheap (narrative bias).
    Misconceptions: A thesis is not a prediction that must be right; it is a hypothesis with predefined failure conditions. Confidence in the thesis does not substitute for a defined invalidation point.
    Example: 'Breakout over the three-month range on twice average volume after earnings; invalid below the range top at 102; target the measured move at 115 within two weeks' - every element checkable.

    Invalidation condition reviewed #

    The predefined price level, event, or condition at which the trade thesis is considered proven wrong and the position must be exited regardless of emotion. It is the logical core of a stop: the exit occurs because the reason for the trade is gone, not merely because pain is felt.
    Intuition: Decide at entry what would prove you wrong; when it happens, the debate is over.
    Mechanics: Set before entry from the structure of the thesis (below the breakout level, on failure of the catalyst, on time-stop expiry) and operationalized as a hard stop order or an enforced mental exit. Together with position size it fixes per-trade risk: risk = (entry - invalidation) x size.
    Failure modes: Invalidations set too tight are hit by noise; set from pain tolerance rather than structure, they are arbitrary. The most common failure is moving or ignoring the level after entry, converting a defined-risk trade into an open-ended one.
    Misconceptions: Invalidation is a statement about the thesis, not about how much money one is willing to lose - the two coincide only by deliberate design. Re-entering later on a new signal is fully consistent with having honored the original invalidation.
    Example: Long at 50 on a range breakout, thesis invalid below 48; price closes at 47.8 and the trader exits without negotiation, later re-entering only when a fresh setup forms.
    Position size = (account risk per trade in currency) / |entry price - invalidation price|; e.g., risking $500 with entry 50 and invalidation 48 gives 250 shares.
    Aliases: Invalidation level, Thesis stop

    Entry criterion reviewed #

    The explicit, predefined conditions that must all be present before a position is opened - the setup pattern, trigger, context filters, and confirmation requirements of the trading plan. Entry criteria turn good ideas into repeatable, testable decisions.
    Intuition: The difference between a system and gambling is that the system can say 'not yet' in one sentence.
    Mechanics: Defined per setup in the playbook - for example trend alignment, level, trigger bar or volume condition, and time window; a trade is taken only when every criterion is met and skipped otherwise. Documented criteria enable backtesting and post-trade grading of selection quality.
    Failure modes: Criteria that are vague admit impulse trades; criteria that are too numerous or curve-fitted to a small sample filter out nearly everything or break out of sample. Under FOMO, entry criteria are the first thing abandoned.
    Misconceptions: A perfect entry is not the goal - sizing and exits matter more to expectancy; criteria exist for consistency, not precision. More confirmation is not always better: each added filter costs opportunity and can add fragility.
    Example: Long only if: price above the rising 20-day average, the pullback holds the prior breakout level, and a reversal bar closes in its top third - all three required, every time.
    Aliases: Entry rule, Entry trigger

    Exit criterion reviewed #

    The predefined rules governing when and how a position is closed - profit targets, stop or invalidation exits, time stops, and trailing logic. Exit criteria are decided at entry so that exits respond to information and structure rather than to fear or greed in real time.
    Intuition: Anyone can get into a trade; the exit is where the P&L is actually written, so it cannot be improvised mid-emotion.
    Mechanics: Typically combines an invalidation exit (stop), one or more profit-taking rules (fixed target, measured move, trailing stop), and optionally a time stop; exchange order types (stop, stop-limit, trailing stop) implement them mechanically. Together with entry and size, exit rules determine the trade's R-multiple distribution.
    Failure modes: Exits improvised under stress systematically become 'sell winners early, hold losers' - the disposition effect. Targets placed at widely watched round numbers invite slippage, and stops at obvious levels invite stop-runs.
    Misconceptions: Letting winners run without any exit rule is not a strategy - trailing or target logic is still required. Exiting at the planned stop is not a failure of the trade; it is the design working.
    Example: Plan: stop at 47.8, scale out half at +2R, trail the remainder below each higher low; when price tags the second target, the rest exits on the first close below the prior swing low.
    Aliases: Exit rule

    Setup quality reviewed #

    A grade of how well a specific trade opportunity matches the trader's validated setups and current market conditions - in proprietary-trading practice expressed as tiers (A/B/C) that gate size and frequency. It operationalizes selectivity: capital concentrates on the highest-quality instances of proven patterns.
    Intuition: Not every valid-looking pattern deserves the same money; an A-setup in the right market is a different bet from a marginal lookalike.
    Mechanics: Playbook setups are scored before entry against confluence factors (trend, level, volume, catalyst, time of day, the setup's recent tracked performance); the grade determines whether to trade and how much to risk. Bellafiore's SMB framework ties grade to conviction and size, and grades are audited in daily review.
    Failure modes: Grades drift under emotion: after losses everything looks like a C, after wins everything looks like an A. Grading without historical per-setup expectancy data is subjective relabeling, not measurement.
    Misconceptions: Quality is a property of the setup-context match, not of how confident or excited the trader feels. Skipping B and C setups is not cowardice - it is how expectancy is protected when edges are thin.
    Example: A pullback-to-VWAP long in a strong trend after earnings grades A and gets full size; the same pattern in a choppy midday tape grades C and is skipped.
    Aliases: Setup grade

    Rule adherence reviewed #

    The degree to which actual trading behavior matches the written rules of the trading plan - entries, exits, sizing, and risk limits taken as specified, with deviations logged. It is the primary process metric of trading discipline.
    Intuition: The system only works if the operator runs it; rule adherence measures whether you ran your system or your mood.
    Mechanics: Each trade is scored rule by rule (valid setup, correct size, stop honored, exit per plan); the adherence rate is the share of fully compliant trades. Deviations are classified (early entry, moved stop, oversized, revenge trade) and tracked over time in the journal.
    Failure modes: High adherence to a bad system yields consistent losses - adherence measures fidelity, not edge. Rules written with ambiguity cannot be scored, which makes adherence unmeasurable and review toothless.
    Misconceptions: Perfect adherence is not rigidity: planned, documented rule changes made in review are part of the process, while improvising mid-trade is not. A winning trade that broke rules counts as an adherence failure, not a success.
    Example: A trader's month shows 22 of 25 trades fully compliant (88%); the three violations - all moved stops - account for 60% of the month's drawdown.
    Rule adherence (%) = (number of trades fully compliant with plan rules) / (total trades) x 100.
    Aliases: Plan compliance

    Execution error reviewed #

    A mistake in implementing an intended trade - wrong order type or price, fat-fingered size, missed or late entry, early or late exit relative to plan - as distinct from a flawed idea. It is measured as the gap between intended and actual execution.
    Intuition: The analysis was right and the plan was right; the fingers or the timing were wrong.
    Mechanics: Detected by comparing each trade to its plan: slippage versus expected fill, entry timing versus trigger, exit versus exit rule. Sources include inattention, platform errors, latency, and emotional hesitation; mitigations are checklists, hotkeys with size limits, and rehearsed order routines.
    Failure modes: Execution errors are most frequent exactly when markets are fastest and stakes highest; in leveraged instruments a single size or order-type error can exceed all planned risk. They are undercounted unless intent is recorded before the trade.
    Misconceptions: A profitable fat-finger is still an error, not a win; grading by outcome hides the defect. Small repeated execution leaks (late entries, chasing fills) compound into a large expectancy drag even when each instance seems trivial.
    Example: Intending a limit buy at 50.00 with a 49.50 stop, a trader market-buys at 50.35 in a spike; the extra 0.35 of slippage turns a planned 2:1 trade into 1.4:1 before it starts.
    Aliases: Implementation error, Fat-finger error

    Analysis error reviewed #

    A flaw in the reasoning or information processing behind a trade - misread trend, wrong level, ignored evidence, miscalibrated probability - as distinct from an execution mistake or a risk-management breach. It is the error class that review and education, rather than checklists, must fix.
    Intuition: You did exactly what you planned; the problem is the plan was built on a misread chart.
    Mechanics: Identified in post-trade and decision-journal review by comparing the recorded thesis with the information available at the time: cognitive biases (confirmation, anchoring, recency), data errors, and logical gaps are the usual culprits. Persistent analysis errors point to gaps in market understanding or to a systematic bias.
    Failure modes: Hindsight bias makes analysis errors hard to diagnose honestly - the misread is rewritten as nearly right. Small samples confound signal: a correct analysis can lose and a wrong one can win several times in a row.
    Misconceptions: A losing trade is not automatically an analysis error - sound analysis of a probabilistic setup still loses its share of times. Conversely, a winning trade built on misread information is still an analysis error, not validation.
    Example: A trader shorts into what he reads as distribution, ignoring that the decline is on falling volume into a major demand zone; review tags it as an analysis error: context filter omitted.

    Risk-management error reviewed #

    A breach of the rules that bound loss - oversizing, moving or removing stops, exceeding daily loss limits, adding to losers, or ignoring correlation between positions. It is the most dangerous error class because it converts bounded, survivable mistakes into account-threatening losses.
    Intuition: You can be wrong a hundred times and survive if risk is controlled; one risk-management error at the wrong leverage can end the account.
    Mechanics: Measured by comparing realized per-trade and per-day losses and exposures against plan limits and against risk capacity; violations are tagged in the journal and reviewed separately from P&L. Defenses are structural: hard stops, platform-enforced size and loss limits, and sizing formulas tied to invalidation distance.
    Failure modes: These errors cluster during tilt and after losses, when the urge to recover overrides limits; they are also hidden by luck - oversized bets that win teach the wrong lesson until variance collects. Correlated positions make nominal diversification illusory.
    Misconceptions: Risk management is not a drag on returns bolted onto the strategy; sizing and exits are part of the strategy's expectancy. A stop that was never hit by luck does not prove the oversize was safe.
    Example: Plan caps risk at 0.5% per trade and 1.5% per day; during a news spike the trader doubles size and lifts the stop, taking a 4% hit - logged in review as a risk-management error, the category the plan exists to prevent.

    Process metric reviewed #

    A quantitative measure of how well the trading process was executed - rule-adherence rate, setup-selection grade distribution, slippage versus plan, review completion - independent of whether individual trades won or lost. Process metrics make discipline measurable and correct for outcome bias in self-evaluation.
    Intuition: P&L tells you what the market did; process metrics tell you what you did.
    Mechanics: Derived from the journal and execution data: percentage of trades on valid playbook setups, stops honored, planned size used, entries within the trigger window, checklist completion. Tracked weekly and monthly to detect behavioral drift before it shows up in the equity curve.
    Failure modes: Metrics get gamed (Goodhart's law): traders contort behavior to hit adherence percentages while hollowing out their meaning. Process metrics also cannot validate the strategy itself - perfect process on zero edge still loses.
    Misconceptions: Process metrics are not vanity statistics; they are leading indicators because process degradation precedes P&L degradation. They also do not replace outcome metrics - the two are read together.
    Example: A trader tracks: 94% of trades on playbook setups, 100% of stops honored, average entry slippage 0.04 - and treats a dip in any of them as a warning independent of the month's profit.

    Outcome metric reviewed #

    A measure of trading results - net P&L, expectancy, win rate, average R-multiple, drawdown, risk-adjusted return - capturing what the strategy earned rather than how it was executed. Outcome metrics are the ultimate test of a process but are noisy and must not be read trade by trade.
    Intuition: Outcomes are the scoreboard, but over any small number of games the scoreboard mostly measures luck.
    Mechanics: Computed from closed trades: expectancy combines win rate with average win and loss sizes; R-distributions, maximum drawdown, and risk-adjusted returns complete the picture. Interpreted over adequate samples and paired with process metrics to attribute results to skill, system, or variance.
    Failure modes: Short-horizon outcome metrics are dominated by variance: dozens to hundreds of trades may be needed before expectancy estimates stabilize. Maximizing outcome metrics directly invites overfitting, hidden tail risk, and outcome-biased self-assessment.
    Misconceptions: A profitable month does not prove a process improvement, and a losing month does not disprove one - sample size governs what outcome metrics can say. A high win rate alone is meaningless without the sizes of wins and losses.
    Example: Over 200 journaled trades a system shows a 42% win rate, average win 2.3R, average loss 1.0R - expectancy about +0.39R per trade, a positive edge invisible in any single week.
    Expectancy E = p_w * W_avg - p_l * L_avg, where p_w = win rate, W_avg = average win, p_l = loss rate, L_avg = average loss (in R-multiples or currency).

    A-trade reviewed #

    In the setup-grading taxonomy used in proprietary trading (popularized by Mike Bellafiore's SMB framework), the highest grade of opportunity: a trade matching the trader's best playbook patterns under favorable conditions, warranting full planned position size. The grade is a sizing decision based on the pattern class's tracked expectancy, not a claim about certainty.
    Intuition: An A-trade is the one you trained for - your best pattern, in the right market, at the right time; that is where size belongs.
    Mechanics: Setups are graded before entry against confluence criteria (pattern quality, trend, volume, catalyst, time of day, recent tracked performance of the setup); A grades receive maximum planned risk, lower grades reduced or zero size. Grades are audited in daily review against outcomes per grade.
    Failure modes: Grade inflation under overconfidence or FOMO turns marginal trades into 'A-trades'; without per-grade outcome tracking the label is decoration. Even genuine A-setups lose often - the grade governs size, not the result.
    Misconceptions: An A-trade is not a guaranteed winner; it is a bet-sizing category grounded in historical expectancy of the pattern class. Confidence felt in the moment is not a grading criterion.
    Example: Earnings-gap momentum continuation with triple relative volume in a trending tape grades A and takes the full 1R risk; the same pattern pre-market in thin volume does not.

    B-trade reviewed #

    In the A/B/C setup-grading taxonomy used in proprietary trading, a valid playbook setup taken under only partially favorable conditions - worth trading but at reduced size relative to an A-trade. It is the middle tier of the setup-quality hierarchy that links conviction to position size.
    Intuition: The pattern is right but the context is average - you can play it, just not with A-trade money.
    Mechanics: A setup meeting playbook definitions but missing some confluence (weaker trend alignment, mediocre volume, suboptimal time of day) is graded B and traded at a fraction of full risk, preserving capital for A opportunities. Per-grade outcome tracking in review keeps the grade boundaries honest.
    Failure modes: Under emotional pressure, B-trades get relabeled as A-trades for size, or skipped entirely after losses; both corrupt the sizing logic the taxonomy exists to enforce. B-trades also have thinner expectancy, so execution slippage hurts them proportionally more.
    Misconceptions: A B-trade is not a bad trade - it is a smaller bet on a positive but lower-expectancy instance. Trading B-setups at full size, not trading them at all, is the common mistake.
    Example: A clean pullback pattern in a directionless midday tape grades B: tradable at half size because the pattern is right but the context is average.

    C-trade reviewed #

    The lowest grade in the A/B/C setup taxonomy used in proprietary trading: a marginal or borderline opportunity that technically resembles a playbook setup but lacks confluence, so its historical expectancy does not cover costs. Standard practice is to skip C-trades or trade them only at minimal, exploratory size.
    Intuition: A C-trade is a trade you take because you are bored or afraid of missing out - the grade exists so you recognize it and pass.
    Mechanics: Borderline instances of playbook patterns - weak volume, wrong time of day, choppy context - are graded C, which in a disciplined framework means no trade or minimal size. Tracking C-grade outcomes in review typically confirms that skipping them adds to net expectancy by cutting cost bleed.
    Failure modes: C-trades are where overtrading and FOMO actually enter the account: each is individually small, but their aggregate cost and negative expectancy erode the edge earned on A and B setups. Grading discipline collapses first on quiet days, when C-setups are most tempting.
    Misconceptions: Skipping C-trades is not excessive caution - when a pattern's tracked expectancy is below costs, not trading is the profitable decision. A C-grade that wins is still a C-grade.
    Example: A breakout with no volume confirmation late on a quiet Friday grades C and is passed; its tracked expectancy over similar past instances was negative after costs.

    Daily review reviewed #

    An end-of-day routine in which the day's trades are logged, graded against plan rules, and analyzed for errors and lessons before the next session. It is the shortest feedback loop in the trading process and the primary driver of incremental improvement.
    Intuition: Ten honest minutes with the day's trades each evening is worth more than ten hours of new course material.
    Mechanics: Typical steps: reconcile fills, tag each trade by setup, score rule adherence, classify errors (analysis, execution, risk), note emotional state, and write one or two actionable lessons; results feed the weekly review. Professional prop desks institutionalize recorded daily trade review.
    Failure modes: It is skipped on losing days - when it matters most - or degrades into P&L checking, losing its function. The opposite failure is overreacting to a single day's noise by rewriting rules nightly.
    Misconceptions: Daily review is not about judging the day's profit; one day is almost pure variance. It is also not optional maintenance - it is where plan, journal, and psychology connect.
    Example: After the close a trader replays his three trades, tags one as a C-setup he should not have taken, notes he hesitated on a valid entry, and writes both into tomorrow's pre-market notes.
