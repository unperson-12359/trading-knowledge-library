    Rolling maximum reviewed #

    The rolling maximum is the highest value of a series within a trailing window of fixed length that slides forward one observation at a time. It underlies channel indicators such as Donchian channels and breakout systems.
    Intuition: At each point, look back over the last N bars and ask: what was the highest price seen? That level moves as old extremes drop out of the window.
    Mechanics: For window length w, compute max over observations x_{t−w+1}…x_t and repeat for each t; standard implementations (e.g., pandas rolling().max()) label the result at the window's right edge and return NaN until the window is full unless min_periods is set.
    Failure modes: The statistic is window-sensitive and jumps discontinuously when an old extreme exits the window, creating artificial 'breakdowns' of the level. Centering the window introduces lookahead bias into backtests.
    Misconceptions: A break above the rolling maximum is a mechanical event defined by the window choice, not independent evidence of a meaningful breakout.
    Example: With a 20-day rolling maximum on daily highs, if the highest high of the last 20 days is 112.30, today's upper Donchian line sits at 112.30.
    RMax_t = max(x_{t−w+1}, …, x_t), where w is the window length.
    Aliases: rolling max, moving maximum, trailing maximum

    Rolling minimum reviewed #

    The rolling minimum is the lowest value of a series within a trailing window of fixed length that slides forward one observation at a time. Together with the rolling maximum it defines the lower boundary of channel indicators such as Donchian channels.
    Intuition: At each point, look back over the last N bars and ask: what was the lowest price seen? The level ratchets as time passes and old lows expire from the window.
    Mechanics: For window length w, compute min over observations x_{t−w+1}…x_t at each t; implementations such as pandas rolling().min() label at the window's right edge and produce NaN until the window fills unless min_periods is set.
    Failure modes: Like the rolling maximum, it jumps discontinuously when an extreme exits the window and is entirely defined by the chosen window length; centered windows leak future data into the past.
    Misconceptions: The rolling minimum is not a support level in any market-microstructure sense — it is purely the trailing extreme over an arbitrary lookback.
    Example: With a 20-day rolling minimum on daily lows, if the lowest low of the last 20 days is 96.10, the lower Donchian line sits at 96.10 today.
    RMin_t = min(x_{t−w+1}, …, x_t), where w is the window length.
    Aliases: rolling min, moving minimum, trailing minimum
    Related:
    • Rolling maximum
    • Donchian channel
    • Low price
    • Rolling median
    Source: pandas User Guide: Windowing operations — Rolling window (supported aggregations incl. min)

    Rolling mean reviewed #

    The rolling mean is the arithmetic average of a series over a trailing window of fixed length that slides forward one observation at a time. Applied to prices it is the simple moving average (SMA), the most common trend-smoothing statistic in market analysis.
    Intuition: The rolling mean smooths out bar-to-bar noise by averaging the last N values, so the line rises when recent values are generally higher than the ones dropping out.
    Mechanics: For window length w, sum the last w observations and divide by w at each step. pandas rolling(window).mean() labels the result at the right edge of the window and returns NaN for the first w−1 points by default (min_periods=w for fixed windows).
    Failure modes: The mean lags the series by roughly half the window and is distorted by outliers, since each extreme pulls the average for w periods. Equal weighting of the oldest and newest observations is arbitrary; window changes can flip signals.
    Misconceptions: A moving-average crossover is a descriptive smoothing artifact, not a proven predictive signal; and the SMA is not 'the fair price' — it is a trailing average.
    Example: A 5-day rolling mean of closes 100, 102, 101, 103, 104 is (100+102+101+103+104)/5 = 102.0 on the fifth day.
    μ_t = (1/w) Σ_{i=0}^{w−1} x_{t−i}, where w is the window length.
    Aliases: rolling average, simple moving average, SMA, moving average
    Related:

    • Rolling standard deviation
    • Moving average
    • Rolling z-score
    Source: pandas User Guide: Windowing operations — Rolling window (mean aggregation)

    Rolling median reviewed #

    The rolling median is the median value of a series over a trailing window of fixed length that slides forward one observation at a time. It is a robust alternative to the rolling mean, insensitive to extreme values within the window.
    Intuition: Instead of averaging the last N bars, take the middle one — a single spike can pull a mean around but cannot move the median much.
    Mechanics: For window length w, sort the last w observations and take the middle value (average of the two middle values for even w) at each step. pandas supports rolling(window).median(), with the same right-edge labeling and min_periods conventions as other rolling aggregations.
    Failure modes: The median is a coarse, step-like statistic that can sit unchanged for many bars and then jump, which makes derivative-based signals awkward. It is also computationally heavier than the mean and still lagged by the window.
    Misconceptions: Robustness to outliers is not always desirable — in return series, extreme moves are often the information, and median-filtering them away changes the risk picture.
    Example: For the last 5 closes 100, 101, 99, 130, 102, the rolling median is 101 while the rolling mean is 106.4, showing the median's immunity to the 130 spike.
    RMed_t = median(x_{t−w+1}, …, x_t), where w is the window length.
    Aliases: moving median, median filter
    Related:
    • Rolling mean
    • Median price
    • Rolling maximum
    • Rolling minimum
    Source: pandas User Guide: Windowing operations — Rolling window (median aggregation)

    Rolling standard deviation reviewed #

    The rolling standard deviation is the sample (or population) standard deviation of a series over a trailing window of fixed length that slides forward one observation at a time. Applied to returns it is the standard estimator of realized volatility and the width driver of Bollinger Bands.
    Intuition: It measures how spread out the last N returns (or prices) were — a live gauge of how violently the market has been moving recently.
    Mechanics: For window w, compute σ over the last w observations at each step; pandas rolling(window).std() uses the sample (unbiased, ddof=1) convention by default and labels at the window's right edge. On returns, annualization scales by √T (e.g., √252 for daily data).
    Failure modes: The estimate assumes the window's observations come from one stable regime; volatility clustering makes short windows noisy and long windows slow to react. Squaring returns gives extreme observations outsized influence, and with w−1 denominators small windows are unreliable.
    Misconceptions: Rolling std of prices is not comparable across instruments or time as price levels drift — volatility is conventionally computed on returns, not price levels.
    Example: The 20-day rolling std of daily log returns at 0.015 (1.5%) annualizes to roughly 0.015 × √252 ≈ 23.8%.
    σ_t = sqrt( (1/(w−1)) Σ_{i=0}^{w−1} (x_{t−i} − μ_t)² ), where μ_t is the rolling mean over the same window (use 1/w for the population convention).
    Aliases: rolling volatility, rolling std, moving standard deviation

    Rolling correlation reviewed #

    Rolling correlation is the Pearson correlation coefficient between two series computed over a trailing window of fixed length that slides forward one observation at a time. It tracks how the co-movement of two assets (typically their returns) evolves through time.
    Intuition: Instead of one correlation number for the whole history, you get a time series showing when two markets moved together and when they decoupled.
    Mechanics: For window w, compute the Pearson coefficient over the paired observations in the last w points at each t; pandas rolling().corr() supports Series–Series and pairwise DataFrame computations, ignoring missing values pairwise. Correlations are typically computed on returns, not price levels.
    Failure modes: Short windows produce wildly unstable estimates that swing between −1 and +1 on noise; correlation computed on trending price levels is spuriously high. Crisis periods reveal that correlations estimated in calm windows understate co-movement exactly when diversification is needed.
    Misconceptions: A high rolling correlation over a window does not establish a stable relationship — window choice can manufacture or erase apparent correlation, and correlation is not causation.
    Example: A 60-day rolling correlation between SPY and TLT daily returns hovering near −0.4 that jumps toward +0.2 signals a regime shift in the stock-bond relationship.
    ρ_t = Σ (x_i − μ_x)(y_i − μ_y) / sqrt( Σ (x_i − μ_x)² Σ (y_i − μ_y)² ), sums over the trailing w paired observations.
    Aliases: moving correlation, trailing correlation

    Rolling beta reviewed #

    Rolling beta is the regression slope of an asset's returns on a benchmark's returns — covariance divided by benchmark variance — estimated over a trailing window that slides forward one observation at a time. It tracks how an asset's market sensitivity evolves rather than assuming a single static beta.
    Intuition: Beta asks 'when the market moves 1%, how much does this asset typically move?'; rolling beta shows that answer changing through time as the asset's risk profile shifts.
    Mechanics: For window w, compute β_t = Cov_w(r_asset, r_market) / Var_w(r_market) over the last w paired return observations at each t; pandas rolling().cov() and rolling().var() provide the components. The result is the CAPM-style beta estimated on the trailing sample.
    Failure modes: Beta estimates are regime-dependent: a window spanning a volatility spike or structural break blends incompatible regimes, and short windows give noisy, unstable slopes. Rolling beta is backward-looking and often fails to anticipate beta shifts at market turning points.
    Misconceptions: Beta is not correlation — it scales correlation by relative volatility — and a low trailing beta does not guarantee low future market sensitivity, especially for assets with option-like payoffs.
    Example: If over the last 252 days Cov(r_stock, r_S&P500) = 0.0030 and Var(r_S&P500) = 0.0020, the stock's rolling 1-year beta is 1.5.
    β_t = Cov_w(r_i, r_m) / Var_w(r_m), where r_i and r_m are the asset's and benchmark's returns over the trailing window of length w.
    Aliases: moving beta, trailing beta
    Related:
    • Rolling correlation
    • Rolling standard deviation
    • Beta
    • CAPM
    Source: pandas User Guide: Windowing operations — Binary window functions (cov) and rolling pairwise covariances
    Note: Beta itself is defined by the CAPM/linear-regression literature; the citation documents the rolling covariance/variance computation used to estimate it on a trailing window.

    Rolling z-score reviewed #

    The rolling z-score standardizes each observation of a series by the rolling mean and rolling standard deviation of its trailing window: how many recent-standard-deviations the current value sits from the recent mean. It converts levels into a locally normalized scale used by mean-reversion and Bollinger-style analyses.
    Intuition: A z-score of +2 says 'today's value is unusually high compared with the recent past' — unusual measured in units of the recent volatility, not absolute price.
    Mechanics: At each t compute z_t = (x_t − μ_t)/σ_t, where μ_t and σ_t are the rolling mean and rolling standard deviation over the trailing window of length w. pandas rolling().mean() and rolling().std() supply the components; care is needed on whether the current observation is included in the window.
    Failure modes: Z-scores assume approximate local normality and stationarity; in trending or fat-tailed series, |z| > 2 events are far more common than the Gaussian 5%, so 'extreme' readings are not reliable reversal signals. The statistic also inherits the window sensitivity of its mean and std components.
    Misconceptions: A high z-score is not automatically an overbought signal — in a persistent trend, price can hold z > 2 for long stretches. The z-score is relative to an arbitrary lookback, not to any fundamental anchor.
    Example: If the 20-day rolling mean of a spread is 1.20 with rolling std 0.15 and today's spread is 1.65, the z-score is (1.65 − 1.20)/0.15 = +3.0.
    z_t = (x_t − μ_t) / σ_t, with μ_t and σ_t the rolling mean and rolling standard deviation over the trailing window of length w.
    Aliases: moving z-score, rolling standardized value
