    Wyckoff method #

    A discretionary technical-analysis framework developed by Richard D. Wyckoff in the early twentieth century that interprets price action, volume, and time to judge the supply/demand balance and hypothesized campaigns of large institutional operators. It organizes market movement into accumulation, markup, distribution, and markdown phases and is an analytical hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: Big players move markets by quietly building and unwinding large positions; if you can read their footprints in price and volume, you can trade in their direction instead of being trapped by them.
    Mechanics: Practitioners apply a five-step approach: assess the broad market trend and its position, select stocks stronger (or weaker) than the market, require a sufficient cause measured by point-and-figure counts, confirm readiness with Wyckoff's nine buying or selling tests, and time entry with turns in the market index. Within trading ranges, events (PS, SC, AR, ST, springs, SOS/LPS) and phases (A-E) are labeled to infer whether accumulation or distribution is occurring.
    Failure modes: Phase and event labeling is subjective and often clear only in hindsight; ranges can extend, morph, or resolve opposite to the labeled interpretation, and the framework assumes the presence of dominant institutional campaigns that may not apply in thinly traded or highly fragmented markets.
    Misconceptions: It is not a mechanical signal system with fixed rules, and its 'laws' are heuristics rather than physical laws; Wyckoff himself cautioned that the market never behaves exactly the same way twice.
    Example: An analyst labels a six-month sideways range after a long decline as accumulation (selling climax, secondary tests, a low-volume spring, then a sign of strength), takes a long position on the last point of support, and projects a price target from a point-and-figure count of the range.
    Aliases: Wyckoff analysis, Wyckoff price cycle, Wyckoff trading method

    Composite operator #

    Wyckoff's heuristic device of treating all market fluctuations as if they were produced by one fictional large operator, the Composite Man, who plans, executes, and concludes campaigns of accumulation and distribution. It is a mental model for interpreting chart behavior, not the claim that a single entity actually controls prices.
    Intuition: Pretend the whole market is one clever dealer running a shop: it builds inventory quietly, advertises to attract buyers at high prices, then sells out before prices fall.
    Mechanics: The analyst studies each chart as the Composite Man's campaign, judging from price, volume, and time whether the operator is accumulating, distributing, or marking prices up or down, and aligns trades with the inferred stage of that campaign. Wyckoff taught that the operator attracts public participation by creating the appearance of a broad, active market.
    Failure modes: The anthropomorphic model invites confirmation bias: almost any price path can be rationalized as operator intent, and modern markets with many competing institutions, index funds, and algorithms fit the single-operator picture poorly.
    Misconceptions: Wyckoff did not claim markets are literally manipulated by one cabal; he explicitly said it does not matter whether moves are 'real or artificial', only that the trader understands the game being played.
    Example: A trader interprets a stock's quiet sideways drift on shrinking volume after a panic low as the Composite Man absorbing supply, then treats a high-volume breakout as the start of the operator's markup campaign.
    Aliases: Composite Man, smart money

    Wyckoff accumulation #

    The phase of the Wyckoff price cycle in which large interests buy a sizeable position inside a trading range after a downtrend, halting the decline and building the 'cause' for a later markup. It is a hypothesized pattern recognized through characteristic events and phases, not a guaranteed bottom formation.
    Intuition: Smart money fills its shopping cart quietly while everyone else is still bearish; the price stops falling because every selloff gets absorbed.
    Mechanics: An accumulation trading range typically shows preliminary support, a selling climax, an automatic rally, and secondary tests (Phase A); a cause-building stretch of back-and-forth institutional buying (Phase B); a decisive test of remaining supply such as a spring (Phase C); signs of strength with higher lows (Phase D); and departure from the range as demand takes control (Phase E). Volume tends to expand on rallies and contract on reactions as supply is absorbed.
    Failure modes: Ranges that look like accumulation can fail and resolve downward, especially if a spring is not followed by strength or if the broad market is falling; labeling is retrospective and a 'base' can simply be a redistribution range in disguise.
    Misconceptions: Accumulation does not require a spring or any single schematic to match exactly, and a completed-looking accumulation does not guarantee a markup of a particular size.
    Example: After a 40% decline, a stock churns sideways for six months on declining reaction volume; a brief dip below the range low on light volume (spring) is followed by a wide-spread rally (SOS), which the analyst reads as completed accumulation.
    Aliases: accumulation trading range, accumulation schematic

    Wyckoff distribution #

    The phase of the Wyckoff price cycle in which large interests sell (or short) a sizeable position inside a trading range after an uptrend, exhausting demand and building the 'cause' for a later markdown. It is a hypothesized topping structure, not a proven signal that prices must fall.
    Intuition: The insiders quietly unload their holdings onto enthusiastic late buyers; the price stops rising because every rally meets a wall of supply.
    Mechanics: A distribution trading range typically shows preliminary supply and a buying climax, an automatic reaction, and secondary tests (Phase A); a cause-building stretch where institutions are net sellers (Phase B); a test of remaining demand such as an upthrust or UTAD (Phase C); signs of weakness with lower highs and last points of supply (Phase D); and a breakdown as supply takes control (Phase E).
    Failure modes: Apparent distribution can be re-accumulation instead, in which case the range breaks out upward; climactic buying can resume rather than top, and in strong bull markets many labeled 'distribution' ranges simply continue higher.
    Misconceptions: A distribution range does not require an upthrust-after-distribution, and the pattern is often recognizable only in its second half; calling a top in real time is one of the framework's most error-prone applications.
    Example: After a long rally, a stock churns sideways for seven months with volume heavier on down days; a brief pop above the range high fails (UTAD), and a later break of range support on wide spreads marks the start of markdown.
    Aliases: distribution trading range, distribution schematic

    Wyckoff reaccumulation #

    A consolidation within an ongoing uptrend in which large operators take profits and acquire additional shares before the markup resumes. Wyckoff described these flat ranges as 'stepping stones' on the way to higher price targets.
    Intuition: Halfway up the hill, the big players pause to catch their breath and reload, shaking out nervous holders before the next leg higher.
    Mechanics: A reaccumulation range forms as a sideways pause during markup; its Phase A typically lacks the climactic PS/SC of a primary base and resembles distribution more closely, while Phases B-E are shorter and smaller in amplitude but structurally similar to accumulation. Its point-and-figure count often confirms ('stepping stone confirming count') the target projected from the original base.
    Failure modes: Reaccumulation is easily confused with distribution in real time; a 'stepping stone' that fails to break out upward becomes an ordinary top, and counts from small reaccumulation ranges are less reliable than from primary bases.
    Misconceptions: A mid-trend consolidation is not automatically reaccumulation; the distinction from distribution is confirmed only by the direction of the eventual resolution and the price-volume character of the range.
    Example: A stock that doubled off its base trades sideways for eight weeks on falling volume, then breaks out; the analyst's count from this smaller range points to roughly the same target as the original base count, confirming it as reaccumulation.
    Aliases: re-accumulation, stepping stone consolidation

    Wyckoff redistribution #

    A consolidation within an ongoing downtrend in which remaining longs sell and large operators build or add to short positions before the markdown resumes. It mirrors reaccumulation on the downside and is identified only by the range's eventual downward resolution.
    Intuition: During a bear market pause, hopeful buyers step in and the big players sell into that demand, restocking supply for the next leg down.
    Mechanics: A redistribution range forms as a sideways pause during markdown; its Phase A may look like the start of accumulation (climactic action to the downside), but Phases B-E are analyzed like distribution, ending with signs of weakness and a break below range support that signals continuation of the downtrend.
    Failure modes: Redistribution is easily confused with accumulation bottoms in real time; bear-market rallies can extend far beyond the expected pause, and a labeled redistribution that breaks upward invalidates the interpretation.
    Misconceptions: Every pause in a downtrend is not redistribution; without characteristic supply dominance (weak rallies, breakdowns on expanding volume), the range may be a genuine bottoming structure.
    Example: Midway through a bear market, the index churns sideways for a month with volume rising on down days; the break below the consolidation low on expanding volume marks the range as redistribution and the downtrend resumes.
    Aliases: re-distribution

    Preliminary support #

    The Wyckoff event (PS) in which substantial buying first provides pronounced support after a prolonged decline, marked by widening price spread and increased volume that signal the downtrend may be approaching its end. It is an early warning, not a confirmed bottom.
    Intuition: The first time in a long slide that buyers show up in force and noticeably slow the fall.
    Mechanics: During a downtrend, the analyst watches for a down-bar sequence where volume and spread expand but downward progress slows; that buying interest is labeled preliminary support. PS typically precedes the selling climax and begins Phase A of an accumulation trading range.
    Failure modes: A single high-volume support day can be absorbed and the decline can continue; PS says nothing about how much further the market may fall before a final low.
    Misconceptions: Preliminary support is not the bottom itself and does not warrant buying on its own; Wyckoff treats it as the first hint, with confirmation coming from the selling climax and secondary test that follow.
    Example: After a 30% slide, a stock posts its heaviest volume day in months but closes well off the low; the analyst marks the bar as preliminary support and waits for climactic action.
    Aliases: PS

    Automatic rally #

    The Wyckoff event (AR) in which price rebounds sharply after a selling climax because intense selling pressure has been exhausted, fueled by institutional demand and short covering. Its high helps define the upper boundary of the accumulation trading range.
    Intuition: Once the panic sellers are done, even modest buying pops the price back up, like a beach ball released underwater.
    Mechanics: After the selling climax absorbs panicky public selling, the near-absence of offers lets price rise quickly; the AR high, together with the lows of the selling climax and secondary test, sets the boundaries of the trading range that the analyst uses for subsequent labeling.
    Failure modes: The rally is 'automatic' and can fade quickly; its high is not necessarily durable resistance, and in a continuing bear market the AR can be followed by new lows rather than a trading range.
    Misconceptions: The automatic rally is not yet evidence of a new uptrend; it reflects exhaustion of sellers more than the arrival of committed buyers.
    Example: After a panic low at 90 on huge volume, a stock bounces to 102 within days on much lighter selling; the analyst draws the top of the developing accumulation range near 102.

    Secondary test #

    The Wyckoff event (ST) in which price revisits the area of a climax to test the supply/demand balance there. In accumulation, a successful secondary test shows significantly diminished volume and spread as price approaches the selling-climax low, confirming that supply is drying up.
    Intuition: The market dips back toward the panic low to see if any frightened sellers are left; if hardly anyone sells this time, the bottom is more likely real.
    Mechanics: After a selling climax (accumulation) or buying climax (distribution), price returns toward the climax level; the analyst compares volume and spread with the climax. Diminished selling near the climax low confirms support, while an ST that breaks materially below the climax low warns of new lows or prolonged consolidation. Multiple secondary tests are common and occur throughout the range.
    Failure modes: A 'successful' low-volume test can still be followed by a breakdown if the broader market deteriorates, and judging whether volume is 'significantly diminished' is subjective.
    Misconceptions: A secondary test does not have to hold exactly at the climax level, and one successful test does not complete a base; several STs over months are typical.
    Example: Two weeks after a panic low at 90, the stock sinks to 91 on one-third of the earlier volume and closes mid-range; the analyst logs a successful secondary test confirming Phase A.

    Spring #

    The Wyckoff event in which price briefly breaks below the support of an accumulation trading range, then quickly reverses back into the range, trapping late sellers while large interests acquire remaining shares at bargain prices. It is a hypothesized bear-trap event whose bullish interpretation must be confirmed by subsequent strength; it is not a reliable standalone buy signal.
    Intuition: A fake breakdown: price dips under support just far enough to scare out weak hands and trigger stops, then snaps right back.
    Mechanics: Late in an accumulation range (Phase C), price pushes below the range low; the analyst watches volume and the speed of recovery. A low-volume spring that reverses and closes back inside the range indicates exhausted supply and is treated as a high-probability long entry, ideally validated by a following test and a sign of strength.
    Failure modes: Not every dip below range support is a spring; if heavy selling continues after the break, the 'spring' was a genuine breakdown and the range interpretation fails. Springs do not occur in every accumulation structure.
    Misconceptions: A spring is not confirmed at the moment of the low; Wyckoff treats the low-volume recovery and subsequent test or SOS as the confirmation, and springs are optional, not required, elements of accumulation.
    Example: A stock ranges between 90 and 102 for months, then spikes to 87 on light volume and closes at 91 the same day; the next week it rallies on widening spread, validating the spring.
    Aliases: bear trap, shakeout (minor)

    Terminal shakeout #

    A large, decisive version of a spring occurring at the end of an accumulation trading range, in which price breaks sharply below support and rapidly recovers, forcing the final transfer of shares from weak holders to large operators. The tutorial describes it as 'a spring on steroids'.
    Intuition: The last violent flush that scares out everyone still holding, right before the real move up begins.
    Mechanics: Like a spring, a terminal shakeout takes price below the range low late in the range, but with a deeper or more dramatic break and a fast, forceful recovery; a subsequent low-volume test of the shakeout low confirms that supply has been exhausted and the stock is ready to be marked up.
    Failure modes: A deep break that fails to recover quickly is a genuine breakdown, not a shakeout; the label is only validated by the speed of recovery and a successful low-volume test afterward.
    Misconceptions: Terminal shakeouts are not required for accumulation and are not identifiable in real time with certainty; many apparent shakeouts turn out to be the start of further declines.
    Example: Near the end of a four-month base, a stock plunges 12% under the range low in two days on heavy volume, then reclaims the range within a week; a quiet retest on shrinking volume completes the terminal shakeout.
    Aliases: shakeout, TSO

    Test of spring #

    The Wyckoff event in which price revisits the low of a spring or shakeout to verify that supply is exhausted. A successful test makes a higher low (or holds near the spring low) on clearly lower volume, indicating the stock is ready for markup.
    Intuition: After the fake breakdown, the market dips once more to double-check that no sellers remain; a quiet, low-volume dip passes the check.
    Mechanics: Large operators repeatedly test the market for supply; after a spring, one or more tests revisit the spring low. The analyst compares volume and spread with the spring: a successful test shows significantly less volume and holds at or above the spring low, and is treated as a confirmation to initiate or add to long positions.
    Failure modes: A test on volume that is not meaningfully lower, or one that undercuts the spring low decisively, negates the bullish reading; tests can also be repeated many times, delaying or preventing markup.
    Misconceptions: The test does not need to exactly match the spring low, and a single successful test is not an absolute guarantee; it is one piece of evidence weighed with the sign of strength that should follow.
    Example: After a spring to 87, a stock drifts back to 89 on the lightest volume in months and closes unchanged; the trader buys a partial position, treating it as a successful test of the spring.
    Aliases: test of shakeout, supply test

    Sign of strength #

    The Wyckoff event (SOS) in which price advances on widening spread and relatively higher volume, showing demand in control; after accumulation, it often validates the preceding spring or test and typically carries price to or through the top of the trading range.
    Intuition: The first rally with real conviction behind it, proving buyers have taken the wheel.
    Mechanics: The analyst looks for rallies whose price bars widen in spread with expanding volume, in contrast to the narrowing, low-volume reactions of the range. An SOS following a spring confirms the bullish reading; a decisive SOS that crosses the range's resistance is called a 'jump across the creek'.
    Failure modes: A single strong bar can be an upthrust or a news-driven spike rather than genuine demand; an SOS that fails to hold its gains and falls back into the range negates the confirmation.
    Misconceptions: An SOS is not simply 'a big green bar'; its meaning comes from context — appearing after accumulation events and being followed by shallow, low-volume reactions (LPSs).
    Example: Following a spring at 87, a stock rallies from 91 to 104 on expanding volume and wide daily ranges, punching through the range top; the analyst labels the move an SOS and looks for a low-volume pullback to enter.
    Aliases: SOS, jump across the creek (JAC)

    Last point of support #

    The Wyckoff event (LPS) marking the low of a reaction or pullback after a sign of strength, typically a retreat to former resistance-turned-support on diminished spread and volume. It is regarded as a low-risk area to initiate or add to long positions before markup.
    Intuition: After the breakout rally, the market takes one last quiet step back to the old ceiling, now a floor — the final boarding call for longs.
    Mechanics: Once an SOS demonstrates demand, subsequent pullbacks are monitored: an LPS holds at or above prior resistance with narrowing spread and shrinking volume, indicating supply is exhausted. Despite the singular name, several LPSs can occur; Wyckoff's point-and-figure counts are conventionally anchored at the LPS level.
    Failure modes: A pullback labeled LPS can fail and re-enter the trading range, invalidating the markup scenario; distinguishing a healthy LPS from the start of a reversal is only possible as subsequent action unfolds.
    Misconceptions: The LPS is not necessarily the last pullback ever, nor must it touch the exact old resistance; it is a zone defined by diminished volume and spread, not a precise price.
    Example: After jumping above its 102 range top, a stock pulls back to 103 on half the breakout volume; the trader buys there with a stop below the old range, treating the dip as the LPS.
    Aliases: LPS

    Backup to the edge of the creek #

    The Wyckoff event (BU) in which price pulls back toward the resistance it just crossed with a sign of strength, combining profit-taking with a test for remaining supply around the old resistance area. The term comes from Robert Evans's creek metaphor and precedes a more substantial markup.
    Intuition: After leaping over the creek of resistance, the market walks back to the water's edge to make sure the far bank is solid before marching on.
    Mechanics: Following a jump across the creek (SOS through resistance), price retreats on narrowing spread and lower volume toward the breakout area; a successful back-up holds above or near the old resistance and is treated as an entry or add-on point. A back-up can take several forms, from a simple pullback to a small new trading range at a higher level.
    Failure modes: A back-up that sinks decisively back into the trading range on expanding volume shows the breakout lacked sponsorship and often precedes a return to the range or a breakdown.
    Misconceptions: A back-up is not merely any dip after a breakout; the Wyckoff reading depends on diminished volume and spread during the retreat and on the creek level acting as support.
    Example: A stock jumps from 100 to 110 through range resistance, then drifts back to 104 on drying volume over five sessions; the analyst treats the drift as a back-up and adds to the long.
    Aliases: back-up, BU, back up to the creek, BUEC

    Preliminary supply #

    The Wyckoff event (PSY) in which large interests begin unloading shares in quantity after a pronounced advance, marked by expanding volume and widening price spread that signal a change of trend may be approaching. It mirrors preliminary support at market tops.
    Intuition: The first time in a long climb that heavy selling shows up and noticeably slows the rise.
    Mechanics: During an uptrend, the analyst watches for bars where volume and spread expand but upward progress stalls; that selling interest is labeled preliminary supply. PSY typically precedes the buying climax and begins Phase A of a distribution trading range.
    Failure modes: A single heavy-volume stall day can be absorbed and the advance can continue; PSY warns of possible topping but gives no information about how much further the rally may extend.
    Misconceptions: Preliminary supply is not the top and does not warrant shorting by itself; confirmation requires the buying climax, automatic reaction, and secondary tests that follow.
    Example: After doubling in a year, a stock posts record volume on a wide-range day that closes near unchanged; the analyst marks it as preliminary supply and watches for climactic buying.
    Aliases: PSY

    Automatic reaction #

    The Wyckoff event (AR) in distribution in which price falls sharply after a buying climax because intense buying has been exhausted while heavy supply continues. Its low helps define the lower boundary of the distribution trading range.
    Intuition: Once the euphoric buyers are spent, the price drops on its own weight — no extra selling pressure needed.
    Mechanics: After the buying climax fills urgent public demand, the withdrawal of bids lets price decline quickly; the AR low, together with the climax high and subsequent secondary tests, sets the boundaries of the distribution range that the analyst uses for later labeling.
    Failure modes: The reaction is 'automatic' and can be brief; in a continuing bull market the dip is bought and the advance resumes, so an AR alone does not establish a top.
    Misconceptions: The automatic reaction is not yet proof of distribution; it reflects exhausted buyers more than committed sellers, and its significance comes only from the range that develops around it.
    Example: After a climax spike to 150 on record volume, a stock sinks to 136 within days on much lighter buying; the analyst draws the bottom of the developing distribution range near 136.
    Aliases: AR

    Upthrust #

    The Wyckoff event (UT) in which price moves above the resistance of a trading range and then quickly reverses to close back inside the range, acting as a test of remaining demand and a trap for uninformed breakout buyers. It is the mirror image of a spring.
    Intuition: A fake breakout: price pops above resistance just long enough to hook breakout traders, then falls back into the range.
    Mechanics: Price pushes above the resistance established by a buying climax or prior secondary tests, then reverses and closes below resistance. In Wyckoff analysis this shows large interests selling to breakout buyers; after an upthrust, price often tests the lower boundary of the range, and an upthrust occurring in Phase C of a distribution range is a candidate short area.
    Failure modes: Not every poke above resistance is an upthrust; if demand is genuine the breakout holds and becomes a jump to a higher range. Upthrusts also occur repeatedly in Phase B of ranges without any bearish resolution.
    Misconceptions: An upthrust is not confirmed at its high; the bearish reading depends on the failure back into the range and subsequent weakness, and aggressive shorting at the first upthrust is repeatedly stopped out, as Wyckoff literature itself warns.
    Example: A stock ranging between 136 and 150 spikes to 153 intraday but closes at 148; two weeks later it slides to the range bottom, completing the upthrust pattern.

    Upthrust after distribution #

    The Wyckoff event (UTAD) occurring in the latter stages of a distribution trading range, in which price breaks above range resistance and then fails, providing a definitive test of new demand before markdown. It is the distributional counterpart of the spring and terminal shakeout in accumulation.
    Intuition: One last fake breakout at the top of a topping range, engineered to squeeze shorts and unload remaining stock onto breakout buyers before the decline.
    Mechanics: Late in distribution (Phase C), price breaks above the range high, then reverses back into the range. The UTAD tests whether sufficient demand remains to support higher prices; its failure confirms supply dominance and often precedes the sign of weakness and markdown. Like springs, a UTAD is not a required element of distribution.
    Failure modes: A UTAD that holds above the range and is followed by strength invalidates the distribution reading; because it looks identical to a genuine breakout at first, shorting it early carries real squeeze risk.
    Misconceptions: Distribution does not require a UTAD — the canonical schematics include distribution ranges with and without one — and a UTAD is not necessarily the range's absolute high.
    Example: After five months of distribution between 136 and 150, a stock breaks to 155 for two days, then collapses back to 145 on heavy volume; the failed breakout is labeled a UTAD before the markdown begins.

    Sign of weakness #

    The Wyckoff event (SOW) in which price declines to or slightly below the lower boundary of a trading range on increased spread and volume, showing supply in control. Together with the automatic reaction, initial SOWs mark the change of character that precedes markdown.
    Intuition: The first selloff with real conviction, proving sellers have taken control.
    Mechanics: The analyst looks for down-moves whose bars widen in spread with expanding volume, breaking toward or through range support. A major SOW that decisively breaks the ice is often retested by a feeble rally that fails at or near the old support, offering a short entry before markdown resumes.
    Failure modes: A single wide-range down bar can be a shakeout or news shock rather than genuine supply; an SOW that is quickly recovered on strength negates the bearish confirmation.
    Misconceptions: An SOW is not merely 'a big red bar'; its meaning comes from context — appearing after distribution events and being followed by feeble, low-volume rallies (LPSYs).
    Example: After a UTAD fails, a stock drops from 148 to 134 through its range floor on the heaviest volume in months; a weak bounce to 138 fails, confirming the SOW before markdown.

    Last point of supply #

    The Wyckoff event (LPSY) in which a feeble rally on narrow spread follows a sign of weakness, showing the market's inability to advance because of weak demand, substantial supply, or both. LPSYs represent the final waves of large operators' distribution and preferred areas for short entries before markdown.
    Intuition: After support breaks, the market stages one last tired bounce — the final chance to get out or get short before the drop.
    Mechanics: Following a SOW, rallies are monitored for narrow spread and low volume, indicating exhausted demand; such rallies that fail at or below the broken support (ice) are labeled LPSYs. Despite the singular name, multiple LPSYs can occur during Phase D of distribution.
    Failure modes: A rally labeled LPSY can regain strength and climb back into or above the range, invalidating the markdown scenario; the label is confirmed only by subsequent weakness.
    Misconceptions: The LPSY is not necessarily the last rally ever, nor a precise price; it is a zone identified by narrow spread and poor volume on the rally, not by an exact level.
    Example: After breaking support at 136, a stock bounces to 138 over four days on shrinking volume and small ranges; the trader shorts there with a stop above the ice, treating the bounce as an LPSY.

    Wyckoff Phase A #

    The first phase of a Wyckoff trading range, marking the stopping of the prior trend. In accumulation it contains preliminary support, the selling climax, the automatic rally, and secondary tests; in distribution it contains preliminary supply, the buying climax, the automatic reaction, and secondary tests.
    Intuition: The moment the old trend finally runs out of gas — panic at bottoms, euphoria at tops — and the sideways battle begins.
    Mechanics: Phase A transfers large volumes of shares between the public and professional interests through climactic price and volume action. The highs and lows formed during Phase A (climax and automatic move) set the boundaries of the trading range used in all subsequent analysis.
    Failure modes: Trends can stop without climactic action, making Phase A hard to recognize; and climactic action can be followed by trend continuation rather than a range, so Phase A labeling is provisional until a range develops.
    Misconceptions: Phase A does not require a dramatic climax — Wyckoff notes trends can end quietly with decreasing spread and volume — and a climax alone does not guarantee a reversal.
    Example: A year-long slide ends with a high-volume support day (PS), a panic flush (SC), a sharp bounce (AR), and a quiet retest (ST); the analyst marks these four events as Phase A and draws range boundaries at the AR high and SC low.

    Wyckoff Phase B #

    The second phase of a Wyckoff trading range, in which institutions build a 'cause' for the next trend by accumulating (or distributing) inventory over an extended period, sometimes a year or more. Its characteristic back-and-forth price action reflects large operators buying low and checking rallies (or the reverse in distribution).
    Intuition: The long, boring middle of the range where the big players quietly load or unload the truck, one oscillation at a time.
    Mechanics: Within the Phase A boundaries, price swings repeatedly between support and resistance. Early swings are wide with high volume; as professionals absorb supply (accumulation) or demand (distribution), volume on counter-swings diminishes. Multiple secondary tests and upthrust-type actions occur, and the aggregate horizontal extent of Phase B largely determines the point-and-figure count and hence the price objective.
    Failure modes: Phase B can extend far longer than expected, exhausting the analyst's patience and capital; the range can also resolve opposite to the presumed accumulation or distribution reading.
    Misconceptions: Phase B is not dead time to be traded aggressively in both directions; its function is cause-building, and the balance of volume on rallies versus reactions — not the day-to-day noise — carries the analytical information.
    Example: For nine months a stock oscillates between 90 and 102; volume on declines shrinks steadily while rallies hold their gains, and the analyst reads Phase B accumulation and measures a 20-column P&F count.

    Wyckoff Phase C #

    The third phase of a Wyckoff trading range, in which price undergoes a decisive test of remaining supply (accumulation) or demand (distribution), allowing large operators to judge whether the market is ready to leave the range. Springs, shakeouts, upthrusts, and UTADs are the characteristic Phase C events.
    Intuition: The final exam: one engineered fake move probes whether any sellers (at bottoms) or buyers (at tops) are still out there.
    Mechanics: In accumulation, a spring or shakeout below range support tests supply; a low-volume break that quickly recovers indicates readiness for markup and offers a high-probability long entry. In distribution, an upthrust or UTAD above range resistance tests demand; its failure indicates readiness for markdown. When testing occurs higher in the range without a spring, Phase C identification is more difficult.
    Failure modes: The Phase C test can fail — a spring that keeps falling or an upthrust that keeps rising invalidates the range reading — and ranges without a clear test make Phase C easy to mislabel in real time.
    Misconceptions: Phase C does not require a spring or UTAD; these are optional, and forcing the label onto every minor penetration of range boundaries is a common misreading.
    Example: A stock dips under its six-month base on the lightest volume of the range and closes back inside it the same day (spring); the analyst declares Phase C and buys a starter position.

    Wyckoff Phase D #

    The fourth phase of a Wyckoff trading range, in which demand (accumulation) or supply (distribution) becomes consistently dominant: advances widen in spread and volume while reactions shrink, carrying price at least to the range boundary. LPSs and LPSYs in this phase are the framework's preferred position-entry areas.
    Intuition: The tug-of-war ends and one side visibly wins — rallies get stronger and pullbacks weaker (or vice versa) as price presses toward the range edge.
    Mechanics: The analyst verifies the Phase C conclusion by observing the character of subsequent swings: in accumulation, signs of strength on widening spread and volume alternate with shallow, low-volume reactions (LPSs) as price moves to or through the top of the range; in distribution, signs of weakness alternate with feeble rallies (LPSYs) as price moves to or through support.
    Failure modes: The expected dominance can fail to materialize — price returns to mid-range and the phase labels must be revised; entering on an LPS/LPSY assumes Phase D is underway, which is only confirmed by later action.
    Misconceptions: Phase D is not the same as the trend itself; the decisive exit from the range belongs to Phase E, and treating every approach to the range edge as a breakout invites losses on failed moves.
    Example: After a spring, a stock makes two widening-spread rallies to the top of its range, each followed by shallow low-volume dips; the analyst buys the second dip (LPS) anticipating Phase E markup.
