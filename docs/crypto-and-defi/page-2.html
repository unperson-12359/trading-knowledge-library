    Decentralized exchange #

    A decentralized exchange (DEX) is a trading venue implemented as smart contracts — usually an automated market maker — that lets users swap tokens directly from their own wallets without depositing funds with an intermediary.
    Intuition: A DEX is a currency-exchange kiosk run by code: open 24/7 to anyone, and your money never leaves your pocket until the exact moment of the swap.
    Mechanics: Traders interact directly with on-chain liquidity pools (e.g. Uniswap) whose smart contracts price and settle swaps atomically in a single transaction; liquidity is supplied by other users earning fees. DEXs are permissionless — anyone can trade, provide liquidity, or list a market — and non-custodial, unlike order-book venues that hold user deposits.
    Failure modes: Public mempool exposure invites sandwich attacks and worse execution than quoted; shallow pools give huge price impact; unaudited or malicious token contracts and rug-pull pools are common because listing is permissionless.
    Misconceptions: DEX prices are not set by an order book of other people's bids/asks (in AMM designs) but by pool ratios, and they track external markets only through arbitrageurs. 'Decentralized' does not mean risk-free — smart contract exploits and MEV are native hazards.
    Example: Swapping 1 ETH for USDC on Uniswap executes entirely within one Ethereum transaction against the ETH/USDC pool: you sign the swap, the pool contract computes the output via x·y=k, and the USDC lands directly in your wallet.
    Aliases: DEX

    Centralized exchange #

    A centralized exchange (CEX) is a trading platform operated by a company that takes custody of user deposits, runs an off-chain central limit order book to match buyers and sellers, and settles trades on its internal ledger.
    Intuition: A CEX is a bank crossed with a stock exchange: fast and familiar, but you are trusting the operator with your money the whole time.
    Mechanics: Users deposit crypto or fiat into exchange-controlled wallets; the exchange matches orders in its own matching engine and updates internal balances, only touching the blockchain on deposits and withdrawals. Most run central limit order books with permissioned access (KYC), and many offer margin, futures and staking services on the same custodial rails.
    Failure modes: Custodial risk dominates: hacks, insolvency or fraud (e.g. FTX) can wipe out deposited balances that are only IOUs on the exchange's ledger; withdrawal freezes, opaque rehypothecation and single-venue price manipulation are structural hazards.
    Misconceptions: Trades on a CEX are not blockchain transactions — they are database entries, which is why they are instant and cheap but also why proof-of-reserves matters. 'Your' balance on a CEX is a claim on the exchange, not on-chain ownership.
    Example: Depositing 2 BTC to Binance and selling it updates only Binance's internal ledger; the on-chain BTC stays in Binance's omnibus wallet until you withdraw, so if the exchange freezes withdrawals your claim is stuck with it.
    Aliases: CEX, custodial exchange

    Automated market maker #

    An automated market maker (AMM) is a decentralized exchange design that replaces the order book with smart-contract liquidity pools: traders swap against pooled reserves and prices are set algorithmically by a pricing function of those reserves.
    Intuition: Instead of matching your buy order with someone's sell order, the AMM is a robot counterparty that always quotes a price from the contents of its vault.
    Mechanics: Liquidity providers deposit token pairs into a pool contract; each swap trades against the reserves and moves the price according to the pool's invariant (e.g. the constant-product rule x·y=k in Uniswap v2). Arbitrageurs align pool prices with external markets, and LPs earn a fee on each swap proportional to their share of the pool.
    Failure modes: AMM prices only track external markets through arbitrage, so thin or manipulated pools can trade far from fair value; LPs bear impermanent loss and toxic-flow losses that can exceed fee income.
    Misconceptions: AMMs do not 'discover' prices independently — they follow prices set elsewhere via arbitrage. Providing liquidity is not passive yield; it is an active short-volatility position.
    Example: In a Uniswap ETH/USDC pool, a trader buying ETH with USDC adds USDC to the pool and removes ETH, automatically raising the pool's ETH price; an arbitrageur then sells ETH back into the pool until its price matches centralized exchanges.
    Aliases: AMM, constant function market maker, CFMM

    Constant-product market maker #

    A constant-product market maker (CPMM) is the canonical AMM pricing rule in which the product of a pool's two token reserves must remain constant across trades (x·y=k), so the marginal price is the ratio of reserves and each trade moves the price along a hyperbola.
    Intuition: The pool is a balanced scale: remove some of one token and the other must grow so their product never shrinks — which is why big orders get progressively worse prices.
    Mechanics: A swap of Δx of token X for token Y must satisfy (x+Δx)(y−Δy)=k (before fees), giving output Δy = y·Δx/(x+Δx). The instantaneous price is y/x, so price impact grows quadratically with trade size relative to depth; a fee (e.g. 0.3% in Uniswap v2) is taken from the input and accrues to LPs, making k grow slightly per trade.
    Failure modes: The full-range design spreads liquidity from 0 to ∞, so most capital sits unused and large trades suffer severe price impact in thin pools; when one token trends, arbitrage continuously extracts value from LPs (impermanent loss).
    Misconceptions: The invariant does not keep k exactly constant — fees make k grow, and k changes whenever liquidity is added or removed. x·y=k also does not prevent LPs from losing money to price divergence.
    Example: A pool holds 10 ETH and 40,000 USDC (k=400,000, price 4,000 USDC/ETH). Buying 1 ETH requires paying 40,000×1/9 ≈ 4,444 USDC — about 11% above the quoted price, illustrating price impact at 10% of pool depth.
    x · y = k, where x, y are token reserves and k is the invariant; output for input Δx: Δy = y·Δx / (x + Δx) (ignoring fees).
    Aliases: CPMM, x*y=k, constant product formula

    Concentrated liquidity #

    Concentrated liquidity is the Uniswap v3/v4 mechanism that lets liquidity providers allocate capital to a custom price range instead of uniformly across all prices, so the same capital provides deeper liquidity (and earns more fees) where trading actually occurs.
    Intuition: Rather than spreading butter thinly from price 0 to infinity, LPs spread it thickly on the slice of the curve where the market actually trades.
    Mechanics: Price space is partitioned into discrete ticks (~0.01% apart); an LP position is defined by a lower and upper tick and only earns fees while the current price is inside its range. Within a range the pool behaves like a bounded constant-product curve; when price exits the range, the position holds 100% of the cheaper token and earns nothing until price re-enters.
    Failure modes: Out-of-range positions earn zero fees and hold entirely the depreciated asset, amplifying impermanent loss versus full-range liquidity; concentrated positions demand active management, and naive 'set and forget' ranges systematically underperform.
    Misconceptions: Higher capital efficiency is not free yield — it is higher leverage on the same LP risk, so impermanent loss per unit of capital is larger, not smaller. Concentrated liquidity does not eliminate the constant-product math; it bounds where it applies.
    Example: On Uniswap v2's DAI/USDC pool, only ~0.5% of capital served trades between $0.99 and $1.01; a v3 LP allocating $10,000 entirely to the 0.99–1.01 range earns the same fees on that volume as roughly $2M of v2-style liquidity.
    Aliases: CL, ranged liquidity

    Liquidity pool #

    A liquidity pool is a smart contract holding reserves of two (or more) tokens that an AMM uses as the counterparty for swaps; traders trade against the pooled reserves and liquidity providers fund them in exchange for a share of fees.
    Intuition: It is a communal vault of token pairs: everyone chips in inventory, the vault quotes prices from its contents, and depositors split the toll every trader pays.
    Mechanics: Providers deposit proportional amounts of both tokens and receive pool shares (LP tokens in v2, NFT positions in v3) representing their pro-rata claim on reserves plus accrued fees. Swaps move the reserve ratio, repricing the pair via the pool's invariant (e.g. x·y=k), and a per-swap fee (e.g. 0.3%) accrues to the pool.
    Failure modes: Pools for scam or exploited tokens can be drained or become worthless, and permissionless listing means anyone can create deceptive pools; thin pools give extreme slippage and are easy to manipulate for oracle attacks.
    Misconceptions: Pool depth (TVL) is not a guarantee of fair pricing — prices still track external markets only via arbitrage. Depositing into a pool is not like depositing into a savings account; the asset mix you withdraw is usually different from what you put in.
    Example: A Uniswap ETH/USDC pool holding 10,000 ETH and 40M USDC lets a trader swap 100,000 USDC for ~24.9 ETH instantly; the 0.3% fee (~300 USDC) stays in the pool, enriching all LP shares.
    Aliases: LP pool, pool

    DeFi liquidity provider #

    A DeFi liquidity provider (LP) is a user who deposits tokens into an AMM liquidity pool (or lending pool) and in return holds a pro-rata claim on the pool's reserves and the fees those reserves generate.
    Intuition: LPs are the inventory suppliers of on-chain markets: they rent out their tokens to the pool and get paid a cut of every trade — while silently selling winners and buying losers as prices move.
    Mechanics: In Uniswap v2, depositing equal values of two tokens mints ERC-20 LP tokens tracking the provider's pool share, redeemable for reserves plus fees; in v3, LPs choose price ranges and receive NFT positions. LP returns equal trading fees plus incentive rewards minus impermanent loss from price divergence.
    Failure modes: Impermanent loss in trending markets can exceed fee income, leaving LPs worse off than holding; LP positions also inherit smart contract risk, and v3 ranges can drift out-of-range and earn nothing.
    Misconceptions: LP'ing is not risk-free yield — it is a short-volatility position whose PnL = fees + rewards − impermanent loss. High APRs quoted from incentive tokens usually decay as more liquidity joins and token emissions fall.
    Example: An LP supplying $5,000 of ETH and $5,000 of USDC to a 0.3%-fee pool with $1M daily volume and $10M TVL earns roughly 0.3%×$1M×(10k/10M) ≈ $3/day before considering impermanent loss.
    Aliases: LP, liquidity provider

    Impermanent loss #

    Impermanent loss is the difference in value between depositing tokens into an AMM liquidity pool and simply holding those same tokens: as relative prices change, arbitrage rebalances the pool so the LP ends up with less of the appreciating asset and more of the depreciating one.
    Intuition: The pool automatically sells your winners and buys your losers; if prices snap back the 'loss' vanishes — but if you withdraw while prices have diverged, it becomes real.
    Mechanics: Under x·y=k, when the external price of one token rises, arbitrageurs buy it from the pool until the internal price matches, changing the reserve ratio. For a relative price change of factor r, IL = 2√r/(1+r) − 1 vs holding (e.g. r=2 → −5.7%, r=5 → −25.5%), independent of direction. Fees and incentives may or may not offset it.
    Failure modes: IL grows non-linearly with divergence, so volatile or trending pairs can leave LPs deep underwater versus holding even after fees; concentrated liquidity amplifies IL per unit of capital when price leaves the range.
    Misconceptions: 'Impermanent' does not mean harmless — the loss is permanent the moment you withdraw at a diverged price, and the original price often never returns. Fee income is not guaranteed to cover IL; for many pairs and periods it does not.
    Example: Deposit 1 ETH + 1,000 USDC when ETH = $1,000 (pool: 10 ETH + 10,000 USDC, you own 10%). ETH doubles to $2,000; arbitrage leaves the pool at 7.071 ETH + 14,142 USDC. Your 10% withdrawal is worth $2,828 vs $3,000 for holding — a 5.7% impermanent loss.
    IL(r) = 2√r / (1 + r) − 1, where r is the ratio of the new relative price to the price at deposit.
    Aliases: IL, divergence loss

    Slippage tolerance #

    Slippage tolerance is the maximum deviation a user accepts between the expected price of an on-chain swap and the price it actually executes at; if execution would exceed this bound, the transaction reverts.
    Intuition: It is the 'don't fill me worse than X' dial on a DEX trade — your insurance against the price moving between signing and execution.
    Mechanics: Price slippage is the gap between the quoted price when a swap is submitted and the realized execution price, caused by other trades landing first and by the trade's own price impact. Wallets/routers encode slippage tolerance as a minimum output amount; if the pool cannot deliver at least that much, the transaction fails (and only gas is lost).
    Failure modes: Setting tolerance too high invites sandwich attackers to push execution to the worst allowed price; too low, and transactions revert repeatedly in volatile markets, wasting gas without executing.
    Misconceptions: Slippage tolerance does not reduce price impact — it only caps the damage you accept; the trade's own impact is computed by the AMM regardless. A failed, reverted swap still costs gas.
    Example: Swapping 50,000 USDC for ETH quoted at 12.5 ETH with 0.5% slippage tolerance sets a minimum of 12.4375 ETH; if a sandwich bot moves the pool so you'd only get 12.3 ETH, the transaction reverts instead of filling.
    Aliases: max slippage

    Price impact in AMMs #

    Price impact is the change in a pool's token price caused by a trader's own swap: under the constant-product rule, larger trades relative to pool depth execute at progressively worse prices, and the impact is deterministic given pool reserves.
    Intuition: It is how much your own order moves the market against you — the bigger the bite you take from the pool, the worse the price of the last bite.
    Mechanics: In a CPMM, output is Δy = y·Δx/(x+Δx), so average execution price worsens as Δx grows relative to reserve x; price impact ≈ Δx/(x+Δx) for small trades, growing quadratically. Unlike slippage (which includes other trades landing first), price impact is caused solely by your own trade and is shown by DEX interfaces before signing.
    Failure modes: In thin pools even modest trades move prices several percent, and a single large swap can be exploited as a temporary oracle price for attacks on dependent protocols; splitting orders reduces but cannot eliminate impact.
    Misconceptions: Price impact is not a fee — it is an execution cost paid through a worse exchange rate, and it is not the same as slippage, which also includes price movement from other actors before your trade confirms.
    Example: A pool holds 10 ETH and 40,000 USDC (spot 4,000). A 10,000 USDC buy (25% of USDC depth) returns 2 ETH at an average 5,000 USDC/ETH — a 25% price impact, versus ~0% impact for a tiny trade.
    For x·y=k, output Δy = y·Δx / (x + Δx); impact ≈ 1 − (execution price / spot price) = Δx / (x + Δx).
    Aliases: market impact (AMM)

    Sandwich attack #

    A sandwich attack is an MEV strategy in which an attacker sees a victim's pending DEX trade in the mempool, inserts a buy immediately before it (front-run) and a sell immediately after it (back-run), profiting from the price movement the victim's own trade creates.
    Intuition: A predator spots your big market order in the waiting room, jumps the queue to buy ahead of you, lets your purchase push the price up, then dumps on you — you become their exit liquidity.
    Mechanics: The searcher computes the victim trade's price impact, front-runs with a buy that raises the price, lets the victim's trade execute at a worse rate (bounded only by their slippage tolerance), then back-runs with a sell at the elevated price. The profit comes directly from the victim's worse execution, net of gas and bribes to builders for ordering.
    Failure modes: Sandwiches are not atomic, so the attacker bears inventory risk if the back-run fails or another bot interferes (e.g. 'salmonella' counter-attacks); tight victim slippage settings can make the attack unprofitable.
    Misconceptions: Sandwiching is not hacking the victim's wallet — it exploits the public ordering of otherwise valid transactions. High slippage tolerance is the attacker's margin; private transaction relays mitigate but centralize order flow.
    Example: A user buys 10,000 UNI with DAI on Uniswap with 5% slippage; a bot front-runs a UNI buy, the user's trade executes near the worst allowed price, and the bot's immediate sell extracts the difference — the user receives materially fewer UNI than quoted.
    Aliases: sandwiching, sandwich MEV

    Blockchain front-running #

    Blockchain front-running is the practice of observing a profitable pending transaction (in the public mempool or via privileged ordering access) and inserting one's own transaction ahead of it to capture the opportunity first.
    Intuition: It is the on-chain version of a broker trading ahead of a client's order — except the order flow is broadcast publicly to everyone, so bots do it at machine speed.
    Mechanics: Because pending transactions are visible before inclusion, bots monitor the mempool, simulate transactions, and copy or preempt profitable ones, winning priority through higher gas bids (priority gas auctions) or by paying block builders via private relays (e.g. Flashbots). Generalized front-runners even clone arbitrary profitable transactions with their own address substituted.
    Failure modes: Front-running gas auctions historically congested the whole network and raised fees for everyone; extreme incentives to capture large MEV can tempt block producers to reorder or reorg blocks, destabilizing consensus.
    Misconceptions: Front-running is not unique to insiders — on public chains anyone can do it, which is why it is an arms race won by latency and gas-bidding infrastructure. Using a private relay hides your trade from the public mempool but not from the relay/builder.
    Example: A bot sees a pending transaction buying a mispriced NFT for 1 ETH worth 5 ETH, resubmits the identical purchase with its own address and a higher gas tip, and wins the listing before the original buyer's transaction executes.
    Aliases: frontrunning, transaction front-running

    Back-running #

    Back-running is an MEV strategy in which a bot places its transaction immediately after a target transaction in the same block, profiting from the state change the target creates — most commonly arbitraging a price dislocation right after a large trade or an oracle update.
    Intuition: Instead of jumping the queue, the bot waits for your transaction to move the market, then cleans up the mispricing you left behind in the very same block.
    Mechanics: Searchers simulate the post-state of pending transactions (e.g. a large DEX swap that unbalances a pool, or a liquidation event) and submit a transaction that must execute directly after — buying the now-cheap asset on one venue and selling on another atomically. Placement is bought via gas bidding or bundle submission to block builders; the second leg of a sandwich attack is a back-run.
    Failure modes: Competition is so intense that most profit is bid away to validators as gas/bribes (searchers may pay >90% of MEV revenue in fees); failed back-runs still cost gas, and racing bots can make the trade revert.
    Misconceptions: Back-running is not parasitic in all cases — DEX arbitrage back-runs are what keep AMM prices aligned with external markets, a service the ecosystem depends on; the harmful variant is when it extracts value from users (e.g. sandwiches).
    Example: A 1,000 ETH sell on Uniswap pushes ETH's pool price 2% below Binance; a back-running bot buys ETH in the same block right after the sell and offloads it on a centralized exchange, pocketing the spread minus fees.
    Aliases: backrunning

    Maximal extractable value #

    Maximal extractable value (MEV) is the maximum value that can be extracted from block production — beyond the standard block reward and gas fees — by including, excluding, or reordering transactions within a block.
    Intuition: Whoever decides the order of transactions in a block holds a printing press: they can see the future of the block and sell positions in it.
    Mechanics: Validators (formerly miners, hence the older term 'miner extractable value') control inclusion and ordering; in practice independent 'searchers' find opportunities — DEX arbitrage, liquidations, sandwich attacks — and capture them via gas bidding or sealed bundles (Flashbots/MEV-Boost), sharing most proceeds with validators through builder auctions (proposer-builder separation).
    Failure modes: Unchecked MEV degrades user execution (sandwiches), congests the network via gas auctions, and — when MEV in a block exceeds the block reward — creates incentives for time-bandit reorgs that threaten consensus stability; private order flow and builder concentration centralize the network.
    Misconceptions: MEV is not inherently theft or purely bad — arbitrage and liquidations keep prices consistent and lending protocols solvent. Nor is it eliminable by protocol upgrades alone; it is an intrinsic property of ordering power, only redistributable or mitigated.
    Example: A searcher spots ETH/DAI priced differently on Uniswap vs Sushiswap and turns 1,000 ETH into 1,045 ETH in one atomic transaction, paying most of the 45 ETH profit to the validator as a bribe for guaranteed inclusion.
    Aliases: MEV, miner extractable value

    Flash loan #

    A flash loan is an uncollateralized loan that must be borrowed and repaid (plus fee) within a single blockchain transaction; if repayment does not complete, the entire transaction reverts as if the loan never happened.
    Intuition: It is a loan that exists only for the duration of one atomic operation — millions of dollars, no collateral, but you must hand it back before the transaction closes or everything unwinds.
    Mechanics: The borrower contract calls the pool (e.g. Aave's flashLoanSimple), receives the funds, executes arbitrary logic (arbitrage, liquidation, collateral swap), and the pool pulls back principal plus fee (~0.05% on Aave) at the end of the same transaction; failure to approve repayment reverts all state changes. Atomicity makes the loan risk-free for the lender.
    Failure modes: Flash loans supercharge exploits: attackers borrow huge capital to manipulate thin DEX prices or oracle feeds, drain vulnerable protocols, and repay in the same transaction — the loan is a tool, and most famous 'flash loan attacks' exploited other contracts' logic, not the lender.
    Misconceptions: Flash loans do not give free money — the borrowed capital must be repaid in the same transaction, so profit must come from a real opportunity executed atomically. The lender is not the party at risk in flash-loan attacks.
    Example: Borrow 10,000 ETH via Aave flash loan, buy an asset priced at $1.00 on exchange A, sell it at $1.10 on exchange B, repay 10,005 ETH (0.05% fee) — all in one transaction; if any leg fails, the whole transaction reverts and the loan never existed.
    Aliases: one block borrow

    Lending protocol #

    A lending protocol is a set of smart contracts (e.g. Aave, Compound) that pools supplied assets and lets borrowers draw loans against deposited collateral, with interest rates, collateral requirements and liquidations enforced automatically by code.
    Intuition: It is a pawnshop run by a robot: depositors earn interest, borrowers post more collateral than they take, and the robot seizes and sells the collateral the moment the loan turns unsafe.
    Mechanics: Suppliers deposit assets into pools and receive interest-bearing receipts (e.g. aTokens); borrowers post collateral and borrow up to an asset-specific LTV, paying a utilization-driven interest rate that accrues algorithmically. Oracles price collateral continuously, and when a position's health factor falls below 1, any third party can liquidate it for a bonus.
    Failure modes: Oracle failure or manipulation can trigger wrongful liquidations or let attackers borrow against worthless collateral; fast crashes can outrun liquidators and leave the protocol with bad debt; smart contract exploits and governance parameter mistakes are systemic risks.
    Misconceptions: DeFi lending is not peer-to-peer matching — lenders face the pool, and borrowers face the protocol, with rates set by utilization curves. Deposit insurance does not exist; 'safe' yields embed smart contract, oracle and liquidation risk.
    Example: A user supplies $10,000 of ETH to Aave (80% liquidation threshold) and borrows $6,000 of a stablecoin; interest accrues to suppliers from the borrower's rate, and if ETH falls enough that the health factor crosses 1, liquidators repay part of the debt and seize discounted ETH.
    Aliases: money market protocol, DeFi lending

    Collateral factor #

    The collateral factor is the per-asset parameter that determines how much borrowing power a unit of deposited collateral confers, expressed as a percentage of the collateral's value; in Aave V3 this concept is split into LTV (borrowing power for new loans) and liquidation threshold (the undercollateralization trigger).
    Intuition: It is the haircut the protocol applies to your collateral: $100 of a blue-chip asset might count as $80 of borrowing power, while a volatile token counts as far less.
    Mechanics: Each listed asset has a governance-set collateral factor/LTV reflecting its volatility and liquidity risk. A user's total borrowing capacity is the sum over collateral assets of value × factor; borrowing beyond it is impossible, and positions drifting past the (higher) liquidation threshold become liquidatable. Riskier or less liquid assets get lower factors.
    Failure modes: Factors set too high for illiquid collateral let bad debt accumulate because liquidators cannot exit seized collateral profitably; factors changed by governance can shift risk parameters on live positions.
    Misconceptions: The collateral factor is not the interest rate — it caps loan size, not cost. Depositing more collateral value does not always raise borrowing power proportionally if the asset has a low or zero factor.
    Example: Aave V3's stablecoin E-Mode category uses 97% LTV and 98% liquidation threshold, while a volatile long-tail asset in isolation mode might carry 50% LTV with a hard debt ceiling.
    Borrowing capacity = Σ (collateral_i × price_i × factor_i); e.g. $10,000 ETH at 80% → $8,000 max borrow.

    Loan-to-value ratio #

    Loan-to-value (LTV) is the ratio of the borrowed amount to the value of the collateral securing it; in DeFi lending, each asset's maximum LTV caps how much can be newly borrowed against it.
    Intuition: LTV is the leverage dial on a collateralized loan: 75% LTV means every $100 of collateral backs at most $75 of new borrowing.
    Mechanics: In Aave, a user's maximum borrowing power is Σ(collateral value × asset LTV); opening a borrow that would exceed it reverts. LTV is set per asset by governance below the liquidation threshold, leaving a buffer so borrowers are not instantly liquidatable at max borrow; E-Mode categories raise LTV for correlated pairs (e.g. stablecoins at 97%).
    Failure modes: Borrowing at maximum LTV leaves almost no buffer: a small adverse price move or accrued interest pushes the position to liquidation; LTV does not protect against oracle mispricing of the collateral itself.
    Misconceptions: A higher max LTV is not 'better collateral' in economic terms — it is a risk parameter choice trading capital efficiency against liquidation safety. LTV caps new borrows; the liquidation threshold, a separate higher number, triggers liquidations.
    Example: With $10,000 of ETH collateral at 75% LTV you can borrow up to $7,500; if ETH's liquidation threshold is 80%, your loan becomes liquidatable only once debt exceeds $8,000 (through price drops or interest).
    LTV = borrow value / collateral value; max borrow = Σ (collateral_i × price_i × LTV_i).

    Health factor #

    The health factor is Aave's numeric measure of how safe a borrow position is: the collateralization of the account weighted by liquidation thresholds, divided by total borrows — a value below 1 means the position can be liquidated.
    Intuition: It is a fuel gauge for your loan: comfortably above 1 is green, approaching 1 is the warning light, below 1 liquidators take the car.
    Mechanics: Health factor = (Σ collateral_i in ETH × liquidation threshold_i) / total borrows in ETH. It rises when collateral appreciates or debt is repaid, and falls when collateral drops or debt grows (including accrued interest). Users improve it by supplying more collateral or repaying; liquidators monitor accounts for HF < 1.
    Failure modes: HF depends entirely on oracle prices, so oracle failure or L2 sequencer downtime can make HF misleading exactly when it matters; in violent crashes HF can skip from safe to insolvent before liquidators act, leaving bad debt.
    Misconceptions: HF = 1 is not where you 'start losing money' — you lose value continuously as collateral falls; HF just marks the point where the protocol allows others to seize it. A high HF today says nothing about HF tomorrow in volatile collateral.
    Example: Supplying $10,000 of ETH at an 80% liquidation threshold and borrowing $6,000 of GHO gives HF = (10,000×0.8)/6,000 = 1.333; if ETH falls 30%, HF drops to ~0.93 and the position is liquidatable.
    HF = (Total collateral value × weighted avg liquidation threshold) / Total borrow value; HF < 1 → liquidatable.

    Liquidation threshold #

    The liquidation threshold is the per-asset percentage of collateral value at which a borrow position is deemed undercollateralized and becomes eligible for liquidation — effectively the maintenance margin of a DeFi loan.
    Intuition: It is the tripwire on your collateral: once your debt exceeds this share of what you posted, the protocol declares the loan unsafe and lets liquidators step in.
    Mechanics: Aave Governance sets a liquidation threshold per asset (e.g. 80% for ETH, 97-98% for stablecoin E-Mode). The account's weighted average threshold enters the health factor formula; when HF falls below 1, a liquidationCall repays up to 50% of debt (100% if deeply insolvent) and seizes collateral at a discount. The threshold sits above LTV, giving borrowers a buffer between max borrow and liquidation.
    Failure modes: Thresholds set too high for volatile assets let insolvency arrive before liquidators can profitably act; governance misconfiguration or oracle errors can liquidate healthy users or fail to liquidate dying ones.
    Misconceptions: The liquidation threshold is not the same as max LTV — LTV limits new borrowing, the (higher) threshold triggers liquidation; confusing them leads borrowers to think max-borrowed positions are safe. It is a parameter, not a market-derived guarantee.
    Example: With ETH's 80% threshold, a borrower with $10,000 ETH and $7,900 debt is still safe (HF ≈ 1.013), but a further $150 drop in collateral value or $150 of accrued interest crosses HF < 1 and opens the position to liquidators.
    Position liquidatable when: borrows > Σ(collateral_i × liquidation threshold_i).

    Liquidation bonus #

    The liquidation bonus (or penalty) is the discount at which a liquidator can claim a borrower's collateral when repaying their debt — the economic incentive that makes third-party liquidation profitable.
    Intuition: It is the bounty for the repo agent: repay part of a failing loan and you get to buy the borrower's collateral at a few percent below market.
    Mechanics: When HF < 1, anyone can call liquidationCall, repay up to 50% of the debt (100% if HF is below the close-factor threshold), and receive collateral worth debt repaid × (1 + bonus). Bonuses are set per asset by governance (e.g. ~5% on majors, higher on risky assets); the discount is the borrower's liquidation penalty.
    Failure modes: Bonuses that are too small fail to attract liquidators in congestion or crashes (bad debt accumulates); too large, and they over-punish borrowers and invite liquidation-hunting manipulation of oracle prices.
    Misconceptions: The bonus is not free money for liquidators — competition, gas costs and price slippage on seized collateral eat most of it. Borrowers lose the bonus on top of their market losses; it is a penalty, not a fee rebate.
    Example: A liquidator repays $5,000 of a failing loan backed by ETH at a 5% bonus and receives $5,250 worth of the borrower's ETH, profiting ~$250 minus gas and any slippage when selling the ETH.
    Collateral seized = (debt repaid × (1 + liquidation bonus)) / collateral price; e.g. repay $1,000 at 5% bonus → $1,050 of collateral.

    Overcollateralized loan #

    An overcollateralized loan is a loan whose collateral value exceeds the borrowed amount — the standard structure of DeFi lending, where borrowers must post more than they take because there is no credit assessment or recourse beyond the collateral.
    Intuition: Borrow $70 by locking up $100: the lender never checks your credit because your collateral is already worth more than the debt and can be seized instantly.
    Mechanics: Lending protocols enforce overcollateralization through per-asset LTV caps (e.g. 75-80%) and liquidation thresholds: oracles revalue collateral continuously, and positions drifting toward undercollateralization (health factor < 1) are liquidated permissionlessly to keep the protocol solvent. Interest accrues on the debt, slowly consuming the collateral buffer.
    Failure modes: Overcollateralization protects only while collateral is liquid and correctly priced: flash crashes, oracle failures or illiquid collateral can flip a loan to undercollateralized before liquidators respond, creating protocol bad debt.
    Misconceptions: Overcollateralized borrowing is capital-inefficient by design, not a flaw to be fixed by higher LTVs alone — it substitutes collateral for creditworthiness. It also does not make the loan risk-free for the borrower, who can still be liquidated at a penalty.
    Example: Locking $15,000 of ETH to borrow $7,500 of USDC (50% effective LTV against an 80% threshold) lets you spend dollars without selling ETH, but a ~47% ETH drawdown makes the position liquidatable.
    Collateralization ratio = collateral value / borrow value > 1 (e.g. $10,000 collateral / $6,000 debt ≈ 1.67).

    Under-collateralized loan #

    An under-collateralized loan is a loan where the borrower's collateral is worth less than the outstanding debt — either by design (credit-based or flash loans) or by deterioration (a liquidated-too-late position that becomes protocol bad debt).
    Intuition: It is lending on trust rather than on locked value — normal in banking, but the exception in DeFi, where code cannot chase a borrower for the difference.
    Mechanics: Mainstream DeFi lending is overcollateralized, so under-collateralized lending appears only in special forms: flash loans (zero collateral, repaid atomically in the same transaction), credit delegation (a depositor delegates borrowing power to a trusted party), and undercollateralized institutional credit pools with legal agreements. When a normal position becomes undercollateralized faster than liquidators act, the deficit becomes bad debt socialized across the protocol.
    Failure modes: Without excess collateral, default risk returns: credit delegation and institutional pools depend on legal enforcement or reputation rather than code, and defaults cannot be clawed back trustlessly; bad debt from failed liquidations is absorbed by the protocol's suppliers.
    Misconceptions: Under-collateralized does not mean unsecured-in-the-moment for flash loans — atomicity is a stronger guarantee than collateral. Conversely, an 'overcollateralized' loan is only as safe as its collateral's liquidity and oracle, and can silently become undercollateralized in a crash.
    Example: A flash loan of 50,000 ETH is 'undercollateralized' for ~12 seconds yet riskless to Aave because repayment is enforced in the same transaction; by contrast, a $1M institutional credit line with $600k collateral relies on contracts and courts if the borrower defaults.
    Aliases: undercollateralized loan, unsecured loan (DeFi context)

    Staking #

    Staking is the act of depositing a network's native token to activate validator software that secures a proof-of-stake chain — proposing and attesting to blocks — in exchange for protocol rewards, with penalties (slashing) for misbehavior or downtime.
    Intuition: Staking is posting a performance bond to become part-time infrastructure: do the work honestly and you earn a yield on your deposit; cheat or sleep and part of the deposit burns.
    Mechanics: On Ethereum, solo staking requires 32 ETH and running execution, consensus and validator clients; rewards accrue for attestations, block proposals and sync duties, while offline validators leak small penalties and equivocating validators are slashed and ejected. Alternatives with lower barriers include pooled staking, staking-as-a-service, liquid staking and exchange staking, each adding its own trust assumptions.
    Failure modes: Slashing (including correlated mass slashing) and inactivity penalties reduce stake; custodial and pooled staking concentrate funds and validators, creating central points of failure; staking yields are diluted as more of the supply stakes (issuance per validator falls with total stake).
    Misconceptions: Staking is not a risk-free interest account — rewards pay for active duties and capital at risk, and third-party staking adds counterparty and smart contract risk. The APR is not fixed: it falls as total stake rises and varies with network activity (tips, MEV).
    Example: A solo staker with 32 ETH earns roughly 3-4% APR from consensus rewards plus priority fees and MEV when proposing blocks; a validator offline for days loses small daily penalties, while double-signing can cost 1+ ETH and forced exit.
    Aliases: PoS staking

    Liquid staking token #

    A liquid staking token (LST) is a transferable token issued by a liquid staking protocol (e.g. Lido's stETH) that represents a claim on staked assets plus accruing rewards, letting stakers stay liquid and use their stake in DeFi while validators do the work.
    Intuition: It is a receipt for your staked ETH that trades like ETH itself — you earn staking yield while still being able to sell, lend or LP the receipt.
    Mechanics: Users deposit ETH into the staking pool; the protocol stakes it across a set of professional node operators and mints stETH (or similar) to the depositor. The token's balance or exchange rate grows with staking rewards net of a protocol fee (Lido takes 10% of rewards), and it can be traded, used as collateral, or redeemed for the underlying (subject to withdrawal queues).
    Failure modes: LSTs can trade below their underlying value (depeg) during market stress or withdrawal congestion, and leveraged looping of LST collateral unwinds violently when the peg slips; smart contract bugs, operator slashing and governance capture add layers beyond native staking risk.
    Misconceptions: An LST is not pegged 1:1 by arbitrage the way a stablecoin is — secondary-market price floats and can deviate for months. 'Liquid' also does not mean instantly redeemable: native withdrawals queue, so liquidity depends on DEX depth.
    Example: Deposit 10 ETH into Lido and receive ~10 stETH; a year later the position represents ~10.3 ETH of staking rewards, and throughout you could have supplied the stETH as Aave collateral to borrow stablecoins.
    User APR ≈ protocol staking APR × (1 − protocol fee); e.g. 3.2% × (1 − 0.10) ≈ 2.9%.
    Aliases: LST, staking derivative, liquid staking derivative (LSD)
