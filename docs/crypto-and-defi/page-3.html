    Restaking #

    Restaking is the reuse of already-staked ETH or liquid staking tokens as cryptoeconomic security for additional services (Actively/Autonomously Verifiable Services, AVSs), letting those services borrow Ethereum's trust while stakers earn extra rewards and accept extra slashing conditions.
    Intuition: It is posting the same collateral as bail for multiple jobs at once: more income from one deposit, but any job can trigger forfeiture of the shared stake.
    Mechanics: On EigenLayer, stakers deposit native ETH or LSTs into restaking contracts and delegate to operators who run AVS software (oracles, bridges, data-availability layers). AVSs pay rewards to operators and stakers for correct service and can slash the restaked stake if commitments are broken — extending Ethereum-grade economic security without each service bootstrapping its own validator set.
    Failure modes: Stacked slashing conditions mean a bug or dispute in any single AVS can destroy stake that also secures others; leverage-like recursive restaking (LSTs used as collateral to restake) creates contagion paths where one depeg or slash cascades across services.
    Misconceptions: Restaking does not create new security from nothing — it re-hypothecates the same stake, so total security is only as strong as the weakest slashing regime in the stack. Extra yield is compensation for extra slashing risk, not free return.
    Example: A staker restakes 32 ETH worth of stETH on EigenLayer and delegates to an operator serving a data-availability AVS; they earn Ethereum staking rewards plus AVS rewards, but an AVS slashing event can burn the same stake backing both.
    Aliases: re-staking

    Yield farming #

    Yield farming is the practice of deploying crypto assets across DeFi protocols — lending pools, liquidity pools, staking — to maximize returns from interest, trading fees and token incentives, often moving capital frequently to chase the highest rates.
    Intuition: It is rate-chasing on steroids: farmers shuttle capital to wherever the combination of fees, interest and reward tokens pays the most this week.
    Mechanics: Farmers supply liquidity or collateral to protocols and harvest multiple income streams: base yield (fees/interest) plus incentive token emissions. Strategies include leveraged looping (deposit, borrow, redeposit) and auto-compounding vaults; net return must be weighed against gas costs, impermanent loss, reward-token price decay and smart contract risk.
    Failure modes: Advertised APYs assume reward tokens hold value — emissions-driven yields collapse when the token dumps; unaudited farms are frequent rug/exploit targets, and leveraged loops liquidate catastrophically in drawdowns.
    Misconceptions: Triple-digit APYs are not sustainable yield — they are mostly marketing paid in inflationary tokens whose sell pressure the farmer joins. 'Auto-compounding' compounds losses just as reliably when the underlying position deteriorates.
    Example: A farmer deposits stETH as Aave collateral, borrows ETH at 2.5%, swaps to stETH and redeposits, looping to ~3x a 2.9% staking yield — until a stETH depeg forces liquidation of the whole stack.
    Net farm PnL = fees + interest + reward token value − impermanent loss − gas − borrow costs.
    Aliases: liquidity farming

    Liquidity mining #

    Liquidity mining is a token incentive program in which a protocol distributes its governance tokens to users who provide liquidity (or other desired behavior), subsidizing early liquidity and decentralizing token ownership.
    Intuition: It is paying suppliers in company stock to stock the shelves: the protocol rents liquidity with newly minted tokens instead of cash.
    Mechanics: The protocol allocates an emission budget to targeted pools; LPs stake their LP tokens in a mining contract and accrue pro-rata governance token rewards (e.g. Uniswap's 2020 program paid 5M UNI per pool to ETH/USDT, ETH/USDC, ETH/DAI and ETH/WBTC LPs). Rewards are additive to trading fees and typically decline as programs end or more capital joins.
    Failure modes: Mercenary capital arrives for emissions and leaves the moment they stop, so TVL and liquidity evaporate post-program; large emissions dilute the token and create persistent sell pressure ('farm and dump').
    Misconceptions: Liquidity mining rewards are not protocol revenue or profit-sharing — they are dilutive token issuance paying for growth. A high liquidity-mining APR says nothing about the pool's organic fee economics.
    Example: Uniswap's September 2020 liquidity mining program distributed 83,333 UNI per pool per day across four v2 pools for two months; participating LPs earned UNI on top of the standard 0.3% swap fees.
    Aliases: LM incentives, liquidity incentives

    Tokenomics #

    Tokenomics is the economic design of a crypto token: its supply schedule (issuance, burns, caps), distribution (allocations, vesting, airdrops), utility (fees, governance, staking) and the incentive structures that determine how value and behavior accrue around it.
    Intuition: It is the monetary policy plus cap table plus business model of a token — the rules that decide who gets tokens, when, and why anyone should hold them.
    Mechanics: Core levers include fixed vs inflationary supply and emission schedules, allocation splits across community/team/investors with vesting, burn or buyback mechanisms, and utility sinks (fee capture, staking, governance). These parameters are set in the whitepaper or governance and drive circulating supply dynamics, dilution and sell-pressure over time.
    Failure modes: Low-float/high-FDV designs with heavy insider allocations create structural overhang: unlocks and emissions overwhelm demand; unsustainable incentive emissions fund short-term growth at the cost of long-term dilution and death spirals.
    Misconceptions: A capped supply does not guarantee scarcity value — emission timing and insider concentration matter more than the headline cap. Price appreciation is not a tokenomics success metric; design quality shows in distribution fairness and sustainable value accrual.
    Example: Uniswap's UNI: 1B minted at genesis, 60% to the community, 21.3% to team and 18% to investors on 4-year vesting, 15% retroactively airdropped to users, and a 2% perpetual inflation after year 4 — a complete tokenomic specification in one announcement.
    Aliases: token economics

    Circulating supply #

    Circulating supply is the number of tokens currently issued and available to the market — excluding locked, vested, escrowed or not-yet-minted tokens — and is the supply basis for the conventional market capitalization.
    Intuition: It is the float: the tokens that could actually change hands today, not the ones still locked in team vaults or scheduled for future emission.
    Mechanics: Different providers compute it differently: reported circulating supply is typically sourced from project disclosures (Coin Metrics' 'Estimated Market Cap' uses circulating supply from project/CoinGecko feeds), while on-chain approaches count issued native units (which can include escrow). The number drifts upward as tokens unlock and emissions accrue.
    Failure modes: Circulating supply is partly self-reported, so projects can understate locked supply or treasuries, inflating the apparent float; low-float tokens show deceptively small market caps that hide massive future dilution.
    Misconceptions: Circulating supply is not directly observable from price feeds and methodologies differ across data providers — comparisons must use a consistent source. It also does not equal 'liquid' supply: much of it may be dormant or staked.
    Example: A token with 100M circulating, 400M vested-locked and 1B max supply trading at $5 shows a $500M market cap but a $5B FDV; holders face 10x potential dilution as unlocks arrive.
    Circulating supply ≈ total issued − locked/vested/escrowed; Market cap = circulating supply × price.
    Aliases: float, circulating tokens

    Total supply #

    Total supply is the total number of tokens that have been created (minted) so far, minus any that have been verifiably burned — i.e. all tokens in existence whether or not they are circulating.
    Intuition: It is the full headcount of minted tokens: circulating plus everything still locked in vesting contracts, treasuries and escrow.
    Mechanics: Total supply = cumulative issuance − cumulative burns; it grows with emissions (block rewards, unlock-related mints) and shrinks with burns (e.g. EIP-1559 base-fee burning on Ethereum). Locked/vested tokens count toward total supply but not circulating supply, so total ≥ circulating at any moment.
    Failure modes: Projects blur total vs circulating to make valuations look smaller or larger as convenient; tokens in 'ecosystem' wallets technically count as total supply while being economically equivalent to insider float.
    Misconceptions: Total supply is not the cap — for inflationary tokens there is no maximum, and total supply grows indefinitely. A low total supply number means nothing for price without knowing emission rate and burns.
    Example: Ethereum's total supply equals all ETH ever issued as block rewards minus all ETH burned via base fees since EIP-1559 — fluctuating near ~120M with no maximum cap.
    Total supply = Σ minted − Σ burned; Circulating supply ≤ Total supply ≤ Maximum supply.
    Aliases: issued supply

    Maximum supply #

    Maximum supply is the hard upper bound on the number of tokens that can ever exist under a protocol's issuance rules, where such a cap exists — most famously Bitcoin's 21 million.
    Intuition: It is the ceiling written into the monetary constitution: no matter what happens, there will never be more than this many coins.
    Mechanics: Protocols with capped issuance hard-code an emission schedule that asymptotically approaches the cap (Bitcoin's subsidy halves every 210,000 blocks until issuance rounds to zero, after which only fees reward miners). Many tokens (ETH, most governance tokens with perpetual inflation) have no maximum supply, so the field is undefined for them.
    Failure modes: The cap is only as credible as the social consensus defending it — a sufficiently dominant coalition could change the code, and the security budget problem (subsidy → 0, fees must replace it) is unresolved for Bitcoin's distant future.
    Misconceptions: Not all tokens have a maximum supply, and a big cap is not automatically 'bad' — what matters is emission schedule and utility. 'Max supply reached' does not mean all coins are circulating; lost keys permanently shrink effective float below the cap.
    Example: Bitcoin's max supply is 21M BTC, of which ~19.9M had been mined by 2025; the final satoshis will be issued around the year 2140 as the 33rd halving approaches zero subsidy.
    Bitcoin: Max supply = 21,000,000 BTC; subsidy_t = 50 / 2^⌊t/210,000⌋ BTC per block, converging to 0.
    Aliases: max supply, supply cap

    Fully diluted valuation #

    Fully diluted valuation (FDV) is a token's price multiplied by its total (or maximum) supply — the market capitalization the asset would have if every token that can ever exist were already circulating at today's price.
    Intuition: FDV is the sticker price of the whole company if all shares, including locked insider shares, traded at today's price — often a fantasy number for low-float tokens.
    Mechanics: FDV = current price × total/max supply, versus market cap = price × circulating supply. Coin Metrics formalizes a related 'Future Market Cap' using known issuance over the next 10 years. A large FDV-to-market-cap gap quantifies overhang from future unlocks and emissions and is a standard dilution-risk gauge.
    Failure modes: FDV assumes future tokens would trade at today's price, which is false under emission-driven sell pressure — it overstates achievable value in either direction; comparing FDVs across projects ignores different float, demand and emission profiles.
    Misconceptions: A high FDV/low float token is not 'cheap' because its market cap looks small — the FDV is the honest size of the claim on future supply. FDV is also not a price target; it is a static dilution snapshot.
    Example: A new L2 token trades at $2 with 100M circulating (market cap $200M) but 10B total supply: FDV = $20B, a 100x float gap warning that 99% of supply is still locked and coming to market.
    FDV = price × total (or max) supply; dilution overhang ≈ FDV / market cap.

    Market capitalization #

    Crypto market capitalization is the total USD value of an asset's supply, conventionally computed as current price multiplied by circulating supply (Coin Metrics' on-chain variant uses all issued native units).
    Intuition: It is the price tag of the entire network at today's price — the standard yardstick for comparing the size of cryptoassets.
    Mechanics: Conventional market cap = circulating supply × price (reported float), while Coin Metrics' CapMrktCurUSD = current issued supply × daily close, deliberately including escrow/foundation holdings; 'free float' variants exclude insider and strategic holdings. The choice of supply basis is why different sites report different market caps for the same coin.
    Failure modes: Market cap treats illiquid, lost and locked coins as if they traded at spot, overstating economically real value; for thin-float tokens, small purchases move price — and thus 'market cap' — far more than real capital committed.
    Misconceptions: Market cap is not 'money invested' in the coin — it is a marginal price times a stock of supply, so a $1B market cap can rest on a few million dollars of actual inflows. It also says nothing about liquidity or exit capacity.
    Example: If BTC trades at $100,000 with ~19.9M coins issued, its market cap is ~$1.99T; Coin Metrics' realized cap — each coin at its last-moved price — sits far lower, showing how much of that figure is unrealized gain.
    Market cap = circulating supply × price; (Coin Metrics on-chain: CapMrktCurUSD = SplyCur × PriceUSD).
    Aliases: market cap, network value

    Token unlock #

    A token unlock is the scheduled release of previously locked tokens (team, investor, advisor or ecosystem allocations) into transferable circulation under a project's vesting terms — a predictable supply shock that markets typically front-run.
    Intuition: It is the day the dam gate opens on insider stock: a known date when a tranche of supply becomes sellable for the first time.
    Mechanics: Vesting contracts release allocations at cliffs and/or linearly per the published schedule; trackers (e.g. Tokenomist, DefiLlama) index these events. Keyrock's analysis of 16,000+ unlocks found ~90% create negative price pressure, with effects starting ~30 days before the event and team unlocks producing the worst drawdowns (~−25% on average).
    Failure modes: Unlocks concentrate sell pressure at known dates, inviting front-running and volatility; opaque or renegotiated schedules destroy predictability, and large unlocks into thin markets can trigger cascading drawdowns.
    Misconceptions: An unlock is not new inflation — it is previously counted total supply becoming liquid, though the price effect can be identical. Not all unlocks dump equally: ecosystem-development unlocks historically showed slightly positive average effects, while team unlocks were the most negative.
    Example: A token with $200M market cap unlocking $40M (20% of float) for team and seed investors sees price slide weeks ahead of the date as traders position for the predictable supply expansion.
    Aliases: unlock event, vesting unlock

    Vesting schedule #

    A vesting schedule is the pre-committed timetable by which locked token allocations (team, investors, advisors) become transferable, typically combining a cliff (no tokens for an initial period) with linear or batched releases thereafter.
    Intuition: It is a golden-handcuffs calendar: insiders earn their tokens over years instead of getting them on day one, aligning them with the project's survival.
    Mechanics: Allocations are locked in smart contracts and released per the published schedule — common shapes are a 1-year cliff followed by 2-3 years of linear monthly unlocks, batch unlocks at the cliff, or hybrids (e.g. Solana combined no-cliff linear community release, a 1-year cliff batch unlock for seed/foundation/validators, and 50%-after-9-months-then-monthly for the team).
    Failure modes: Heavy cliff unlocks create cliff-edge supply shocks; schedules enforced only by promises (multisig rather than contract) can be renegotiated, and short vesting relative to project maturity leaves insiders liquid before the product proves itself.
    Misconceptions: Vesting does not eliminate sell pressure, it only delays it — the overhang remains and markets price it in advance. A '4-year vest' with a 1-year cliff still releases a large lump at month 12.
    Example: Uniswap's UNI allocated 21.266% to team and 18.044% to investors with 4-year vesting; the governance treasury itself vested 40%/30%/20%/10% across years 1-4, with control unlocked after a 30-day grace period.
    Aliases: vesting, vesting calendar

    Token burn #

    A token burn is the permanent, verifiable removal of tokens from supply by sending them to an unspendable destination or destroying them in-protocol, reducing total supply.
    Intuition: It is shredding banknotes on-chain: the tokens provably cease to exist, making each remaining token a larger share of the whole.
    Mechanics: Two forms exist: protocol burns, where issuance rules destroy value automatically (Ethereum burns every transaction's base fee via EIP-1559), and discretionary burns, where projects send tokens to the zero address or a burn() function — often funded by buybacks. Burns are public and auditable, unlike token buyback-and-hold programs.
    Failure modes: Burns funded by minting elsewhere (burn-and-mint equilibrium optics) can be theatrics that obscure net issuance; discretionary burn announcements are sometimes used to hype price without changing fundamentals, and burned supply is irreversible if later needed for security budgets.
    Misconceptions: A burn does not automatically raise price — value accrues only if the burn transfers real economic value (e.g. fee burns) rather than just shrinking a number; burning tokens held by the treasury is accounting, not value creation. Ethereum's base-fee burn is not guaranteed to make ETH deflationary — it depends on burn vs issuance.
    Example: Since EIP-1559, every Ethereum transaction burns its base fee: at a 20 gwei base fee, a 21,000-gas transfer destroys 0.00042 ETH; over high-demand periods this has burned millions of ETH cumulatively, at times exceeding new issuance.
    Aliases: burn, coin burn

    Minting #

    Minting is the creation of new tokens on-chain, increasing total supply — whether as protocol issuance (block rewards to miners/validators) or as application-level token creation by smart contracts.
    Intuition: It is the printing press of a token economy: new units come into existence by rule, either paying for security or instantiating new assets.
    Mechanics: At the protocol layer, minting follows the consensus issuance schedule: Bitcoin's coinbase transaction creates new BTC for the block's miner, and Ethereum issues new ETH to validators for proposals and attestations. At the application layer, contracts mint tokens (e.g. LP tokens on deposit, LSTs on staking, NFTs on purchase) according to their code; unbacked or infinite-mint bugs are a classic exploit.
    Failure modes: Smart contract minting flaws (infinite mint, unbacked mint) let attackers fabricate supply and drain pools or depeg assets; at the protocol level, minting is inflation that dilutes holders unless offset by demand or burns.
    Misconceptions: Mining and minting are related but distinct: PoW miners and PoS validators earn newly minted issuance, but 'minting' also covers any contract-created token. Newly minted rewards are not free value — their cost is dilution of all holders.
    Example: Depositing 5 ETH into Lido mints ~5 stETH to your wallet (application-level mint), while Ethereum's protocol simultaneously mints small new ETH amounts every slot to pay validators (protocol issuance).
    Aliases: mint, token issuance

    Emission schedule #

    An emission schedule is the predetermined rule governing the rate and timing of new token issuance over time — the protocol's monetary policy expressed as supply growth per block, epoch or era.
    Intuition: It is the central-bank timetable baked into code: exactly how many new tokens are printed, when, and for whom, years into the future.
    Mechanics: Bitcoin emits a block subsidy that halves every 210,000 blocks (50 → 25 → 12.5 → 3.125 BTC...) toward the 21M cap; Ethereum issues ETH per epoch to validators with rates that fall as total stake rises, net of base-fee burns. Application tokens define their own schedules for incentives and treasury releases. Coin Metrics' future-supply metrics formalize projecting supply from the known schedule.
    Failure modes: Declining emissions create a long-run security-budget question (fees must eventually fund security alone); for application tokens, front-loaded emissions fund growth but guarantee heavy early dilution and sell pressure.
    Misconceptions: A fixed emission schedule makes supply predictable, not price — demand shocks still dominate valuation. 'Deflationary' claims require net issuance (emissions − burns) below zero, not just a burn mechanism.
    Example: After the April 2024 halving, Bitcoin emits 3.125 BTC per ~10-minute block (~450/day, ~0.8% annualized); UNI instead has a 2% perpetual annual inflation beginning in year 4 after its 1B genesis mint.
    Bitcoin subsidy = 50 / 2^⌊height/210,000⌋ BTC per block; annualized inflation = issuance per year / current supply.

    Airdrop #

    An airdrop is the free distribution of tokens to a set of wallet addresses, typically to reward past users, bootstrap a community, or decentralize governance ownership, with eligibility usually set by an on-chain snapshot.
    Intuition: It is a retroactive thank-you payment and marketing campaign in one: wake up, check your wallet, and there are tokens because you used the protocol before a cutoff date.
    Mechanics: The project snapshots eligible addresses and activity, then makes tokens claimable (users pay gas to claim) or sends them directly. Uniswap's September 2020 UNI airdrop gave 400 UNI to each of 251,534 historical user addresses (10.06% of supply) plus pro-rata allocations to LPs. Airdrops are often paired with vesting for insiders while community allocations vest immediately.
    Failure modes: Sybil farmers create thousands of wallets to harvest eligibility, diluting genuine users; unexpected random airdrops are frequently dusting or scam tokens whose approval transactions drain wallets; large immediate floats can crash the token at listing.
    Misconceptions: Airdrops are not free money in aggregate — their value comes from dilution and future expectations, and 'connect your wallet to claim' sites are the most common phishing lure. Airdropped tokens may be taxable income at receipt in many jurisdictions.
    Example: In September 2020, every address that had ever called Uniswap v1 or v2 could claim 400 UNI (worth ~$1,200 at launch); even ~12,000 addresses whose transactions had only ever failed were included.
    Aliases: token airdrop

    Governance token #

    A governance token is a token whose primary utility is voting power over a protocol's decisions — parameter changes, treasury spending, upgrades — typically exercised through a DAO's proposal-and-vote contracts.
    Intuition: It is a shareholder ballot in token form: holding it gives you a say in how the protocol runs, weighted by how much you hold.
    Mechanics: Holders (or their delegates) create and vote on proposals; if quorum and majority thresholds pass, execution happens automatically or via timelocked multisig (e.g. UNI: 1% of supply to propose, 4% quorum, 7-day voting, 2-day timelock). Many governance tokens also carry fee-switch or value-accrual potential, but voting power itself is the core function.
    Failure modes: Low participation lets small coalitions or whales capture governance; vote-buying, bribery markets and flash-loan governance attacks can pass malicious proposals; delegation concentrates effective power in a few hands.
    Misconceptions: A governance token is not equity — it usually grants no legal claim on revenue or assets, only on-chain voting rights. 'Decentralized governance' in practice often means a handful of delegates and a multisig execute most decisions.
    Example: UNI holders can vote to activate Uniswap's protocol fee switch or spend the community treasury (43% of supply); ENS holders instead delegate their votes to community stewards who vote on their behalf.
    Aliases: gov token

    Protocol revenue #

    Protocol revenue is the portion of the fees a protocol collects that is kept by the protocol entity itself — flowing to its treasury or governance token holders — as distinct from fees passed through to liquidity providers or other suppliers.
    Intuition: If fees are the till, protocol revenue is what the house actually keeps after paying the market makers who stocked the shelves.
    Mechanics: Using DefiLlama's taxonomy: total fees = all value collected from users; revenue = fees kept by the protocol = protocol revenue (to treasury/team) + holders revenue (distributed to token holders via buybacks, burns or dividends); the remainder is supply-side revenue to LPs/lenders. Only the kept fraction represents income attributable to the protocol and its token.
    Failure modes: Revenue funded by unsustainable incentive cycles or one-off events (liquidation spikes) is not recurring income; comparing revenue across protocols without distinguishing holders vs treasury vs supply-side fractions invites apples-to-oranges valuation errors.
    Misconceptions: Fees are not revenue — a DEX's 0.3% swap fee mostly belongs to LPs, and only the protocol's cut (if any) is revenue. TVL is not revenue either; it is capital parked, not income earned.
    Example: If a lending protocol collects $1M of borrower interest in a day and keeps a 10% reserve factor, its daily revenue is $100k — split between treasury accrual and any buybacks for token holders — while $900k is supply-side revenue to lenders.
    Revenue = ProtocolRevenue + HoldersRevenue; Fees = Revenue + SupplySideRevenue.
    Aliases: protocol income

    Protocol fees #

    Protocol fees are all charges and value a protocol collects from its operation — swap fees, borrow interest, liquidation penalties, management fees — excluding the gas costs users pay to the underlying network.
    Intuition: Fees are the gross till of the protocol: everything users pay in, before it is split between liquidity suppliers, the treasury and token holders.
    Mechanics: Per DefiLlama's dimensions, dailyFees aggregates all value flows into the protocol's ecosystem (user fees like swap fees and interest, plus yield and staking-derived cuts); it is then decomposed into supply-side revenue (to LPs/lenders), protocol revenue (to treasury) and holders revenue (to token holders). Fee levels and switches are typically set by governance.
    Failure modes: Fee revenue can be inflated by circular or incentive-driven volume (wash trading, farm-driven churn) that vanishes when incentives stop; fee-switch promises may never activate, leaving token valuations resting on hypothetical future fees.
    Misconceptions: Protocol fees are not gas fees — gas goes to the chain's validators, not the application. High gross fees do not imply tokenholder value unless a mechanism routes some share to the treasury or holders.
    Example: A DEX processing $100M daily volume at a 0.3% swap fee generates $300k daily fees; with no fee switch, 100% is supply-side revenue to LPs and protocol revenue is $0 — flipping the switch might route ~$50k/day to the treasury.

    Total value locked #

    Total value locked (TVL) is the aggregate USD value of assets deposited by users into a protocol's own smart contracts — the standard gauge of how much capital a DeFi protocol or chain secures.
    Intuition: TVL is the size of the vault: how much money users have parked in the protocol's contracts at this moment.
    Mechanics: Aggregators like DefiLlama sum the value of tokens held in protocol contracts, with filters separating staking (native token), pool2 (LP tokens with the protocol's own token), borrowed deposits and vesting; off-chain assets (e.g. Tether's bank dollars) and native chain staking are excluded. TVL moves with both net deposits and token prices.
    Failure modes: TVL can be gamed: recycled/looped deposits, incentive-farmed liquidity with no real users, self-dealt wrapper assets and double-counting across nested protocols inflate it; DefiLlama actively delists such 'unproductive' TVL. It also rises and falls with token prices independent of usage.
    Misconceptions: TVL is not revenue, profit or even stickiness — mercenary capital can halve it overnight when incentives end. It measures deposited capital, not economic activity; a high-TVL protocol can earn near-zero fees.
    Example: A lending protocol holding $2B of supplied assets has $2B TVL; if $600M is one whale's deposit earning points with no borrows against it, aggregators may strip it as unproductive, reporting $1.4B of economically real TVL.

    Stablecoin supply #

    Stablecoin supply is the total outstanding issuance of stablecoins — cryptocurrencies designed to hold a stable value against a reference asset, usually the US dollar — and is tracked as a proxy for dry powder and fiat liquidity in the crypto economy.
    Intuition: It is the pile of dollar chips sitting at the crypto casino's tables: when it grows, new fiat-backed buying power is entering; when it shrinks, capital is cashing out.
    Mechanics: Stablecoins preserve their peg by design: asset-linked issuers (USDT, USDC) back tokens with reserves and mint/redeem against fiat, while crypto-collateralized designs (Dai) mint against overcollateralized vaults and algorithmic ones adjust supply programmatically. Aggregate supply across issuers and chains is observable on-chain and, per FSB/BIS definitions, reflects how much of the ecosystem's settlement happens in dollar terms.
    Failure modes: Reserve opacity or a run on an issuer can break the peg (redemptions forcing fire sales of reserve assets); bridge fragmentation splits supply across chains where a wrapped version is only as good as its bridge; regulatory action against a major issuer is systemic for the whole market.
    Misconceptions: Stablecoin supply growth is not pure bullish flow — some of it rotates from existing crypto sales into stables (risk-off) rather than fresh fiat in. 'Backed 1:1' claims have historically been revised (Tether's composition disclosures), so attestations, not labels, are the evidence.
    Example: Total stablecoin market value grew from ~$14B in August 2020 to over $300B by 2026, with USDT (~$186B) and USDC (~$79B) comprising roughly 85% of circulation — a scale the BIS flags as a financial-stability consideration.

    Exchange inflow #

    Exchange inflow is the volume of coins transferred on-chain into wallets attributed to centralized exchanges over a period, watched as a leading indicator of intent to sell or trade.
    Intuition: Coins moving onto an exchange are like goods carried into a pawnshop — they are positioned to be sold, even if the sale hasn't happened yet.
    Mechanics: On-chain analytics firms (Glassnode, CryptoQuant) label exchange-controlled addresses and sum transfers into them; inflow is reported in native units or USD per day. Spikes historically cluster around sharp price moves — the March 2020 crash saw deposit volumes surge as holders rushed to sell — while deposit counts and average deposit size distinguish retail from whale behavior.
    Failure modes: Attribution depends on heuristics and labeled wallets, so new exchange addresses or internal reshuffles can masquerade as inflows; inflow ≠ immediate selling, since market makers and custodians also receive deposits for operational reasons.
    Misconceptions: A large inflow is not a guaranteed dump — coins may sit on the exchange for months, and inflows can also fund derivatives collateral or market-making. Raw inflow without reserve context (is the total balance actually growing?) misleads.
    Example: During the March 2020 crash, BTC exchange inflow spiked to unusually high volumes as panicked holders deposited to sell; Glassnode noted the number of daily deposits simultaneously hit a 3-year low, meaning the spike was driven by large whale-sized deposits.

    Exchange outflow #

    Exchange outflow is the volume of coins withdrawn on-chain from exchange-attributed wallets over a period, commonly read as accumulation or movement to self-custody and reduced immediate sell pressure.
    Intuition: Coins leaving the exchange are goods carried home from the market — they are being put back into storage rather than offered for sale.
    Mechanics: Analytics providers sum transfers out of labeled exchange wallets; sustained net outflow shrinks exchange balances (reserves). After the March 2020 crash, outflow declined more slowly than inflow, so net flow stayed negative and the total BTC balance on exchanges dropped ~7% from its February high — a classic accumulation signature.
    Failure modes: Outflows to new exchange wallets or institutional custodians can be misread as self-custody accumulation; reserve decline can also reflect an exchange losing customers (a red flag, not bullishness) — context matters.
    Misconceptions: Outflow is not proof of buying — coins bought OTC or already held can simply be withdrawn, and a single whale reshuffling custody distorts daily figures. Persistent trends over weeks carry far more signal than one-day spikes.
    Example: In mid-2020, BTC exchange outflows persistently exceeded inflows at 50-100k BTC per month rates in later Glassnode reporting, draining exchange balances as post-crash accumulators moved coins to cold storage.
    Net exchange flow = inflow − outflow; negative net flow = reserves shrinking.

    Exchange reserve #

    Exchange reserve (exchange balance) is the total amount of a coin held in wallets attributed to centralized exchanges — the immediately sellable inventory sitting on trading venues.
    Intuition: It is the stock of goods on shop shelves: a bulging shelf means plenty of potential supply for sale; emptying shelves mean holders have taken their coins home.
    Mechanics: Analytics firms cluster and label exchange addresses and track their aggregate balance over time; reserves fall when outflows exceed inflows (accumulation/cold-storage trend) and rise when inflows dominate (distribution risk). Glassnode documented BTC exchange balances dropping ~7% from their February 2020 high as post-crash withdrawals outpaced deposits.
    Failure modes: Label coverage is incomplete and changes as exchanges rotate wallets, so levels and trends embed attribution error; reserves also embed coins that will never be sold (market-maker inventory), and reserve trends can invert during exchange insolvencies for non-bullish reasons.
    Misconceptions: Falling reserves are not mechanically bullish — they can reflect loss of trust in a venue (FTX-style runs) or migration to other custodians. Reserves measure potential, not actual, sell pressure.
    Example: If labeled exchange wallets hold 2.4M BTC out of ~19.9M supply, roughly 12% of all coins sit as venue inventory; a slide to 2.0M over a year of net outflows signals tightening liquid supply.
    Exchange reserve_t = Σ balances of labeled exchange addresses; Δreserve ≈ inflow − outflow.

    Realized capitalization #

    Realized capitalization (realized cap) values each coin at the price when it last moved on-chain, rather than at the current price — approximating the aggregate cost basis of the network and the value 'stored' in the asset.
    Intuition: Instead of pricing every coin at today's quote, realized cap asks: what did the market actually pay for all these coins when they last changed hands?
    Mechanics: For UTXO chains, realized cap = Σ (UTXO value × price at the time that UTXO was created); Coin Metrics extends the same idea to account-based chains using each account's last-movement date. Coins last moved cheap are re-priced upward when spent (raising realized cap), and vice versa; lost or ancient coins carry near-zero weight, unlike in market cap.
    Failure modes: Exchange internal shuffles and self-transfers re-price coins at current levels without economic change of hands, injecting noise; on account-based chains the 'last moved' convention is cruder than UTXO-level data.
    Misconceptions: Realized cap is not 'real money invested' — it ignores off-chain purchases and approximates cost basis from on-chain movement only. A coin unmoved since 2009 counts at ~$0, which is a feature (discounts lost coins), not a data bug.
    Example: Coin Metrics' worked example: accounts holding 100 coins last moved in 2010 ($0), 1,000 coins moved in 2016 ($10) and 500 coins moved in 2019 ($100) give a realized cap of $0 + $10,000 + $50,000 = $60,000 regardless of today's price.
    Realized Cap = Σ value_i × price_last_moved_i (over all UTXOs/accounts).
    Aliases: realized cap, realized value

    Market-value-to-realized-value ratio #

    MVRV (market-value-to-realized-value) is the ratio of an asset's market capitalization to its realized capitalization, comparing the market's current valuation to the aggregate on-chain cost basis.
    Intuition: MVRV asks whether holders are, on average, sitting on big paper gains (ratio high, temptation to sell) or paper losses (ratio low, capitulation territory).
    Mechanics: MVRV = market cap / realized cap. Values >1 mean the average holder is in profit; extremes (>~3.5 historically for BTC) marked late-stage bull markets, while MVRV <1 — price below aggregate cost basis — historically coincided with bear-market bottoms and accumulation zones. It functions as a mean-reversion gauge with realized cap as the mean.
    Failure modes: As market structure matures (ETFs, derivatives, longer holder memory), historical thresholds like 3.5 drift and should not be applied mechanically; exchange-internal coin movements distort realized cap and hence the ratio.
    Misconceptions: MVRV is not a timing tool for entries and exits — it identifies valuation regimes, and markets can stay 'overvalued' or 'undervalued' for months. It is an analytical framework from on-chain data, not a proven predictive law.
    Example: With BTC market cap at $1.2T and realized cap at $500B, MVRV = 2.4 — deep aggregate profit historically associated with elevated distribution risk, though below the >3.5 extremes of prior cycle tops.
    MVRV = Market Cap / Realized Cap.
