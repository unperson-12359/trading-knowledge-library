    Spent output profit ratio #

    SOPR (Spent Output Profit Ratio) is the ratio of the fiat value of spent coins at the time they are spent to their value when they were created, measuring whether coins moving on-chain are, on average, being sold at a profit or a loss.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: SOPR is the market's daily P&L receipt: above 1, spenders are cashing out winners; below 1, they are realizing losses.
    Mechanics: Computed over coins moved in a period: SOPR = Σ(value × price_spent) / Σ(value × price_created) across spent outputs. SOPR >1 signals realized profit-taking (typical in bull rallies), ≈1 is breakeven (often a support/resistance pivot in trends), and <1 marks loss realization or capitulation; aSOPR filters outputs with lifespan under one hour.
    Failure modes: Exchange wallet reshuffles and self-spends register as 'spent' at current price, injecting noise; SOPR describes realized behavior only and ignores the large dormant supply, so it can diverge from aggregate profitability (NUPL/MVRV).
    Misconceptions: SOPR >1 is not automatically bearish — sustained profit-taking above 1 with quick resets to 1 is the signature of a healthy bull trend; only failure of the 1 level to hold flips the regime. It is a behavioral gauge, not a price predictor.
    Example: If coins moved today were created at an average $40,000 and are spent at $50,000, daily SOPR = 1.25 — profits being realized; in a correction, SOPR dipping below 1 and then reclaiming it marks seller exhaustion.
    SOPR = Σ(value_i × price_spent_i) / Σ(value_i × price_created_i), over outputs spent in the period.
    Aliases: SOPR, aSOPR (adjusted variant)

    Net unrealized profit and loss #

    NUPL (Net Unrealized Profit/Loss) measures the network's total paper profit or loss as a share of market cap: the difference between market cap and realized cap, divided by market cap.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: NUPL answers: if every holder sold today, what fraction of the market's value would be profit? It is the aggregate sentiment dial from capitulation to euphoria.
    Mechanics: NUPL = (Market Cap − Realized Cap) / Market Cap = Relative Unrealized Profit − Relative Unrealized Loss. Above 0 the average holder is in profit; historical sentiment bands are <0 capitulation, 0-0.25 anxiety, 0.25-0.5 hope/optimism, 0.5-0.75 belief, >0.75 euphoria — zones that have bracketed cycle bottoms and tops.
    Failure modes: NUPL embeds unrealizable profit of lost coins and long-dormant supply, inflating apparent profit; its historical bands were calibrated on early, smaller markets and drift as the holder base and market structure mature.
    Misconceptions: NUPL is a macro regime indicator, not a short-term signal — markets can sit in 'euphoria' for months during strong trends and in 'capitulation' through long bear phases. It differs from SOPR, which tracks only coins actually spent.
    Example: December 2018: NUPL fell to about −0.28 — the average holder underwater by 28% of market cap — coinciding with the $3,200 cycle bottom; April and November 2021 saw NUPL ≈ 0.74-0.75 at the cycle's twin tops.
    NUPL = (Market Cap − Realized Cap) / Market Cap.
    Aliases: NUPL, relative unrealized profit/loss

    Coin days destroyed #

    Coin Days Destroyed (CDD) is an on-chain activity metric that weights coin movements by dormancy: each unspent coin accumulates one 'coin day' per day, and spending it destroys those accumulated days, so movements of old coins count far more than churn of young ones.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Moving 10 BTC held for a decade rings a louder bell than moving 10 BTC bought yesterday — CDD measures the age-weighted significance of what moved.
    Mechanics: CDD for a spent output = value × lifespan in days; the daily metric sums this over all spent outputs. A 2 BTC UTXO dormant 100 days destroys 200 coin days. Spikes flag long-term holders spending into strength or stress; supply-adjusted CDD normalizes for the steady growth of potential coin days as supply ages and inflates.
    Failure modes: Exchange and custodian wallet maintenance periodically destroys huge coin days without any sale, producing false alarms; absolute CDD levels trend upward over the protocol's life, so raw values are not comparable across eras without supply adjustment.
    Misconceptions: High CDD does not prove old holders are selling — it proves old coins moved, which may be custody reshuffling; context (exchange inflows, SOPR) is needed. Also, repeatedly moving the same coins can destroy at most one coin day per coin per day.
    Example: Baseline BTC traffic runs under ~5M coin days destroyed daily; readings above 20M days are rare and historically appear during high-volatility episodes when large volumes of old coins move, such as cycle tops or capitulation events.
    CDD = Σ (value_i × lifespan_i [days]) over outputs spent in the period.
    Aliases: CDD, coindays destroyed

    HODL waves #

    HODL waves are a visualization of the age distribution of the coin supply: colored bands show what fraction of all coins last moved within each age bucket (from <1 day to >10 years), revealing accumulation and distribution cycles.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is a geological cross-section of holder behavior: warm young layers swell when coins churn, cool old layers thicken when conviction holders sit tight.
    Mechanics: Each band's thickness is the share of total supply whose last on-chain movement falls in that age band (bands: <1d, 1d-1w, 1w-1m, 1-3m, 3-6m, 6-12m, 1-2y, 2-3y, 3-5y, 5-7y, 7-10y, >10y). Young bands swell near bull-market tops as old coins are spent and reactivated; old bands thicken in bear markets as accumulation matures into dormancy.
    Failure modes: Movement-based aging resets a coin's age even for self-transfers and exchange reshuffles, so apparent 'reactivation' may be pure custody plumbing; bands lag reality by construction — coins must survive to a band's lower bound before appearing in it.
    Misconceptions: Thickening old bands do not prove accumulation by smart money — they also include lost coins, which grow monotonically and are often assumed out of circulation. HODL waves describe structure, not timing.
    Example: At bull-market peaks, the <6-month bands have historically swollen toward 50%+ of supply as long-term holders distributed; in deep bear markets, 1y+ bands thicken as coins migrate into cold storage and mature.
    Aliases: HODL wave, age bands, UTXO age distribution

    Active addresses #

    Active addresses is the count of unique addresses that participated in the network as a sender or receiver in successful transactions over a period — the standard on-chain proxy for network usage and user activity.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is the daily foot-traffic count of a blockchain: how many distinct addresses actually showed up and transacted.
    Mechanics: Analytics providers count unique addresses appearing as input/output of successful transactions per day (or hour); Glassnode's BTC definition counts only addresses active in successful transactions. Trends and divergences from price are the signal — rising activity during a correction, for example, suggests strengthening underlying demand.
    Failure modes: One user can control thousands of addresses, so counts overstate unique users; batching, exchange omnibus wallets and L2 migration all distort comparability across time and chains, and address-count heuristics differ between providers.
    Misconceptions: Active addresses do not equal active users — an exchange can serve millions from a handful of wallets while a coin-mixing user spawns thousands. Raw counts also say nothing about transaction value.
    Example: Glassnode reports roughly 700,000-750,000 unique BTC addresses active per day in recent readings; a sustained climb from ~2M toward 3M+ on Tron during a price pullback was read as usage diverging positively from price.
    Aliases: daily active addresses, DAA

    Transaction count #

    Transaction count is the total number of successful on-chain transactions over a period — the basic measure of how much settlement activity a blockchain processes.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is the number of checks the ledger cleared today, regardless of their size.
    Mechanics: Glassnode defines the metric as the total number of successful transactions per interval (hour/day); it reflects demand for block space but not economic weight, since a $10 payment and a $100M transfer each count once. Fee levels, mempool congestion and batching practices all interact with raw counts.
    Failure modes: Counts are trivially inflated by spam, inscription-style data embedding or self-transfers, so raw transaction count is a weak measure of economic activity — CDD and transfer-value metrics exist partly to fix this; exchange batching suppresses counts even as volume grows.
    Misconceptions: More transactions do not mean more adoption or more value moved — bot activity and dust spam inflate counts. Compare value-weighted or age-weighted metrics before drawing conclusions.
    Example: Bitcoin processes on the order of 500,000-650,000 successful transactions per day; during inscription booms, counts spiked sharply while transfer value stayed flat — activity up, economic throughput unchanged.
    Aliases: tx count, number of transactions

    Hash rate #

    Hash rate is the estimated total computational power that miners devote to a proof-of-work network, expressed in hashes per second; it is the primary gauge of PoW security expenditure.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is the horsepower of the mining fleet: the more hashes per second the network throws at the puzzle, the costlier it is for an attacker to rewrite history.
    Mechanics: Hash rate is not directly observable on-chain — it is inferred from realized block intervals against current difficulty (Glassnode: average estimated hashes/second produced by miners). Difficulty retargets keep block time constant as hash rate fluctuates, so price-driven miner entry/exit shows up as hash-rate trends with a lag.
    Failure modes: The metric is an estimate with error bars, especially over short windows (block-interval luck); falling hash rate after price crashes reflects miner capitulation but can be misread as failing security while absolute security remains high.
    Misconceptions: Higher hash rate does not speed up transactions — difficulty retargets to keep block time constant; it raises attack cost, not throughput. Hash rate also does not measure decentralization: a few pools can control most of it.
    Example: Bitcoin's mean hash rate reached roughly 1.1 × 10^21 hashes per second (~1,100 EH/s) in recent Glassnode readings; reversing even a few blocks against that wall of computation is economically out of reach for any realistic attacker.
    Hash rate ≈ difficulty × 2^32 / average block interval (Bitcoin); units: hashes/s (networks now in EH/s).
    Aliases: hashrate, network hash rate

    Staking ratio #

    Staking ratio is the percentage of a proof-of-stake network's eligible token supply that is actively staked, indicating how much capital currently secures consensus and how much remains liquid.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is the share of the town's wealth posted as security bonds: higher means more skin in the game, but also less free capital circulating in the economy.
    Mechanics: Computed as staked supply / total (or circulating) supply from on-chain staking data. Per-validator rewards decline as total stake grows (Ethereum's base reward scales with the inverse square root of total staked ETH), so the ratio self-regulates around an equilibrium where marginal yield matches holders' opportunity cost; liquid staking raises the practical ratio by removing the lockup deterrent.
    Failure modes: A high ratio achieved mostly through a few custodial or liquid-staking providers concentrates validator control despite the healthy headline number; ratio changes lag price moves, and cross-chain comparisons ignore different unbonding periods and slashing regimes.
    Misconceptions: A higher staking ratio is not unambiguously safer — what matters is the distribution of stake across independent operators, not the aggregate. The ratio also does not measure network usage or demand for the token.
    Example: If ~35M of ~120M ETH is staked, the staking ratio is ~29%; each validator's yield falls as that number climbs, which is why yields compress after liquid-staking-driven staking booms.
    Staking ratio = staked supply / total supply × 100%.
    Aliases: staking rate, percent staked

    Funding rate #

    The funding rate is a periodic payment exchanged directly between long and short holders of a perpetual futures contract, sized by the gap between the perpetual's price and the underlying spot index, designed to pull the perpetual price back toward spot.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Perpetuals have no expiry to force convergence, so the market rents a tether instead: whoever is on the crowded, premium side pays the other side every few hours.
    Mechanics: Funding amount = position notional (mark price × size) × funding rate. When the perp trades above spot the rate is positive and longs pay shorts; below spot it is negative and shorts pay longs. On Binance the rate combines a fixed interest component (0.01% per 8h interval by default) with a premium index computed from impact bid/ask prices vs the index, clamped within bounds; only positions open at the funding timestamp pay/receive.
    Failure modes: Deeply negative or positive funding in stressed markets makes holding the paying side ruinously expensive, forcing exits that amplify the move; funding is venue-specific, and extreme events can change intervals or caps with little notice.
    Misconceptions: Funding is not an exchange fee — it is a peer-to-peer transfer (Binance takes no cut), and you only pay or receive it if you hold the position at the exact funding timestamps. Positive funding is not 'free yield' for shorts; it compensates for being on the losing side of crowded positioning.
    Example: BTC perp at $102,000 vs $100,000 index: positive funding, say 0.01% per 8h — a $1M long pays $100 to shorts each interval (~0.9%/month annualized if persistent), incentivizing shorts and arbitrage that close the premium.
    Funding = notional × funding rate; Premium Index = [max(0, impactBid − index) − max(0, index − impactAsk)] / index.
    Aliases: funding, perp funding

    Predicted funding rate #

    The predicted (estimated) funding rate is the exchange's real-time forecast of the funding rate that will apply at the next funding timestamp, computed from the premium index samples collected so far in the current interval.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is the meter running on the next funding bill: it shows what longs or shorts would pay if the interval ended right now.
    Mechanics: Binance continuously samples the premium index (impact bid/ask vs price index, roughly every minute) over the current 8-hour window; the displayed predicted rate is the estimate from samples to date — note the convention that the rate shown at e.g. 09:00 UTC uses the premium dataset from 01:00 to 09:00, not just the final hour. The realized rate is fixed only at the funding timestamp.
    Failure modes: The prediction moves with order-book depth: thin or spoofed books late in the interval can swing the final rate away from hours of stable estimates, so positioning on the predicted rate carries estimate risk.
    Misconceptions: The predicted rate is not locked in — only the rate at the funding timestamp applies, and late premium swings can change it materially. A persistent large prediction is a signal about crowded positioning, not a guaranteed payment.
    Example: If the premium index has averaged +0.02% through six hours of the interval, the predicted funding shows ~+0.02%+interest; a sharp sell-off in the last hour can drag the realized rate to +0.005% instead.
    Predicted funding ≈ time-weighted average of premium index samples in the current interval + interest component (venue-specific).
    Aliases: estimated funding rate
    Related:
    • Funding rate
    • Funding interval
    • Perpetual basis
    Source: Binance Support - Introduction to Binance Futures Funding Rates — How are funding rates calculated

    Funding interval #

    The funding interval is the fixed period between funding payments on a perpetual futures contract — the clock that determines how often the funding rate is applied to open positions.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is the billing cycle of the perp tether: every interval, the payment between longs and shorts settles and the meter resets.
    Mechanics: Binance settles funding every 8 hours at 00:00, 08:00 and 16:00 UTC; only positions open at those timestamps pay or receive (with a ~15-second execution deviation), so closing before the timestamp avoids the charge entirely. In extreme volatility venues can shorten the interval (e.g. to 4h or 1h) to tighten the peg; the default interest component (0.03%/day) is split per interval (0.01% per 8h).
    Failure modes: Interval changes during stress (announced per venue rules) alter the carry economics of open positions mid-trade; timing games around timestamps (closing seconds before, reopening after) add noise and can distort the rate's anchoring function.
    Misconceptions: Intervals are venue- and contract-specific, not universal — assuming 8h everywhere misprices carry; and funding accrues only at the timestamps, not continuously: intraday holding between timestamps costs no funding.
    Example: A trader opening a BTC long at 08:00:05 UTC still pays the 08:00 funding (within the 15-second deviation window), while one who closed at 07:59 pays nothing regardless of how large the rate is.
    Aliases: funding period, funding frequency
    Related:
    • Funding rate
    • Predicted funding rate
    • Perpetual basis

    Perpetual basis #

    The perpetual basis is the price difference between a perpetual futures contract and the underlying spot (index) price, typically expressed as a percentage; the funding mechanism exists to keep this basis mean-reverting around zero.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is how far the tail (perp) wags from the dog (spot): positive when perp buyers pay a premium, negative when perps trade at a discount.
    Mechanics: Basis = perp price − index price (or (perp/index) − 1). Unlike dated futures, whose basis converges at expiry, perp basis is anchored continuously by funding: a persistent positive basis raises funding, charging longs and attracting cash-and-carry arbitrage (buy spot, short perp, collect funding) that compresses it. Binance's premium index operationalizes the basis using impact bid/ask prices rather than last trade.
    Failure modes: In liquidation cascades or index outages, the basis can gap violently (perp trading far below spot) before funding arbitrage restores it; basis also embeds the premium index's order-book sampling, so thin books give noisy readings.
    Misconceptions: A wide positive basis is not a free carry trade — funding can flip or spike, and execution of both legs has costs and margin risk. Perp basis is not a forecast of spot direction; it primarily reflects positioning imbalance.
    Example: BTC spot index at $100,000 while the perp prints $100,500: basis +0.5%; funding turns positive, and arbitrageurs shorting the perp against spot longs collect ~0.01% per 8h until the premium closes.
    Basis % = (perp price / spot index − 1) × 100; annualized carry ≈ average funding rate × 3 × 365 (8h interval).
    Aliases: perp premium, futures-spot basis (perp)

    Liquidation cascade #

    A liquidation cascade is a self-reinforcing chain of forced position closures: falling prices trigger liquidations whose forced market sells push prices lower still, triggering the next tier of liquidations in rapid succession.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is an avalanche on a leveraged mountainside: each margin call knocks loose more snow, and the falling snow triggers the margin calls below.
    Mechanics: Leveraged positions have liquidation prices set by maintenance margin (or health factors in DeFi); when price crosses a dense cluster of them, engines and liquidators market-sell collateral/positions into the book, deepening the move and hitting the next cluster. Open interest collapses, funding and basis gap, and thin order books amplify each leg — visible in Coin Metrics-style analyses combining OI, funding and liquidation data.
    Failure modes: Cascades overshoot any fundamental value — price can wick far below fair levels before recovering (the 'liquidation hunt' zone); exchange risk systems (ADL, insurance funds) socialize residual losses when liquidations cannot fill in time.
    Misconceptions: Cascades are not proof of manipulation — they are the mechanical consequence of stacked leverage at similar levels, though actors can deliberately push price toward liquidation clusters ('stop hunting'). High leverage in a market is the fuel; any spark works.
    Example: BTC drops 3% to $97,000 where a dense band of 25x longs liquidates; their forced selling drives $94,000, liquidating 10x longs, then $90,000 — billions in open interest wiped in an hour before buyers absorb the flow.
    Aliases: liquidation spiral, forced liquidation chain

    Open interest leverage ratio #

    The open-interest leverage ratio compares total derivatives open interest to the underlying market's size (spot market cap or exchange reserves), gauging how much leveraged positioning the market carries relative to its real capital base.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is the debt-to-equity ratio of the whole market: how big the tower of leveraged bets is compared to the pile of actual coins it leans on.
    Mechanics: Open interest is the count of outstanding, unsettled derivatives contracts (Coin Metrics collects it per market, ~1-minute cadence, noting venues report one- or two-sided conventions differently). Ratios such as futures OI / market cap or OI / exchange reserve estimate aggregate leverage; elevated readings mean liquidation cascades can move spot violently, and sharp OI drops mark deleveraging events.
    Failure modes: OI conventions differ by venue (one- vs two-sided, contract units), so cross-exchange aggregation can misstate leverage; high OI alone is ambiguous — hedged and arbitrage positions inflate it without directional risk, and spot inflows can quietly lower the ratio.
    Misconceptions: Rising OI is not inherently bearish or bullish — it is fuel, not direction; the risk comes from OI concentration near the current price with one-sided positioning. OI is also not 'money in futures'; it is the stock of open contracts.
    Example: If BTC futures OI is $40B against a $1T market cap (4%), a 5% spot move forces limited liquidation flow; if OI is $120B (12%) with funding at extremes, the same move can trigger multi-billion cascades.
    OI leverage ratio ≈ futures open interest (USD) / spot market cap (USD) [or / exchange reserve].
    Aliases: OI/market cap ratio, estimated leverage ratio
    Related:

    Long-short ratio #

    The long-short ratio is the proportion of long to short positioning in a derivatives market — reported by exchanges either by account count or by position size — used as a sentiment and crowding gauge.
    Intuition: It is the seating chart of the derivatives arena: how many traders are on the bullish side versus the bearish side, and how lopsided the crowd has become.
    Mechanics: Computed as longs / shorts; e.g. a BTCUSDT ratio of 1.8145 means 1.81 long positions per short (64.5% long, 35.5% short). Venues publish variants: all accounts, top-trader positions (dollar-weighted) and top-trader accounts (count-weighted), which can diverge usefully when whales lean one way and the crowd the other. Extreme readings are often used contrarian.
    Failure modes: The ratio says nothing about leverage size per account (count-based) or hedged books — a 'long' account may be hedged short elsewhere; venue-specific populations (retail-heavy Binance vs institutional CME) make cross-venue comparison meaningless.
    Misconceptions: A high ratio is not a bullish signal to follow — extreme one-sidedness historically precedes squeezes in the opposite direction (contrarian use). Account-count ratios also don't measure conviction; one whale can outweigh thousands of minnows in notional terms.
    Example: Binance BTCUSDT top-trader ratio at 2.5 (dollar-weighted) with funding at +0.05% signals crowded, leveraged longs — a setup where a small dip can trigger outsized long liquidations.
    Long/short ratio = number (or size) of long positions / number (or size) of short positions.
    Aliases: long/short ratio, LS ratio

    Cross margin #

    Cross margin is a margin mode in which all available balance in the margin/futures account is shared as collateral across every open position, so unrealized profits and the whole account equity support each position's maintenance margin.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is one shared life raft for all your trades: a losing position can drain the entire account to stay afloat — and if it sinks, everything goes down with it.
    Mechanics: Under cross margin, the account's total margin balance (wallet balance + unrealized PnL) counts toward every position's maintenance requirement, making individual positions harder to liquidate but exposing the entire account to any single failure. Leverage can be adjusted on open cross positions (on Binance); liquidation occurs when account equity falls below the maintenance margin (notional × maintenance margin rate − maintenance amount, per tier).
    Failure modes: A single oversized losing position can consume the equity supporting every other position, turning one bad trade into a whole-account liquidation; correlated losses across positions hit the shared pool simultaneously.
    Misconceptions: Cross margin does not reduce risk — it redistributes it: positions are individually safer but the account as a whole is more exposed. Adding leverage across many cross positions multiplies hidden total exposure beyond any single position's setting.
    Example: With $10,000 in a cross account and two 5x positions of $20,000 each, a drawdown on one position is buffered by the full $10,000; but if combined maintenance margin reaches $10,000, both positions liquidate together.
    Maintenance margin = notional position value × maintenance margin rate − maintenance amount (tiered, per Binance).
    Aliases: cross margin mode, cross collateral
    Related:

    Isolated margin #

    Isolated margin is a margin mode in which each position carries its own dedicated collateral allotment, capping the maximum loss of that position at the margin assigned to it and ring-fencing the rest of the account.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is a fire door between trades: each position burns with only the fuel you loaded into it, never spreading to the rest of the account.
    Mechanics: The trader assigns a fixed margin amount per position (and can manually add more); the position's liquidation price is computed from that isolated allotment against the maintenance margin requirement. If breached, only the isolated margin is lost — other positions and wallet balance are untouched. On Binance, leverage on open isolated positions cannot be reduced, and each isolated margin account/pair maintains its own independent risk level.
    Failure modes: Isolated positions liquidate much earlier than cross positions because they lack the account buffer — a normal wick can take out the margin; over-reliance on manual margin top-ups fails exactly in fast markets when you cannot react.
    Misconceptions: Isolated margin limits loss per position, not total risk: running many isolated positions still sums to large account exposure. The 'cap' is the assigned margin plus any added along the way, not the initial amount if you keep topping up.
    Example: With $10,000 in the account, you open a 10x long with $500 isolated margin ($5,000 position); a ~10% adverse move liquidates the position for a $500 loss while the remaining $9,500 stays intact.
    Aliases: isolated margin mode
