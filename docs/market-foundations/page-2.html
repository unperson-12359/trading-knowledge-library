    Pre-market session reviewed #

    Extended-hours trading that occurs before the regular session opens; in U.S. equities, pre-market trading typically begins as early as 4:00 a.m. ET and runs until the 9:30 a.m. open. It is part of what regulators call extended-hours or after-hours trading — trading outside regular trading hours.
    Intuition: The warm-up window: overnight news gets traded before the official open, but with fewer participants and wider spreads.
    Mechanics: Trades execute on ECNs and exchanges' extended sessions (Nasdaq accepts extended-hours orders from 4:00 a.m. ET); many order types are unavailable, LULD bands do not apply in the same way, and the pre-market flow feeds into the opening auction's indicative prices.
    Failure modes: Thin liquidity exaggerates moves: pre-market prices can gap and reverse violently at the open, and executions resting on stale overnight references can be far from where the stock will actually open.
    Misconceptions: The pre-market 'price' is not the opening price — the official open is set by the opening auction, and heavy pre-market moves routinely fade once full liquidity arrives.
    Example: A company reports earnings at 7:00 a.m. ET; its shares trade up 8% in the pre-market session before the 9:30 a.m. Opening Cross sets the official opening price.
    Aliases: Pre-market trading, Early session

    After-hours session reviewed #

    Extended-hours trading that occurs after the regular session closes; per the SEC, after-hours trading (also called extended-hours trading) is trading outside regular trading hours, which for U.S. exchange-listed stocks end at 4:00 p.m. ET. Late sessions on U.S. equity venues typically run until 8:00 p.m. ET.
    Intuition: Trading continues after the closing bell, letting investors react to late news, but in a thinner, jumpier market.
    Mechanics: After the closing cross completes in a stock, after-hours trading commences on ECNs and exchange late sessions with reduced order-type support and no LULD bands; executions report to the tape but do not change the official closing price.
    Failure modes: Low volume and wide spreads make after-hours prices unreliable guides to the next day's open; a stock can move sharply on a few hundred shares, and stop or market orders can fill at extreme prices.
    Misconceptions: An after-hours trade does not change 'the closing price' — the official close was set by the 4:00 p.m. closing auction — and after-hours gains or losses frequently reverse at the next open.
    Example: A company issues a profit warning at 4:30 p.m. ET; the stock drops 12% in the after-hours session on light volume, while the tape's official closing price remains the 4:00 p.m. cross.
    Aliases: After-hours trading, Post-market session, Late session

    Overnight session reviewed #

    Trading that occurs outside a market's daytime session, spanning the evening, night, and early morning; in U.S.-based futures markets, the overnight session on CME Globex runs from the evening open (e.g., 6:30 p.m. ET) through to the next day's close, encompassing Asian and European hours.
    Intuition: Futures markets barely sleep: after the U.S. day ends, trading hands off to Asia and Europe before the next U.S. morning.
    Mechanics: Electronic venues like CME Globex operate near-24-hour schedules with a daily maintenance break; activity is commonly described by regional sessions (Asia, London, U.S.), and the overnight session establishes the price levels at which the daytime session will open.
    Failure modes: Overnight liquidity is thinner and headline-sensitive — weekend gaps and overnight spikes can be sharp, and risk limits sized for daytime volatility can be breached while staffing is minimal.
    Misconceptions: The overnight session is not a separate market with separate prices — it is the same continuous contract — but its price action still belongs to the same or next trade date, which confuses P&L and chart construction.
    Example: E-mini S&P 500 futures reopen at 6:00 p.m. ET Sunday on CME Globex; the overnight session trades through Asian and European hours before U.S. equity markets open Monday at 9:30 a.m.
    Aliases: Globex session, Night session, Evening session

    Opening range reviewed #

    The price range established during the initial part of a trading session — commonly the first minutes or the first hour — used by traders as a reference for the session's early balance between buyers and sellers. The concept is institutionalized in exchange terminology (trades made 'on opening' occur within the opening price range) and in intraday frameworks such as opening range breakout analysis, which are analytical conventions rather than proven predictive laws.
    Intuition: The high and low of the first stretch of trading sketch the day's initial battle lines; traders watch what happens when price later leaves that box.
    Mechanics: Record the high and low over a chosen opening window (e.g., first 5, 15, or 30 minutes of the regular session); subsequent breaks above or below that range are used as reference or signal levels in intraday analysis, with the choice of window being a convention, not a rule.
    Failure modes: The window choice is arbitrary and the levels are self-referential: in trendless or news-driven days the opening range is immediately swept or meaningless, and breakout signals degrade when many traders key off the same levels.
    Misconceptions: There is no official or universal opening-range definition — 5, 15, 30, and 60 minutes are all in use — and a breakout of the opening range is a descriptive event, not a validated predictor of the day's direction.
    Example: A trader marks the S&P 500 E-mini's high and low from 9:30-10:00 a.m. ET; a sustained move above that band later in the morning is treated as an upside range extension.
    OR_high = max(High_t) and OR_low = min(Low_t) for t in the chosen opening window (e.g., first 30 minutes of RTH); OR width = OR_high - OR_low.
    Aliases: OR, Opening price range

    Initial balance provisional #

    In Market Profile analysis (developed by J. Peter Steidlmayer at the Chicago Board of Trade in the 1980s), the price range established during the first hour of the regular trading session — the first two 30-minute TPO periods — taken to represent the range set by short-term, day-timeframe traders before longer-term participants extend it. It is an analytical convention within the Market Profile framework, not a proven predictive law.
    Intuition: The first hour's high and low mark the territory day traders carve out; whether later trading holds or breaks that range tells you whether bigger money is participating.
    Mechanics: On a Market Profile (TPO) chart, the session is divided into 30-minute periods labeled with letters; the initial balance is the price range covered by periods A and B of the regular session. Moves beyond the IB high or low are called range extensions and are used to gauge initiative buying or selling.
    Failure modes: The one-hour convention fits the pit-era U.S. day session for which it was designed; in near-24-hour electronic markets the 'first hour' is a design choice, and IB-based rules lose meaning on days dominated by a single scheduled news event.
    Misconceptions: The initial balance is not an official exchange statistic and has no universal definition across platforms (some use the first hour, others the first two TPO periods of a user-defined session); IB extensions describe structure, they do not predict direction with verified accuracy.
    Example: If CBOT-era bond futures trade between 98-04 and 98-20 in the first hour, that band is the initial balance; a move above 98-20 in period C is an upside range extension attributed to longer-term buyers.
    IB_high = max price traded during the first two 30-minute periods (first hour) of RTH; IB_low = min price over the same window; range extension = any trade beyond IB_high or IB_low in later periods.

    Settlement cycle reviewed #

    The standard interval between the trade date and the settlement date for a class of transactions, conventionally written T+n (trade date plus n business days). The SEC shortened the standard settlement cycle for most U.S. broker-dealer securities transactions from T+2 to T+1, effective May 28, 2024.
    Intuition: The plumbing delay between agreeing a trade and actually exchanging cash for securities — the shorter it is, the less can go wrong in between.
    Mechanics: During the cycle, trades are matched, confirmed, affirmed, and cleared; obligations are netted and margined; on settlement day, securities and funds move, typically delivery-versus-payment. Shortening the cycle compresses the window in which counterparty, market, and liquidity risks can accumulate.
    Failure modes: Shorter cycles squeeze operational timelines: affirmation, securities lending recalls, and cross-timezone FX funding (for foreign investors buying U.S. assets) must complete faster, raising fail risk for unprepared participants.
    Misconceptions: T+1 does not mean you wait a day to use your shares or proceeds in all cases — brokers may credit accounts at execution — and the cycle is a market-standard maximum convention, with same-day and negotiated settlements also existing.
    Example: A U.S. stock trade executed on Monday, June 3, 2024 settles on Tuesday, June 4 (T+1); the same trade in 2023 would have settled on Wednesday (T+2).
    Settlement date = trade date T + n business days (n = 1 for most U.S. securities since May 28, 2024; n = 2 previously and still in some markets and instruments).
    Aliases: T+1, T+2, Settlement period

    Netting reviewed #

    The offsetting of multiple obligations between parties so that only the net balance must be settled, replacing many gross transfers with a smaller number of net payments or deliveries. Netting may be bilateral (between two parties) or multilateral (across many participants, typically at a central counterparty).
    Intuition: If I owe you $100 and you owe me $80, we settle $20 one way instead of moving $180 around — scaled up to thousands of trades, this shrinks what actually has to move.
    Mechanics: A clearinghouse or settlement system aggregates each participant's buys, sells, payments, and receipts per instrument and currency, computes a single net position or obligation per participant, and settles only those net amounts, drastically reducing settlement volume and liquidity needs.
    Failure modes: Netting concentrates and redistributes risk into the netting system: its legal enforceability in insolvency is critical (close-out netting rights), and netting reduces gross exposures while leaving the net exposure to the CCP intact.
    Misconceptions: Netting does not cancel economic exposure or P&L — positions and market risk remain; only the settlement flows are compressed.
    Example: If a bank's futures desk buys 1,000 contracts and sells 950 of the same contract during the day, the clearinghouse settles margin and variation on the net 50-contract position rather than processing 1,950 gross deliveries.
    Net obligation of participant i = sum of amounts due to i minus sum of amounts due from i (per instrument/currency); only the residual is settled.
    Aliases: Multilateral netting, Bilateral netting

    Novation reviewed #

    The legal substitution, upon clearing, of the original contract between buyer and seller with two new contracts in which the central counterparty becomes the buyer to the seller and the seller to the buyer. Novation replaces mutual counterparty exposure among traders with exposure to the clearinghouse.
    Intuition: Once your trade clears, the person on the other side legally disappears — your contract is now with the clearinghouse, and so is theirs.
    Mechanics: When a trade is accepted for clearing, the clearing organization discharges the original bilateral contract and creates matched contracts with itself as central counterparty; this enables multilateral netting and lets either party exit by offsetting, regardless of the original counterparty's identity.
    Failure modes: Novation centralizes credit risk in the CCP, so its benefit depends entirely on the CCP's risk management; in legal disputes, whether and when novation occurred (trade acceptance timing) can determine who bears a default.
    Misconceptions: Novation does not make markets risk-free — it substitutes a single, well-collateralized counterparty for many bilateral ones — and uncleared (bilateral) trades never receive this substitution.
    Example: Two CME clearing members execute a Eurodollar futures trade with each other; upon clearing, CME Clearing becomes the seller to the buyer and the buyer to the seller, and the members no longer bear each other's credit risk.
    Aliases: Open offer (equivalent mechanism)

    Delivery versus payment reviewed #

    A securities settlement mechanism that links the transfer of securities to the transfer of funds so that delivery occurs if and only if payment occurs, eliminating principal risk — the risk of delivering securities without receiving the money, or vice versa.
    Intuition: The shares and the cash swap hands atomically: neither side can end up having given away its asset without receiving the other side of the deal.
    Mechanics: The settlement system conditions the final transfer of securities on the final transfer of funds within the same process (e.g., DTC book-entry delivery against a simultaneous settlement debit/credit); models range from gross-gross (DvP model 1) to net-net (DvP model 3) in BIS terminology.
    Failure modes: DvP removes principal risk but not liquidity or replacement-cost risk: a counterparty can still fail to deliver, forcing a buy-in, and gridlock can arise when participants wait for incoming securities to fund outgoing deliveries.
    Misconceptions: DvP does not guarantee the trade happens — it guarantees that if one leg settles, the other does too; a failed trade under DvP simply does not settle either leg.
    Example: When an investor's share purchase settles T+1 at DTC, the seller's account is credited cash and debited shares in the same settlement pass, so the seller never parts with the stock unpaid.
    Aliases: DvP, DVP

    Payment versus payment reviewed #

    A foreign exchange settlement mechanism that ensures the final transfer of one currency occurs if and only if the final transfer of the countercurrency occurs, eliminating FX settlement (Herstatt) risk — the loss of the full value of a currency paid without receiving the currency bought.
    Intuition: Both currency legs of an FX trade move simultaneously; you can never wire your dollars away and get no euros back because the other bank failed that afternoon.
    Mechanics: A PvP system (notably CLS Bank, per BIS analysis of FX settlement risk) holds both sides' payment instructions and releases the two currency transfers together; where PvP is unavailable, legs settle in different payment systems and time zones, exposing the paying party until the counterleg arrives.
    Failure modes: PvP eliminates principal risk but not liquidity risk (a party may fail to fund its side, unwinding the day's queue) or replacement-cost risk (re-doing the trade at a worse rate); coverage gaps leave non-eligible currencies and counterparties exposed.
    Misconceptions: PvP is not automatic in FX — it requires settlement through a PvP mechanism such as CLS, and a large share of global FX turnover historically settled with one leg at risk (the 1974 Bankhaus Herstatt failure being the canonical loss).
    Example: A USD/EUR trade settles through CLS: the member's dollar payment and euro receipt are released simultaneously at settlement, so neither bank is exposed for the full principal if the other fails intraday.
    Aliases: PvP, PVP
