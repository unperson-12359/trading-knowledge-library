    Secured Overnight Financing Rate #

    A broad measure of the cost of borrowing cash overnight collateralized by U.S. Treasury securities, published each business day by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. It is the principal U.S. dollar benchmark that replaced LIBOR for derivatives and loans.
    Intuition: It is the going rate for a one-day loan backed by Treasuries — essentially a near-risk-free overnight borrowing cost built from actual repo market transactions.
    Mechanics: SOFR is calculated as a volume-weighted median of transaction-level tri-party repo data, GCF Repo data, and bilateral Treasury repo cleared through FICC's DVP service (filtered to remove specials), and is published at approximately 8:00 a.m. ET each business day.
    Failure modes: Because it is secured and transaction-based, SOFR can spike when repo funding is scarce (e.g. September 2019) and reflects collateral market conditions rather than bank credit risk; unlike LIBOR it has no term or credit component, complicating its use in loan pricing.
    Misconceptions: SOFR is not set by the Federal Reserve as a policy rate, nor is it a forecast — it is a backward-looking measure of realized overnight repo transactions.
    Example: A floating-rate note might pay 'SOFR + 80 bp', resetting its coupon off the daily-published SOFR benchmark.

    Federal funds rate #

    The interest rate at which depository institutions lend reserve balances to other depository institutions overnight, uncollateralized. The FOMC sets a target rate or range for it, and the New York Fed publishes the effective federal funds rate (EFFR) as the volume-weighted median of actual transactions.
    Intuition: It is the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans of their reserve balances at the Fed — the rate U.S. monetary policy directly targets.
    Mechanics: The federal funds market consists of domestic unsecured overnight borrowings in U.S. dollars by depository institutions from other institutions, primarily GSEs. The EFFR is computed as a volume-weighted median of transactions reported on the FR 2420 report and published for the prior business day at about 9:00 a.m.; the Desk's operations keep it within the FOMC's target range.
    Failure modes: The fed funds market is thin and dominated by specific players (GSEs lending to banks), so the EFFR can be unrepresentative of marginal funding costs; the rate also lost its role as the sole steering variable once abundant reserves made administered rates the main control tools.
    Misconceptions: The Fed does not directly set the market fed funds rate by decree — it sets a target range and uses operations and administered rates to steer the traded rate into it.
    Example: With an FOMC target range of 3.50–3.75%, the EFFR typically prints near the middle of the range, e.g. 3.62%.
    Aliases: fed funds rate, EFFR (effective federal funds rate)

    Repo rate #

    The implicit interest rate on a repurchase agreement, a transaction in which one party sells securities and agrees to repurchase them later at a slightly higher price — economically a loan collateralized by securities. The rate is set by the difference between the sale and repurchase prices relative to the term.
    Intuition: It is the cost of borrowing cash against bonds: you hand over the securities, get cash, and pay back a bit more later — that bit more, annualized, is the repo rate.
    Mechanics: In a repo the cash borrower sells securities and commits to repurchase them at a predetermined higher price; the price gap implies the repo interest. General collateral (GC) rates apply to non-specific securities, while repos on particular issues in high demand ('specials') trade at lower rates. The New York Fed uses repo and reverse repo operations to help keep the fed funds rate in its target range.
    Failure modes: Repo rates can spike abruptly when collateral or dealer balance-sheet capacity is scarce (September 2019, March 2020), transmitting stress across funding markets; specialness can also drive individual-issue repo rates far below GC, distorting funding-cost readings.
    Misconceptions: A repo is not an outright sale of the bond for risk-transfer purposes — the seller retains the economic exposure and the obligation to repurchase, so it functions as secured borrowing.
    Example: A dealer sells $100 million of Treasuries overnight and agrees to repurchase them for $100.015 million; the implied overnight repo rate is about 5.4% annualized (0.015/100 × 360).
    Repo rate = ((repurchase price − sale price) / sale price) × (360 or 365 / term in days), per the market's day-count convention.

    Haircut #

    The percentage reduction applied to the market value of collateral when determining how much cash can be borrowed against it. In a repo, the haircut is the gap between the collateral's value and the cash lent, protecting the lender against a fall in collateral value before it could be sold.
    Intuition: If you pledge $1 million of bonds with a 5% haircut, the lender only gives you $950,000 — the haircut is the lender's safety cushion.
    Mechanics: Cash lent = collateral market value × (1 − haircut). Haircuts are set per asset class based on price volatility, liquidity, credit quality, and borrower risk; riskier or less liquid collateral receives larger haircuts.
    Failure modes: Haircuts set in calm times can prove inadequate in stressed markets when collateral gaps down in value; conversely, abrupt haircut increases force deleveraging and can propagate fire sales, as seen in 2008.
    Misconceptions: A haircut is not a fee or interest charge — it reduces the amount lent, not the price of the loan, and the full collateral value (less the loan) is returned when the repo unwinds.
    Example: Pledging $1,000,000 of Treasury bonds at a 2% haircut lets the borrower draw $980,000 in cash; the extra $20,000 of collateral covers the lender if bond prices fall.
    Loan amount = collateral value × (1 − h), where h is the haircut (e.g. h = 0.05 for 5%).
    Aliases: margin (in collateral lending)

    Term premium #

    The extra yield investors demand for holding a longer-term bond instead of rolling over a series of short-term instruments — compensation for the risk that future rates and inflation differ from expectations. It is the difference between a long-term yield and the expected average of future short rates, and it is not directly observable.
    Intuition: Locking your money up for ten years is riskier than renewing a one-year loan ten times; the term premium is the extra pay for bearing that risk.
    Mechanics: Estimated with term-structure models: a model (e.g. the Fed's Kim–Wright three-factor arbitrage-free model, published on FRED as THREEFYTP10) decomposes observed yields into expected short-rate paths plus a residual term premium. Estimates are model-dependent and can be negative.
    Failure modes: Term premium estimates vary substantially across models and can flip sign with specification changes; using any single estimate as ground truth for 'rich/cheap' bonds is fragile.
    Misconceptions: The term premium is not a measured quantity like a yield — it is a model-implied residual, and a rising long yield does not tell you whether expectations or the premium moved without a model.
    Example: If the 10-year Treasury yields 4.5% while the expected average short rate over the decade is 4.0%, the model-implied 10-year term premium is about 0.5%.
    y(T) ≈ (1/T) × Σ E[short rate_t] + TP(T), where y(T) is the T-year yield and TP(T) the term premium.

    Breakeven inflation #

    The inflation rate that would make an investor indifferent between holding a nominal Treasury and a same-maturity Treasury Inflation-Protected Security (TIPS), computed as the nominal yield minus the real (TIPS) yield. It is a market-based measure of expected average inflation over the horizon, though it also embeds inflation risk and liquidity premia.
    Intuition: If nominal bonds pay 4.5% and inflation-protected bonds pay 2.0%, the market is pricing roughly 2.5% average inflation — that gap is the breakeven.
    Mechanics: Computed from constant-maturity series, e.g. FRED's T10YIE = 10-Year Treasury Constant Maturity (DGS10) minus 10-Year Treasury Inflation-Indexed Constant Maturity (DFII10); the latest value implies what market participants expect inflation to average over the next 10 years.
    Failure modes: Breakevens mix inflation expectations with inflation risk premia and the liquidity disadvantage of TIPS, so they can diverge from true expectations — e.g. they collapsed in 2008 partly because TIPS became deeply illiquid, not because deflation was genuinely expected.
    Misconceptions: Breakeven inflation is not a pure forecast of CPI; it is the rate that equates two bond prices, so reading it as the market's literal inflation expectation ignores embedded premia.
    Example: With the 10-year nominal Treasury at 4.3% and the 10-year TIPS real yield at 2.0%, the 10-year breakeven inflation rate is about 2.3%.
    Breakeven inflation ≈ y(nominal, T) − y(real TIPS, T); exactly, (1 + y_nom)/(1 + y_real) − 1 under compounding.
    Aliases: breakeven inflation rate, TIPS breakeven

    Inflation swap #

    A derivative in which one party pays a fixed rate and the other pays the realized change in an inflation index (e.g. U.S. CPI-U) on a notional amount, transferring inflation risk. In the standard zero-coupon form, both legs are compounded and exchanged as a single payment at maturity.
    Intuition: It is a bet or hedge on inflation itself: one side pays a pre-agreed inflation rate, the other pays whatever inflation actually turns out to be.
    Mechanics: The fixed leg compounds at the agreed fixed rate; the floating leg accrues the realized growth of the reference inflation index (USD uses CPI-U), typically with a publication lag of a few months. In a zero-coupon inflation swap the two compounded amounts are netted and paid once at maturity; the quoted fixed rate is the market's swap-implied inflation level.
    Failure modes: Index publication lags, revisions, and seasonal patterns complicate pricing and hedging; the market is also less liquid than nominal swaps, and quoted rates embed inflation risk premia just like TIPS breakevens.
    Misconceptions: The fixed rate on an inflation swap is not a pure inflation forecast; like breakevens, it includes risk and liquidity premia, so it overstates or understates true expected inflation.
    Example: A pension fund enters a 10-year zero-coupon inflation swap paying a fixed 2.6% and receiving realized CPI-U growth on $50 million notional, hedging its inflation-linked liabilities.
    Fixed leg payment = N × [(1 + k)^n − 1]; inflation leg payment = N × [I(T)/I(0) − 1], where N is notional, k the fixed swap rate, n the tenor in years, and I the inflation index level.
    Aliases: zero-coupon inflation swap, ZCIS
