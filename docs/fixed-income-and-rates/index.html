    Yield curve #

    A curve that plots the yields of bonds of the same credit quality (typically government securities) against their times to maturity at a given point in time. The U.S. Treasury's official version is a par yield curve relating the par yield on a Treasury security to its time to maturity, derived daily from market price quotations.
    Intuition: It is a snapshot of what it costs to borrow money for different lengths of time, showing how the market prices time, risk, and expectations about future rates.
    Mechanics: Market prices of on-the-run securities are converted to yields, and a curve-fitting method (Treasury uses bootstrapping of forward rates plus monotone convex interpolation) produces a continuous curve from which constant-maturity yields are read.
    Failure modes: Curve shapes mix expected future rates, term premia, and supply/demand effects, so the curve alone cannot identify which driver dominates. Fitted curves are also model-dependent: different fitting methods produce slightly different yields at the same maturities.
    Misconceptions: A common mistake is treating the yield curve as a forecast of future rates; Treasury itself states that the curve indicates what rates were and are, not what they will be.
    Example: On a day when the 2-year Treasury yields 4.0% and the 10-year yields 4.3%, the 2s10s segment of the curve is upward sloping by 30 basis points.
    Aliases: term structure of interest rates

    Normal yield curve #

    A yield curve configuration in which longer-maturity yields are higher than shorter-maturity yields, giving the curve an upward slope. It is the historically most common configuration, reflecting term premia and typical expectations that rates will not fall sharply.
    Intuition: Lenders usually demand more compensation for tying up money longer, so longer loans carry higher rates — that is the 'normal' state of the curve.
    Mechanics: The slope is measured as a spread between constant-maturity yields, e.g. the 10-year minus the 2-year Treasury yield; a positive value corresponds to a normal (upward-sloping) curve.
    Failure modes: An upward slope does not prove rates will rise; the slope conflates term premium, rate expectations, and issuance/demand effects, so reading it as a pure forecast is unreliable.
    Misconceptions: It is wrong to assume 'normal' means guaranteed or stable; curves can steepen or flatten quickly, and a normal curve is simply the modal historical shape, not a law.
    Example: If 3-month bills yield 3.5%, the 2-year note yields 4.0%, and the 10-year yields 4.5%, the curve is normal (upward sloping).
    Slope ≈ y(long maturity) − y(short maturity) > 0, e.g. 10-year CMT minus 2-year CMT.
    Aliases: upward-sloping yield curve, positive yield curve

    Inverted yield curve #

    A yield curve configuration in which shorter-maturity yields exceed longer-maturity yields, giving the curve a downward slope. The U.S. Treasury notes that short-term rates can exceed longer-term rates when market expectations about future rates and monetary policy shift; inversions have historically preceded U.S. recessions, though the relationship is a statistical regularity, not a causal law.
    Intuition: Investors accept lower yields on long bonds because they expect rates to fall in the future — often because they expect the central bank to cut rates in a slowdown.
    Mechanics: Measured as a negative term spread, e.g. the 10-year Treasury constant maturity yield minus the 2-year (FRED series T10Y2Y) falling below zero. Inversions often arise when policy tightening pushes short rates up while long yields stay anchored by weak growth expectations.
    Failure modes: Inversions have produced false or very early recession signals, and post-2008 term premium compression (e.g. from quantitative easing) can invert curves without implying a downturn; timing between inversion and recession varies widely.
    Misconceptions: Inversion does not cause recessions, nor does it guarantee one; it is an indicator of market expectations and term premia, not a mechanical trigger.
    Example: In 2022–2023 the 2-year Treasury yield rose above the 10-year yield, leaving the 2s10s spread negative for an extended period.
    Inversion when y(short) − y(long) > 0, e.g. 2-year CMT − 10-year CMT > 0.
    Aliases: negative yield curve

    Flat yield curve #

    A yield curve configuration in which yields are approximately equal across maturities, so the term spread is near zero. It typically appears as a transitional state between an upward-sloping (normal) curve and an inverted curve, or vice versa.
    Intuition: The market is charging roughly the same rate to borrow for one year as for ten years — a sign that expectations about the future are unusually uncertain or in transition.
    Mechanics: Identified when the spread between long- and short-maturity constant-maturity yields (e.g. 10-year minus 2-year Treasury) is close to zero; flattening occurs when short rates rise toward long rates (bear flattener) or long rates fall toward short rates (bull flattener).
    Failure modes: A flat curve carries little standalone signal: it can precede steepening or inversion, and 'flatness' has no precise threshold, so classification is judgment-dependent.
    Misconceptions: A flat curve is not a neutral or 'no information' state in a predictive sense, and it is not a distinct market regime with proven consequences — it is simply a descriptive shape.
    Example: If the 2-year Treasury yields 4.20% and the 10-year yields 4.25%, the 2s10s segment is essentially flat at 5 basis points.
    Flat when |y(long) − y(short)| ≈ 0.

    Spot rate curve #

    The curve of yields on zero-coupon instruments across maturities — the set of rates at which a single future cash flow is discounted to today. It is built by bootstrapping coupon-bearing bond prices so that each instrument is repriced without error, and it is the curve used for present-value calculations.
    Intuition: Instead of one blended yield per bond, the spot curve gives a separate 'pure' interest rate for every single future date, like a price tag for money delivered at each point in time.
    Mechanics: Starting from the shortest maturity, each bond's price is decomposed into its cash flows; the discount rate that prices each successive maturity's cash flow exactly (given already-solved shorter rates) is the spot rate for that maturity. The U.S. Treasury bootstraps instantaneous forward rates from on-the-run prices, which equivalently determines the spot curve; Treasury does not publish a daily zero-coupon curve itself.
    Failure modes: The bootstrapped curve depends on the fitting/interpolation method and the input securities chosen, so different constructions give different spot rates, especially between quoted maturities and at the long end.
    Misconceptions: Spot rates are not directly observable in coupon-bond markets and are not the same as par yields; the par yield curve and the spot curve differ whenever the curve is not flat.
    Example: If the 2-year spot rate is 4.2%, a single $100 payment due in exactly 2 years is worth 100 / (1.042)^2 ≈ $92.10 today.
    P = sum over t of CF_t / (1 + z_t)^t, where z_t is the t-year spot (zero-coupon) rate, CF_t the cash flow at time t, and P the bond's price.
    Aliases: zero-coupon yield curve, zero curve

    Forward rate curve #

    The curve of interest rates implied today for borrowing or lending over future periods, derived from the spot (zero-coupon) curve so that investing to a long horizon equals rolling over at the shorter rate plus the forward rate. The U.S. Treasury's curve construction bootstraps instantaneous forward rates at input maturities as an intermediate step.
    Intuition: If you know the 1-year rate and the 2-year rate, the market is implicitly quoting a rate for year two alone — the forward curve is the set of all such implied future-period rates.
    Mechanics: Forward rates are extracted from spot rates by no-arbitrage: chaining the short spot rate with the forward rate over the remaining period must reproduce the long spot rate. Treasury derives instantaneous forward rates so that input securities are sequentially priced without error.
    Failure modes: Forward rates embed term and risk premia, so they are biased predictors of realized future rates; empirically the expectations hypothesis performs poorly, especially at longer horizons.
    Misconceptions: An implied forward rate is not a market forecast agreed by participants; it is an arithmetic consequence of today's spot curve plus premia, not a prediction.
    Example: If the 1-year spot rate is 4.0% and the 2-year spot rate is 4.5%, the implied 1-year forward rate one year ahead is (1.045^2 / 1.04) − 1 ≈ 5.0%.
    (1 + z_T)^T = (1 + z_t)^t × (1 + f(t,T))^(T−t), so f(t,T) = [ (1 + z_T)^T / (1 + z_t)^t ]^(1/(T−t)) − 1, where z_t, z_T are spot rates and f(t,T) is the forward rate from t to T.
    Aliases: implied forward curve

    Discount factor #

    The present value of one unit of currency received at a specified future date, equivalently the price today of a risk-free zero-coupon payment of 1 at that date. The full set of discount factors across dates is the discount function, which is derived from the spot rate curve.
    Intuition: A discount factor answers: how much is a dollar delivered on a given future date worth right now?
    Mechanics: Each cash flow of an instrument is multiplied by the discount factor for its payment date and summed to get the instrument's present value. Discount factors are obtained from spot rates (or, equivalently, from the bootstrapped forward-rate curve used in curve construction such as Treasury's).
    Failure modes: Because discount factors inherit the chosen curve-fitting method and day-count/compounding conventions, they can differ across vendors even for the same date; using a single yield for all maturities instead of term-specific factors misprices non-standard cash flows.
    Misconceptions: A discount factor is not a 'discount' in the retail sense and is not the same as a coupon rate; it is a present-value weight between 0 and 1 for a specific date.
    Example: With a 2-year spot rate of 4%, the 2-year discount factor is 1 / 1.04^2 ≈ 0.9246, so $1,000 due in 2 years is worth about $924.60 today.
    DF(t) = 1 / (1 + z_t)^t with annual compounding (DF(t) = e^(−z_t × t) under continuous compounding), where z_t is the t-year spot rate; PV = Σ CF_t × DF(t).
    Aliases: present value factor, price of a zero-coupon bond

    Zero-coupon rate #

    The yield earned on an investment that makes a single payment at maturity with no intermediate coupons, for a specific maturity. It is the building block of the spot (zero-coupon) curve used to discount individual future cash flows.
    Intuition: It is the pure interest rate for money delivered at one exact future date, with nothing paid in between — like the rate on a Treasury bill or a STRIP.
    Mechanics: For a zero-coupon instrument the rate follows directly from price and face value; for coupon-bearing markets the zero-coupon rates are bootstrapped from bond prices so all input securities price without error. The U.S. Treasury notes its published par yield curve is a semiannual bond-equivalent curve and that it does not publish daily zero-coupon curve rates.
    Failure modes: Zero-coupon rates are model-derived rather than directly quoted for most maturities, so they inherit bootstrapping and interpolation assumptions; liquidity differences between coupon bonds and STRIPS can also create small inconsistencies.
    Misconceptions: The zero-coupon rate for maturity t is not the yield of the t-year coupon bond; confusing par yields with spot rates produces systematic valuation errors when the curve is sloped.
    Example: A 2-year zero-coupon bond priced at 92.10 per 100 face implies a 2-year zero rate of (100/92.10)^(1/2) − 1 ≈ 4.2%.
    z_t = (F / P)^(1/t) − 1, where P is the price today, F the single payment at maturity, and t the time in years.
    Aliases: spot rate, zero rate

    Par yield #

    The coupon rate at which a hypothetical bond of a given maturity would price exactly at par (100% of face value), given the current discount function. The U.S. Treasury's official daily yield curve is a par yield curve, and its constant-maturity (CMT) rates are par yields read from that curve.
    Intuition: It is the coupon a brand-new bond of a given maturity would have to pay today for investors to accept it at full face value.
    Mechanics: Treasury converts indicative bid-side price quotations on the most recently auctioned securities to yields, bootstraps forward rates, and applies monotone convex interpolation to construct a curve of true par rates; CMT yields are then read at fixed maturities and quoted as semiannual bond-equivalent yields.
    Failure modes: CMT par yields are read from a fitted curve and may not match the actual yield of any specific traded security; the curve also depends on the fitting methodology, which Treasury has changed over time.
    Misconceptions: A par yield is not the same as the spot rate for that maturity, and a CMT rate is not the yield on an actual outstanding bond of exactly that maturity.
    Example: If the fitted 10-year par yield is 4.30%, a new theoretical 10-year bond with a 4.30% coupon would be worth exactly 100.
    Par coupon c solves 100 = Σ_t (c/2) × DF(t) + 100 × DF(T), where DF(t) are discount factors and T is maturity (semiannual coupons).
    Aliases: par rate, par coupon rate

    Yield spread #

    The difference between two yields, typically expressed in basis points. Depending on context it may compare maturities on the same curve (term spread, e.g. 10-year minus 2-year Treasury), or securities of different credit quality at the same maturity (credit spread).
    Intuition: It is the extra (or lesser) yield one instrument offers relative to another, summarizing how the market prices the difference in time, risk, or liquidity between them.
    Mechanics: Computed as a simple subtraction of yields at matched or specified maturities; e.g. FRED's T10Y2Y is the 10-year Treasury constant maturity yield minus the 2-year, and term or credit spreads widen or narrow as relative yields move.
    Failure modes: A raw yield spread ignores differences in maturity, coupon, embedded options, and compounding conventions, so comparing spreads across unlike instruments can mislead; spreads also mix risk, liquidity, and tax effects.
    Misconceptions: A wider spread does not automatically mean a bond is 'cheap'; it may simply reflect genuinely higher expected losses or worse liquidity.
    Example: If a 10-year corporate bond yields 5.6% and the 10-year Treasury yields 4.3%, the yield spread is 130 basis points.
    s = y_A − y_B, quoted in basis points (1 bp = 0.01 percentage point).
    Aliases: spread, yield differential

    Credit spread #

    The difference between the yield on the debt securities of a particular corporate or sovereign borrower (or a rating class of borrowers) and the yield on similar-maturity Treasury securities. It compensates investors for bearing default and credit-migration risk, plus a liquidity component.
    Intuition: It is the extra interest a risky borrower must pay over the government to persuade investors to lend to it.
    Mechanics: Measured as corporate (or other non-Treasury) yield minus the matched-maturity Treasury yield, or at index level as an option-adjusted spread over the Treasury spot curve, e.g. the ICE BofA US Corporate Index OAS published on FRED.
    Failure modes: Spreads conflate expected default losses, risk premia, and illiquidity, so they overstate pure default compensation; in crises, liquidity-driven widening can look like surging default risk. Maturity or option mismatches also distort simple yield differences.
    Misconceptions: The credit spread is not a direct estimate of default probability, and a high spread does not by itself imply a mispricing or an attractive trade.
    Example: If BBB-rated 10-year corporate bonds yield 5.5% while the 10-year Treasury yields 4.3%, the credit spread is 120 basis points.
    Credit spread = y(corporate bond) − y(matched-maturity Treasury), in basis points.
    Aliases: default spread

    Option-adjusted spread #

    The constant spread added to a benchmark spot curve that makes the model value of a security's cash flows — adjusted for the value of embedded options such as calls or prepayment rights — equal to its market price. It is the standard spread measure for callable bonds and mortgage-backed securities.
    Intuition: It is the yield premium a bond pays over Treasuries after stripping out the value of any options hidden in the bond, leaving a cleaner measure of credit and liquidity compensation.
    Mechanics: An interest-rate model generates many rate paths; cash flows on each path reflect option exercise (e.g. calls or prepayments). The spread over the benchmark curve that equates the average present value across paths to the market price is the OAS. ICE BofA computes index OAS as the market-cap-weighted average of constituent bond OAS versus a spot Treasury curve.
    Failure modes: OAS is model-dependent: results vary with the volatility assumptions and interest-rate/prepayment model used, so OAS values from different models are not strictly comparable. It is also an average over scenarios, not a guaranteed earned spread.
    Misconceptions: OAS is not the spread an investor will actually realize; realized excess return depends on path, default, and prepayment outcomes, not just the option adjustment.
    Example: The ICE BofA US Corporate Index OAS (FRED series BAMLC0A0CM) stood around 0.8–1.0% in calm periods and spiked above 4% in March 2020, reflecting the market-wide corporate spread adjustment over the Treasury spot curve.
    Price = (1/N) Σ_paths Σ_t CF_t(path) × DF_t(path; benchmark + OAS); OAS is solved numerically.
    Aliases: OAS

    Z-spread #

    The constant spread that, when added to each point of the Treasury (or benchmark) spot rate curve, makes the present value of a bond's cash flows equal to its market price. Unlike a nominal spread measured at a single maturity, it accounts for the entire term structure; unlike OAS, it does not adjust for embedded options.
    Intuition: It is the single number of extra yield the bond must offer at every point along the curve for its promised cash flows to be worth today's price.
    Mechanics: Each cash flow is discounted at the benchmark spot rate for its maturity plus the spread Z; Z is solved numerically so the discounted cash flows sum to the full (dirty) price. For option-free bonds the Z-spread and OAS coincide; for bonds with embedded options the Z-spread includes compensation for option risk.
    Failure modes: For callable or prepayable bonds the Z-spread misattributes option value to credit/liquidity, overstating the option-free spread; it also assumes cash flows are certain, so it is misleading for credit-risky securities near distress.
    Misconceptions: The 'zero-volatility' name does not mean the bond is riskless; it means the spread is constant and no interest-rate volatility model (option adjustment) is applied.
    Example: A 3-year bond priced at 104.90 with cash flows of 5, 5, and 105 against spot rates of 2.5%, 2.7%, and 3.0% has a Z-spread of about 0.29% (29 bp).
    P = Σ_t CF_t / (1 + (z_t + Z)/2)^(2t), where P is the full price, CF_t the cash flow at time t, z_t the spot rate at t, and Z the Z-spread (semiannual convention).
    Aliases: zero-volatility spread, static spread

    Asset swap spread #

    The spread over the floating benchmark in an asset swap — a package in which an investor buys a fixed-rate bond and simultaneously enters an interest rate swap, paying the bond's fixed coupons and receiving a floating rate plus a spread. It expresses the bond's yield relative to the swap curve rather than to government bonds.
    Intuition: It measures how much extra return a bond offers once its interest-rate exposure has been swapped away, isolating the credit and liquidity premium versus the derivatives market's benchmark.
    Mechanics: In a par asset swap, the investor pays the bond's fixed coupon into the swap and receives floating (e.g. SOFR) plus or minus a fixed spread; the package is priced at par, and the spread solves for the floating-leg margin. The asset swap spread is thus the difference between the bond's economics and the matched-maturity swap rate, expressed in basis points.
    Failure modes: The spread depends on the swap benchmark and the assumed funding of the position; benchmark transitions (LIBOR to SOFR) changed its level and comparability. Unlike a CDS, an asset swap does not terminate on default, so it embeds recovery-risk differences.
    Misconceptions: A high asset swap spread does not mean the position is protected against default — the investor keeps the bond's credit risk, since the swap typically continues even if the issuer defaults.
    Example: An investor buys a bond yielding 6% when the matched swap rate is 5%; after accounting for the price premium, the asset swap spread might be quoted as SOFR + 50 bp.
    ASW spread ≈ bond's spread over the swap curve such that (bond price + swap value) = par at inception; roughly ASW ≈ (bond yield − swap rate) adjusted for any premium/discount to par amortized over the swap.
    Aliases: ASW spread

    Swap spread #

    The difference between the fixed rate on a plain-vanilla interest rate swap of a given maturity and the yield on a government bond of the same maturity. It reflects the relative pricing of the derivatives and government bond markets, including intermediation and funding costs.
    Intuition: It compares the cost of exchanging fixed-for-floating payments in the swap market with the government's borrowing cost — a gauge of how dealers price balance-sheet usage and of relative supply and demand in the two markets.
    Mechanics: Swap rate minus same-maturity government bond yield, in basis points. Arbitrage links the two markets (hold the bond funded via repo, pay fixed on the swap), but dealer balance-sheet costs, repo funding rates, bond supply, and demand to receive fixed can push spreads away from zero; long-maturity USD spreads have been persistently negative in recent years, and the shift from LIBOR to risk-free rates such as SOFR mechanically lowered swap spreads.
    Failure modes: Spreads are not a pure credit measure: they move with repo funding costs, dealer constraints, Treasury supply, and benchmark conventions, so treating them as a simple risk gauge misleads. Negative spreads are not exploitable arbitrage profits — they compensate intermediaries for real balance-sheet costs.
    Misconceptions: A negative swap spread is not a 'free lunch' arbitrage left on the table; capturing it requires repo-funded bond holdings whose financing and balance-sheet costs consume the apparent profit.
    Example: If the 10-year SOFR swap rate is 3.60% and the 10-year Treasury yields 4.05%, the 10-year swap spread is −45 basis points.
    Swap spread = swap rate(maturity T) − government bond yield(maturity T), in basis points.
    Aliases: interest rate swap spread

    DV01 #

    The change in a bond's (or portfolio's) value, in currency units, for a one-basis-point (0.01%) change in yield. In the U.S. it is also called the dollar value of an 01; it is essentially the price value of a basis point (PVBP), often written PV01.
    Intuition: It answers the trader's core question: how many dollars do I make or lose if yields move by one basis point?
    Mechanics: Computed either analytically as modified duration times full price times 0.0001, or numerically by repricing the position with yields shifted up and down one basis point and halving the difference. Because it is additive across positions, it is the standard unit for sizing and hedging interest-rate risk.
    Failure modes: DV01 is a linear (first-order) sensitivity: for large yield moves it ignores convexity, so realized P&L deviates from the DV01 estimate. For option-embedded bonds it shifts with the level of rates.
    Misconceptions: DV01 is not a percentage and not a probability; and a DV01-hedged book is only hedged against small parallel yield moves, not against curve reshaping or volatility changes.
    Example: A bond with modified duration 8.5 and full price 101 has DV01 ≈ 8.5 × 101 × 0.0001 ≈ $0.0859 per $100 face — about $859 per $1 million face per basis point.
    DV01 = ModDur × P × 0.0001, where ModDur is modified duration and P the full price; equivalently DV01 = (PV(−1bp) − PV(+1bp)) / 2.
    Aliases: dollar value of a basis point, PVBP, basis point value, BPV

    PV01 #

    The change in the present value (full price) of a bond or swap for a one-basis-point change in yield. It is the same quantity as PVBP and the U.S. market term DV01; in swap markets PV01 is the standard sensitivity measure per basis point of the swap rate.
    Intuition: It is the cash value gained or lost when the relevant rate ticks up or down by a single basis point.
    Mechanics: Estimated numerically by repricing with yields one basis point lower and one basis point higher and taking half the difference, which works even when cash flows are uncertain (e.g. callable bonds); analytically it equals modified duration times price times 0.0001.
    Failure modes: Like all first-order sensitivities it ignores convexity and is valid only locally; PV01 quoted on swaps depends on curve-construction and discounting conventions, so cross-vendor values can differ slightly.
    Misconceptions: PV01 and DV01 are sometimes claimed to be fundamentally different measures; in practice both denote the present-value change per basis point, with naming differences by market segment rather than by concept.
    Example: If a swap's present value rises by $4,600 when its rate falls 1 bp and falls by $4,400 when the rate rises 1 bp, its PV01 ≈ (4,600 + 4,400)/2 = $4,500 per bp on the position.
    PV01 = (PV(−1bp) − PV(+1bp)) / 2 ≈ ModDur × P × 0.0001.

    Key rate duration #

    A measure of a bond's or portfolio's price sensitivity to a change in yield at one specific maturity point on the curve, holding other points fixed. The set of key rate durations across maturities decomposes total interest-rate sensitivity, and they sum (approximately) to the effective duration.
    Intuition: Instead of asking 'what if all rates move?', it asks 'what if only the 5-year rate moves?' — locating where along the curve a portfolio's risk actually sits.
    Mechanics: The yield at a chosen key maturity (e.g. 2, 5, 10, 30 years) is shifted by a small amount (with the shift tapered to neighboring points), the security is repriced both ways, and duration is computed from the price changes; repeating across 11 standard maturities gives a sensitivity profile used for non-parallel curve hedging.
    Failure modes: Real yield curves rarely move one point at a time, so isolated key rate shifts are artificial; results also depend on how the shift is tapered to adjacent maturities, making values convention-dependent.
    Misconceptions: A high total duration does not reveal curve exposure: two portfolios with the same effective duration can have very different key rate profiles and behave oppositely when the curve twists.
    Example: A portfolio with a 10-year key rate duration of 4.0 loses roughly 4% of value if the 10-year rate alone rises 100 bp, regardless of what other maturities do.
    KRD_i = (P(−) − P(+)) / (2 × Δy × P_0), where P(−), P(+) are prices after a 1% down/up shift of the key rate at maturity i, and P_0 the original price.

    Effective duration #

    A bond's price sensitivity to a parallel shift in the benchmark yield curve, computed numerically by repricing the bond at higher and lower yields while allowing cash flows to change (e.g. via call or prepayment options). It is the appropriate duration measure for bonds with embedded options, where cash flows are not fixed.
    Intuition: It measures how much a bond's price actually moves when rates move, recognizing that for callable or mortgage bonds the payments themselves change when rates change.
    Mechanics: The bond is valued under a small downward and upward parallel shift of the curve (using a model that adjusts cash flows for embedded options), and duration is approximated from the three prices; it equals the percentage price change for a 1% parallel rate move.
    Failure modes: It assumes parallel curve shifts, so it misses reshaping risk; it is also model-dependent for option-embedded bonds, since the prepayment or option model determines the shifted prices.
    Misconceptions: Duration is not the bond's maturity and not a fixed property of the security — effective duration changes as rates, volatility, and time move, especially for callable bonds.
    Example: If a callable bond priced at 100 would be worth 103.5 at rates 50 bp lower and 96.8 at rates 50 bp higher, its effective duration is (103.5 − 96.8)/(2 × 0.005 × 100) = 6.7.
    EffDur = (P(−) − P(+)) / (2 × Δy × P_0), where P(−), P(+) are prices after a parallel curve shift down/up of Δy, and P_0 the current price.

    Macaulay duration #

    The weighted-average time until a bond's cash flows are received, with each cash flow weighted by its share of the bond's present value. Expressed in years, it equals maturity for a zero-coupon bond and is always shorter than maturity for a coupon bond; it is the foundation from which modified and effective duration are derived.
    Intuition: It is the 'center of gravity' of a bond's payments — the single point in time at which the average dollar of the bond's value arrives.
    Mechanics: Each cash flow's present value is divided by the bond's full price to get a weight; the weights are multiplied by the time to receipt and summed. Dividing Macaulay duration by (1 + yield per period) gives modified duration, the direct price-sensitivity measure.
    Failure modes: As a time measure it applies cleanly only to fixed cash flows; for callable or floating-rate instruments the cash-flow weights are not fixed, so Macaulay duration is undefined or misleading without an options model.
    Misconceptions: Duration in years is often mistaken for the bond's remaining life; a 10-year coupon bond's Macaulay duration is well below 10 years, and duration (not maturity) is what governs rate sensitivity.
    Example: A 4-year, 4% semiannual-pay bond at a 6% yield has an annualized Macaulay duration of about 3.72 years — less than its 4-year maturity because coupons arrive earlier.
    MacDur = Σ_t [ t × PV(CF_t) ] / P, where PV(CF_t) is the present value of the cash flow at time t and P the full price; ModDur = MacDur / (1 + r), with r the yield per period.

    Negative convexity #

    A property of a bond whose price-yield relationship is concave: prices rise less when yields fall and fall more when yields rise than duration alone predicts. It is typical of callable bonds and mortgage-backed securities, whose cash flows shorten when rates decline (calls/prepayments) and lengthen when rates rise.
    Intuition: The bond works against you both ways: it stops appreciating just when rates fall (because it gets called or prepaid) and keeps falling when rates rise.
    Mechanics: Convexity is the second derivative of price with respect to yield, approximated as (P(−) + P(+) − 2P_0) / (P_0 × Δy²); when this quantity is negative, duration rises as yields rise (extension risk) and falls as yields fall (contraction risk), making duration-only hedges systematically wrong in the adverse direction.
    Failure modes: Duration-based estimates fail precisely when they are most needed (large rate moves) for negatively convex securities; hedging MBS or callable books with duration alone creates pro-cyclical hedging flows that can amplify rate swings.
    Misconceptions: Negative convexity does not mean the bond must lose money; it means the price response is asymmetric, and investors are compensated for bearing it through higher yield (option-adjusted spread).
    Example: A callable bond priced at 100 might gain only 3 points if yields fall 100 bp (its price is capped near the call price) but lose 6 points if yields rise 100 bp.
    Convexity ≈ (P(−) + P(+) − 2 × P_0) / (P_0 × Δy²); negative when P(−) + P(+) < 2 × P_0.

    Positive convexity #

    A property of a bond whose price-yield relationship is convex: prices rise more when yields fall and fall less when yields rise than duration alone predicts. All standard non-callable fixed-rate bonds exhibit positive convexity; the effect grows with longer maturity and lower coupons.
    Intuition: The bond's price accelerates in your favor: you gain a bit extra when rates fall and lose a bit less when rates rise, compared with the straight-line duration estimate.
    Mechanics: Convexity is the curvature (second derivative) of the price-yield function; with positive convexity, duration falls as yields rise and rises as yields fall. The effect is most pronounced in long-duration, low-coupon bonds, and the price impact is captured by adding a convexity adjustment to the duration estimate.
    Failure modes: Convexity benefits are already priced in (higher-convexity bonds trade at lower yields), so it is not a free advantage; for small rate moves the convexity term is negligible and adds little to the duration estimate.
    Misconceptions: Positive convexity is not a guarantee of outperformance and does not mean prices cannot fall; it only describes the curvature of the price-yield relationship.
    Example: A 30-year zero-coupon bond has very high positive convexity: a 100 bp yield decline lifts its price by more than duration suggests, while a 100 bp rise costs it less.
    Convexity ≈ (P(−) + P(+) − 2 × P_0) / (P_0 × Δy²) > 0; price change ≈ −ModDur × Δy × P_0 + ½ × Convexity × (Δy)² × P_0.

    Basis point #

    A unit of measure for interest rates and yields equal to one one-hundredth of a percentage point (0.01%). It is the standard unit for quoting yield changes, spreads, and rate moves.
    Intuition: It is the 'cent' of interest rates: saying 'up 25 basis points' unambiguously means up 0.25 percentage points.
    Mechanics: Yields, spreads, and rate changes are multiplied by 100 to convert percentage points to basis points; e.g. the Federal Reserve describes changes in the federal funds target in basis points, and DV01 measures value change per one basis point.
    Failure modes: Confusion between basis points and percent (or between relative percent change and percentage-point change) causes order-of-magnitude errors in P&L and risk calculations.
    Misconceptions: A '1% rise in rates' is ambiguous; professional usage distinguishes a 100 bp (one percentage point) rise from a 1 percent relative increase, which are very different.
    Example: If the 10-year Treasury yield moves from 4.25% to 4.50%, it has risen 25 basis points.
    1 bp = 0.01% = 0.0001 in decimal; value in bp = value in percent × 100.

    Policy rate #

    The short-term interest rate that a central bank sets (or targets) to implement monetary policy. In the United States it is the Federal Open Market Committee's target range for the federal funds rate, which the New York Fed's trading desk defends through open market operations and administered rates.
    Intuition: It is the central bank's main dial: raising it tightens financial conditions to cool the economy, lowering it loosens conditions to stimulate activity.
    Mechanics: The FOMC announces a target range for the federal funds rate; the Desk conducts repo, reverse repo, and other operations, and sets administered rates (interest on reserve balances, ON RRP offering rate, standing repo rate) to keep the effective market rate within the range.
    Failure modes: The policy rate directly anchors only very short-term rates; long-term yields are driven by expectations and term premia, so policy moves can fail to transmit (e.g. the 2000s 'conundrum' when long yields fell as the Fed hiked). At the zero lower bound, the rate loses room as a tool.
    Misconceptions: The central bank does not 'set all interest rates'; it targets one overnight rate and influences the rest of the curve only indirectly through expectations and operations.
    Example: In 2022–2023 the FOMC raised its target range for the federal funds rate from 0–0.25% to 5.25–5.50% in a series of moves of 25 to 75 basis points.
    Aliases: official interest rate, target rate

    Overnight rate #

    The interest rate on loans or deposits with a maturity of one business day, typically traded between financial institutions to manage daily reserve or cash positions. Key U.S. overnight rates include the effective federal funds rate (unsecured) and SOFR (secured by Treasury collateral).
    Intuition: It is the price of borrowing money until tomorrow — the shortest-maturity interest rate in the financial system and the anchor for all longer rates.
    Mechanics: Institutions lend and borrow cash for one day in money markets; transaction-level data are aggregated into published benchmarks (the New York Fed computes EFFR and SOFR as volume-weighted medians of overnight transactions). Central banks steer overnight rates to implement policy.
    Failure modes: Overnight rates can spike under collateral or reserve scarcity (e.g. the September 2019 repo spike), breaking the link with the policy target; month- and quarter-end reporting effects also distort measured rates.
    Misconceptions: There is no single 'overnight rate': secured (repo/SOFR) and unsecured (fed funds) overnight rates coexist and can diverge because they price different risks and counterparties.
    Example: On a typical day, a bank lending reserves to another bank overnight earns the federal funds rate, while a money market fund lending cash against Treasury collateral earns a rate near SOFR.
