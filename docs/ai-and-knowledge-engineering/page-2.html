    Adversarial test reviewed #

    Deliberately probing an AI system with inputs designed to make it fail — evasion attacks, poisoned data, prompt injections, extraction attempts — in order to find vulnerabilities before an adversary does. NIST's adversarial machine learning taxonomy (NIST AI 100-2) classifies such attacks by ML method, lifecycle stage, and attacker goals, capabilities, and knowledge, and pairs them with mitigations.
    Intuition: Hire the burglar to test the locks: instead of asking 'does it work?', ask 'how can it be made to break?'
    Mechanics: Testers enumerate the attack surface (inputs, retrieved content, tools, training data) and mount attack classes from the taxonomy — e.g., crafting inputs that bypass safety controls or manipulate decisions — then record success rates and trace them to mitigations such as input filtering, least privilege, and human approval for high-risk actions. Penetration-testing practice treats the model as an untrusted user.
    Failure modes: A passed adversarial test proves only that the tested attacks failed — novel attack classes and adaptive attackers evolve past any fixed test suite. Tests scoped to the model alone miss system-level paths (tools, retrieval corpora, downstream consumers of output).
    Misconceptions: Adversarial testing is not the same as accuracy testing on hard examples; it assumes an intelligent opponent who adapts. Red teaming once, pre-launch, is not a durable defense — it must recur as the system and attack landscape change.
    Example: Before enabling a trading assistant's report-summarization feature, testers feed it research PDFs containing hidden injected instructions and verify that approval gates and output filters block every attempted policy override.
    Aliases: adversarial testing, red teaming, attack simulation

    Human-in-the-loop review reviewed #

    A workflow design in which human judgment is inserted into an AI system's pipeline — labeling data, reviewing outputs, approving consequential actions — rather than letting the system run end-to-end autonomously. Surveys of human-in-the-loop machine learning organize these roles into data processing, interventional model training, and system-level HITL design.
    Intuition: The AI drafts; a person signs off. Anything the system gets wrong still has to pass a human checkpoint before it counts.
    Mechanics: Outputs or proposed actions are routed to reviewers under defined triggers (low confidence, high stakes, sampled audits); reviewer decisions correct errors, generate training signal, and authorize or block actions. Effective designs manage reviewer load via prioritization and measure inter-rater agreement and reviewer override rates.
    Failure modes: Automation bias hollows out the review: humans rubber-stamp model output at volume, so the checkpoint exists on paper but catches little. Review also fails when reviewers lack the domain expertise or the time budget the sampling rate assumes.
    Misconceptions: Putting a human 'in the loop' is not automatically safer — an overloaded or under-informed reviewer is a fig leaf. HITL is also not limited to labeling training data; it spans oversight of live decisions.
    Example: In this library, entries generated or edited by AI can reach 'reviewed' status with citations, but promotion to 'trusted' requires a recorded human or independent review — a structural HITL gate on knowledge quality.
    Aliases: HITL review, human oversight, human verification step

    Confidence calibration reviewed #

    The property that a model's stated confidence matches its true correctness likelihood: among predictions made with 80% confidence, about 80% should be correct. Guo et al. (2017) showed modern deep networks are typically poorly calibrated (overconfident) and that simple post-processing such as temperature scaling can substantially fix it.
    Intuition: A well-calibrated model's 'I'm 90% sure' is right nine times out of ten — its confidence number means what it says.
    Mechanics: Calibration is measured with reliability diagrams and metrics such as Expected Calibration Error (bin predictions by confidence, compare average confidence to empirical accuracy). Miscalibration is corrected post-hoc by rescaling the output distribution, e.g., dividing logits by a learned temperature before the softmax.
    Failure modes: Calibration is distribution-dependent: it breaks under data or concept drift, so a model calibrated at validation time becomes overconfident in production. Raw LLM verbalized confidence ('I'm fairly sure') is generally much worse calibrated than classifier probabilities.
    Misconceptions: High accuracy does not imply good calibration — a model can be accurate and systematically overconfident. Softmax probabilities are not calibrated confidences by default; calibration must be measured and usually corrected.
    Example: A signal classifier says 0.9 confidence on 100 trades; if only 70 are correct, it is overconfident — after temperature scaling, its 0.9-confidence bucket actually wins about 90% of the time.
    ECE = Σ_m (|B_m|/n) · |acc(B_m) − conf(B_m)|, where predictions are binned into M bins B_m by confidence, acc(B_m) is empirical accuracy in bin m, conf(B_m) is average predicted confidence in bin m, and n is the total number of predictions.
    Aliases: probability calibration

    Abstention reviewed #

    The design choice that lets a model decline to answer on inputs where it is likely to be wrong, trading coverage for accuracy. In selective classification (Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017), the system pairs a predictor with a selection function that rejects low-confidence instances so that a user-chosen risk level is met with high probability.
    Intuition: A system allowed to say 'I don't know' can be far more trustworthy on the answers it does give — you choose how much silence to buy for how much accuracy.
    Mechanics: A selection function g(x) (e.g., a confidence threshold) decides whether the predictor h(x) answers or abstains; selective risk is the loss measured only on answered instances, and coverage is the fraction answered. Raising the abstention threshold lowers risk but reduces coverage, tracing a risk-coverage curve used to pick an operating point.
    Failure modes: Abstention quality depends on the confidence signal: an overconfident miscalibrated model abstains on the wrong cases and answers confidently where it is wrong. It also fails exactly on distribution shift, where inputs unlike anything in validation produce confident nonsense.
    Misconceptions: Abstaining is not an admission the system is broken — it is a designed operating mode that converts unknown errors into visible non-answers. A high abstention rate is also not automatically good; it can hide a useless system that answers nothing.
    Example: A trading-knowledge assistant abstains — 'no verified source available' — when asked for a contract specification absent from its knowledge library, instead of guessing a plausible tick value.
    Selective risk R(h, g) = E[ℓ(h(x), y) · g(x)] / E[g(x)]; coverage φ(h, g) = E[g(x)], where ℓ is the loss, h the predictor, and g ∈ {0,1} the selection (accept) function.
    Aliases: reject option, selective prediction, selective classification

    Trading-agent guardrail reviewed #

    A deterministic control that bounds what an automated trading agent may do, enforced outside the model: order-size and price limits, credit/capital thresholds, duplicate-order rejection, restricted-instrument lists, and kill switches. The regulatory archetype is SEC Rule 15c3-5, which requires broker-dealers with market access to maintain automated, systematically applied financial and regulatory risk management controls that prevent the entry of erroneous or excessive orders.
    Intuition: The agent proposes; hard code disposes. Whatever the model decides, orders must pass through non-negotiable software checks before they can reach the market.
    Mechanics: Guardrails sit in the execution path as pre-trade checks: each proposed order is validated against pre-set credit or capital thresholds, price and size parameters, duplicative-order detection, and restricted-security lists, and is rejected on violation; post-trade execution reports flow to surveillance. Because they are enforced in code — not in the model's prompt — they cannot be talked around by prompt injection.
    Failure modes: Guardrails only bound what they measure: mis-set thresholds, stale restricted lists, or unchecked order types leave holes, and controls allocated to or dependent on third parties weaken accountability. They bound order flow, not reasoning quality — a guardrail-compliant stream of bad trades is still possible.
    Misconceptions: Guardrails in the prompt ('do not exceed X') are not guardrails — model behavior is stochastic and injectable; effective controls are external, deterministic, and under the direct control of the responsible party, as Rule 15c3-5 requires of broker-dealers.
    Example: An execution agent proposes selling 50 ES contracts; the pre-trade layer rejects it because it exceeds the 10-contract per-order size cap and the account's daily loss threshold was already breached that session.
    Aliases: pre-trade risk control, agent risk control

    Read-only trading agent reviewed #

    A trading agent whose tool permissions are restricted to data retrieval and computation — market data, knowledge bases, analytics — with no capability to submit, modify, or cancel orders. It operationalizes the least-privilege principle: the system's action space is limited to the minimum necessary for its function, so even malfunction or prompt injection cannot move money.
    Intuition: An analyst with a terminal and no trading button: it can look everything up and tell you what it thinks, but it physically cannot place a trade.
    Mechanics: The agent's declared tool set contains only query/compute functions; the broker or exchange connection either has no API credentials for order entry or routes through an account with trading disabled. Because enforcement is in credentials and infrastructure rather than instructions, no prompt — user or injected — can escalate its privileges.
    Failure modes: The safety property fails if the 'read-only' boundary is implemented in the prompt or application layer rather than in credentials, or if read access itself leaks sensitive data (positions, strategies) to injected instructions. Read-only agents can still cause harm through wrong analysis that a human acts on.
    Misconceptions: Read-only does not mean harmless — outputs still influence decisions, and data access still needs scoping. It also is not a compromise on usefulness for many roles: research, review, and coaching agents need no order capability at all.
    Example: A knowledge agent answers 'walk me through the margin math on this spread' using market-data and library lookups; its API token has trading disabled at the broker, so no prompt it receives can result in an order.
    Aliases: analysis-only agent, advisory agent

    Execution-enabled trading agent reviewed #

    A trading agent with the technical capability to submit, modify, or cancel orders on a market — the category of automation that pre-trade risk controls exist to govern. Under SEC Rule 15c3-5, any broker-dealer providing such market access must maintain automated financial and regulatory risk management controls — credit/capital thresholds, erroneous-order prevention, restricted-security checks — under its direct and exclusive control.
    Intuition: The moment an agent can touch the order book, it stops being software and starts being market access — with the same regulatory obligations and blow-up potential as any automated trading system.
    Mechanics: The agent holds order-routing credentials; every order it generates passes through mandatory pre-trade risk checks (size, price, credit, duplicates, restricted lists) before reaching the exchange or ATS, and executions are reported to surveillance in real time. The control layer must be documented, tested annually, and certified by the CEO under 15c3-5(e).
    Failure modes: Failure modes compound the model's and the market's: hallucinated or injected instructions become real orders at machine speed, and feedback loops between an errant agent and a moving market can amplify losses before humans react. Kill switches and throttles bound the blast radius but cannot prevent the first bad orders.
    Misconceptions: A paper-trading track record does not certify an execution-enabled agent — live order flow faces slippage, partial fills, and market impact the simulation may omit. 'The strategy is good' is not an argument against mandatory pre-trade controls; the controls exist for when the agent is wrong or compromised.
    Example: An execution agent managing a futures portfolio can submit orders only through a gateway enforcing 15c3-5-style checks: per-order size caps, daily loss limits, fat-finger price bands, and a restricted list excluding illiquid contracts.
    Aliases: order-routing agent, live-trading agent

    Approval gate reviewed #

    A control point in an agentic workflow where a designated human must explicitly authorize a proposed action before the system executes it. OWASP lists requiring human approval for high-risk actions — human-in-the-loop controls for privileged operations — as a core mitigation against unauthorized actions by LLM-integrated applications.
    Intuition: The agent can prepare the trade, fill in the ticket, and explain the reasoning — but a person has to click 'confirm' before anything happens.
    Mechanics: The workflow pauses at the gate: the proposed action, its parameters, and supporting evidence are presented to an authorized approver; execution proceeds only on an affirmative decision, and the decision (who, what, when) is written to the audit log. Gates are typically risk-tiered, so routine low-risk actions proceed automatically while consequential ones block.
    Failure modes: Approval fatigue turns the gate into a rubber stamp: too many requests, or too little context per request, and approvers stop reading. Gates also fail if the approver lacks authority or expertise, or if the same action can be reached by an ungated path.
    Misconceptions: An approval gate is not the same as notifying a human — notification after execution provides no control. Nor does a gate transfer accountability to the model: the approver and the system owner remain responsible for what is approved.
    Example: A trading agent proposes rolling a futures position three days before expiry; the order ticket appears in a review queue and is sent to the broker only after the portfolio owner clicks approve, with the approval recorded in the audit log.
    Aliases: human approval gate, authorization checkpoint

    Audit log reviewed #

    A tamper-evident, time-ordered record of significant system events — actions taken, by whom or what, when, and with what inputs and outcomes — maintained for security, accountability, and forensics. NIST SP 800-92 provides guidance on establishing log management infrastructures and processes, covering log generation, protection, storage, analysis, and disposal across an organization.
    Intuition: The black-box recorder of the system: when something goes wrong (or right), you can reconstruct exactly what happened, in order, and prove it wasn't rewritten afterward.
    Mechanics: Components emit structured log records at defined events (authentication, tool calls, orders, approvals, overrides); records are transmitted to protected centralized storage with synchronized timestamps, integrity protection, access controls, and retention policies, and are analyzed for anomalies and compliance reporting.
    Failure modes: Logs fail when they are incomplete (the decisive event was never logged), writable by the entity they monitor (the agent or attacker edits history), or too voluminous to analyze. Poor time synchronization across components destroys ordering, which is often the crucial evidence.
    Misconceptions: Logging is not the same as monitoring: an unreviewed log only helps after the fact. Application-level 'history' features (chat transcripts) are not audit logs — they lack integrity protection and completeness guarantees.
    Example: For every order an execution agent submits, the audit log records the model's proposal, the guardrail checks applied, the approver's decision, the broker's acknowledgment, and the fill — enabling reconstruction of any trading day.
    Aliases: audit trail, security log

    Reproducible analysis reviewed #

    An analysis whose results can be independently regenerated from the same data, code, and documented procedure. The U.S. National Academies (2019) distinguish reproducibility — obtaining consistent results using the same input data, computational steps, methods, and code — from replicability, obtaining consistent results across studies aimed at the same question with new data.
    Intuition: Someone else (or you, six months later) should be able to rerun the pipeline from the recorded inputs and get the same numbers — otherwise the result is a story, not a finding.
    Mechanics: Reproducibility is engineered by pinning data versions, code, parameters, random seeds, and environments; recording provenance of every artifact; and automating the pipeline so re-execution is one command. Reports declare assumptions, costs, and evaluation splits so results can be checked rather than trusted.
    Failure modes: Silent dependencies break reproduction: unpinned library or data versions, hidden manual steps, nondeterministic components (including LLM calls at nonzero temperature), and lookahead-prone data handling that cannot be reconstructed later. Vendor data that is revised retroactively (e.g., restated fundamentals) makes past inputs unrecoverable unless snapshotted.
    Misconceptions: Reproducibility is not a claim that the result is correct — a wrong analysis can be perfectly reproducible, and a true finding can be irreproducibly presented. 'The code is available' is not enough if the data and environment are not.
    Example: A backtest report ships with the exact data snapshot, commit hash, parameter file, and seed; a reviewer reruns it and regenerates the identical equity curve and drawdown statistics before the strategy is discussed further.
    Aliases: computational reproducibility, replicable research

    Model drift reviewed #

    The decay of a deployed model's predictive performance over time as the world departs from the conditions captured in its training data. It is the observable symptom of underlying data drift or concept drift: the model stays the same, but the mapping it learned fits reality less and less.
    Intuition: The model is a snapshot of how the world worked at training time; as the world moves on, the snapshot yellows — predictions get stale even though nothing in the model changed.
    Mechanics: Production monitoring tracks performance proxies (error rate, calibration, business metrics) and input/output statistics against validation baselines; sustained degradation beyond tolerance triggers investigation into data drift or concept drift and, where warranted, retraining or recalibration. In markets, regime change, crowding of known effects, and structural breaks are the dominant drivers.
    Failure modes: Drift is hard to detect quickly when ground-truth labels arrive late (e.g., long-horizon trade outcomes), so degradation is discovered only after losses accumulate. Retraining on recent data can overfit the newest regime and discard still-valid older structure.
    Misconceptions: Model drift does not mean the model's weights changed — deployed models are usually static; the environment moved. Monitoring aggregate accuracy alone misses drift concentrated in specific segments or regimes.
    Example: A classifier that ranked momentum signals well through a trending year silently degrades after a volatility-regime shift; weekly monitoring shows its precision falling from 0.62 to 0.51 before anyone re-examines the strategy.
    Aliases: model degradation, performance drift

    Source: Lu et al., 'Learning under Concept Drift: A Review' (IEEE TKDE, 2018) — Abstract

    Data drift reviewed #

    A change over time in the distribution of a model's input data, P(X), while the relationship between inputs and labels may remain unchanged. In the concept-drift literature it is distinguished from 'real' concept drift: the world looks different, but the rule mapping features to outcomes has not (yet) changed.
    Intuition: The questions arriving at the model change, even if the correct answers to each question would still be computed the same way — like an exam suddenly full of topics the course barely covered.
    Mechanics: Detected by comparing the distribution of live inputs against training/validation baselines using per-feature statistics, population stability indices, or multivariate tests; sustained shift flags inputs the model may handle poorly because they are sparse in training data. Mitigations include retraining on current data, importance weighting, and input validation gates.
    Failure modes: Distribution-shift detectors fire on benign seasonal or cyclical variation (alert fatigue) while missing slow multivariate drift no single feature reveals. Purely statistical detection cannot tell harmless shift from shift that actually hurts predictions.
    Misconceptions: Data drift is not necessarily a data-quality bug — legitimate market evolution (new participants, new instruments) shifts inputs without any pipeline error. And passing input validation does not mean the model remains accurate on the shifted data.
    Example: After a volatility regime change, the input feature '20-day realized vol' for an index-forex model shifts from a 8-15% range to a 25-40% range; the model now operates mostly on inputs near the edge of its training data.
    Data drift at time t: P_t(X) ≠ P_{t0}(X), possibly with P_t(y|X) = P_{t0}(y|X) unchanged, where t0 is the training-distribution reference time.
    Aliases: covariate shift, input drift, feature drift

    Concept drift reviewed #

    An unforeseeable change over time in the underlying relationship between inputs and outputs — formally, a change in P(y|X) — so that the mapping a model learned no longer holds even for identical inputs. The concept-drift literature (e.g., Lu et al., IEEE TKDE) organizes the field into drift detection, drift understanding, and drift adaptation.
    Intuition: The rules of the game changed: the same setup that used to mean 'buy' now means nothing — the model's knowledge itself is outdated, not just its inputs.
    Mechanics: Drift may be sudden (structural breaks, rule changes), gradual, incremental, or recurring (regimes). Detection uses error-rate monitoring and statistical change tests; adaptation uses incremental learning, sliding windows, or ensemble reweighting. In trading, crowding out of an anomaly, a central-bank regime shift, or a market-structure rule change are canonical causes.
    Failure modes: Concept drift can be invisible in input statistics (P(X) unchanged), so input-only monitoring misses it while errors accumulate. Adaptation mechanisms risk chasing noise: retraining on every apparent drift whipsaws the model and destroys stability.
    Misconceptions: Concept drift is not the model 'forgetting' or a bug to be patched once — in non-stationary environments like markets it is a permanent operating condition requiring continuous detection and adaptation. Retraining on more old data does not help when the old relationship itself is obsolete.
    Example: A carry-trade signal learned when central banks moved in sync stops working after policy divergence: identical interest-rate inputs now map to different outcomes, and only error-rate monitoring reveals the shift.
    Concept drift between times t0 and t1: P_{t1}(y|X) ≠ P_{t0}(y|X) — the conditional distribution of the label given the features changes, with or without a change in P(X).
    Aliases: real concept drift, posterior shift
