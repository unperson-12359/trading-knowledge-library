    Call option reviewed #

    A call option is a contract that gives its buyer (holder) the right, but not the obligation, to purchase a specified quantity of an underlying asset or to enter a long futures position at a specified strike price on or before a specified expiration date. The option writer (seller) has the corresponding obligation to sell the underlying if the holder exercises.
    Intuition: Buying a call is like paying a small fee to lock in a purchase price: if the asset rises above that price you can buy it cheap, and if it doesn't you simply walk away, losing only the fee.
    Mechanics: The buyer pays a premium to the writer. At expiration a call is worth its intrinsic value max(S - K, 0); before expiration it also carries time value reflecting volatility, time remaining, rates, and dividends. American-style calls may be exercised any time before expiry; European-style only at expiry.
    Failure modes: Buyers routinely overpay for time value: even a correct directional view loses money if the move is smaller or later than the premium implies (volatility crush, time decay). Sellers face theoretically unlimited loss on uncovered calls.
    Misconceptions: A call is not 'leveraged stock ownership': the holder has no voting rights or dividends and the contract can expire worthless. Nor is exercise the usual exit; most listed calls are closed by offsetting sale.
    Example: Buy one XYZ $100 call for a $3 premium (contract = 100 shares, cost $300). If XYZ finishes at $110 at expiration, the call is worth $10 and profit is ($10 - $3) x 100 = $700; if XYZ finishes at or below $100, the loss is the full $300 premium.
    Payoff at expiration (long call) = max(S_T - K, 0); profit = max(S_T - K, 0) - premium, where S_T is the underlying price at expiration and K is the strike price.

    Put option reviewed #

    A put option is a contract that gives its buyer (holder) the right, but not the obligation, to sell a specified quantity of an underlying asset at a specified strike price on or before a specified expiration date. The put writer has the corresponding obligation to buy the underlying if the holder exercises.
    Intuition: A put works like an insurance policy on an asset: you pay a premium for the right to sell at a guaranteed price, so a price collapse no longer hurts you beyond the premium.
    Mechanics: The buyer pays a premium. At expiration a put is worth max(K - S, 0); beforehand it also carries time value. The put holder need not own the underlying and can exercise into a short position. American-style puts may be exercised early; deep in-the-money puts can be rational early-exercise candidates.
    Failure modes: Long puts bleed time value while the underlying moves sideways, and puts bought as 'crash insurance' are chronically expensive because downside implied volatility is usually bid up (skew). Put writers can face losses up to the full strike value if the underlying goes to zero.
    Misconceptions: A long put is not the same as shorting stock: risk is capped at the premium but the position decays and can expire worthless even if the stock eventually falls. Owning the stock is not required to buy or exercise a put.
    Example: Buy one XYZ $100 put for a $2 premium. If XYZ falls to $90 by expiration, the put is worth $10 and profit is ($10 - $2) x 100 = $800; if XYZ stays at or above $100, the $200 premium is lost.
    Payoff at expiration (long put) = max(K - S_T, 0); profit = max(K - S_T, 0) - premium, where K is the strike price and S_T the underlying price at expiration.

    Moneyness reviewed #

    Moneyness is the position of an underlying asset's price relative to an option's strike price, classifying the option as in-the-money (positive intrinsic value if exercised now), at-the-money (strike equals the current price), or out-of-the-money (zero intrinsic value). It can be measured against spot or against the forward price, and quantified numerically, e.g., by log-moneyness or by the option's delta.
    Intuition: Moneyness tells you whether an option would be worth anything if it expired right now, and roughly how much 'cushion' it has.
    Mechanics: Simple moneyness is S/K (or its reciprocal); log-moneyness is ln(F/K); standardized moneyness divides ln(F/K) by sigma*sqrt(T) to express moneyness in standard deviations. Calls are ITM when S > K, puts when S < K. Delta is a common practical proxy for percent moneyness.
    Failure modes: Spot-based moneyness misclassifies options when carry is large (high rates or dividends); forward-based (ATMF) classification is then more meaningful. Delta-as-moneyness breaks down for long-dated or high-volatility options where delta diverges from the risk-neutral probability of finishing ITM.
    Misconceptions: Moneyness is not a statement about profitability of the position — an ITM long option can still be a losing trade if the premium paid exceeds the intrinsic value realized. 'At-the-money' in practice usually means nearest strike, not exact equality.
    Example: With stock at $100, the $95 call is in-the-money by $5, the $100 call is at-the-money, and the $105 call is out-of-the-money; for puts the relationship is reversed.
    Simple moneyness M = F/K (or S/K); standardized moneyness m = ln(F/K) / (sigma*sqrt(T)), where F is the forward price, K the strike, sigma implied volatility, T time to expiry.
    Aliases: ITM/ATM/OTM status

    In-the-money option reviewed #

    An in-the-money (ITM) option is an option with positive intrinsic value: a call whose strike is below the current underlying price, or a put whose strike is above it. Its premium equals intrinsic value plus any remaining time value.
    Intuition: An ITM option is already 'winning' — exercising it immediately would capture real value, so most of its price moves one-for-one with the underlying.
    Mechanics: Intrinsic value = max(S - K, 0) for calls and max(K - S, 0) for puts. ITM options have deltas approaching +1 (calls) or -1 (puts) and are the ones exercised (or automatically exercised) at expiration.
    Failure modes: Deep ITM options are illiquid and wide bid-ask spreads can erode the apparent value; holders of ITM American options also carry early-assignment and dividend considerations. Time value can be negative for deep ITM European puts under discounting.
    Misconceptions: Being ITM does not mean the trade is profitable — an option bought for $8 that is now $5 ITM is still a losing position. ITM short options can be assigned at any time (American style), not only at expiration.
    Example: With stock at $55, the $50 call is $5 in-the-money; if it trades at $6.20, then $5 is intrinsic value and $1.20 is time value.
    Intrinsic value: call = max(S - K, 0); put = max(K - S, 0). Total premium = intrinsic value + time value.

    At-the-money option reviewed #

    An at-the-money (ATM) option is an option whose strike price equals (or is nearest to) the current price of the underlying; in forward terms (ATMF) the strike equals the forward price. An ATM option has no intrinsic value — its entire premium is time value.
    Intuition: The ATM option is the coin-flip strike: it sits exactly at the boundary between finishing valuable and finishing worthless, which makes it the most sensitive to volatility and time.
    Mechanics: ATM options have delta near +0.5 (calls) or -0.5 (puts), the highest gamma and theta of the chain, and vega near its maximum. ATM implied volatility is the standard quoting benchmark from which skew/smile adjustments are measured.
    Failure modes: Because gamma and theta peak ATM, hedging errors and time-decay estimates are largest here, especially into expiration when tiny price moves flip the option between ITM and OTM (pin risk).
    Misconceptions: ATM does not mean a 50/50 real-world probability of profit — the buyer must overcome the premium, so the breakeven lies beyond the strike; also, 'ATM spot' and 'ATM forward' strikes differ when rates or dividends are material.
    Example: With stock at $100.10, the $100 strike is treated as at-the-money; its call might trade at $2.50, entirely time value.

    Out-of-the-money option reviewed #

    An out-of-the-money (OTM) option is an option with no intrinsic value: a call whose strike is above the current underlying price, or a put whose strike is below it. Its premium consists entirely of time value, and it expires worthless if still OTM at expiration.
    Intuition: An OTM option is a lottery-like claim: cheap, but it only pays if the underlying moves far enough before time runs out.
    Mechanics: OTM options have small absolute deltas, lower premiums, and percentage-wise high sensitivity to volatility. The CFTC glossary example: a call with a $400 strike on gold trading at $390 is $10 out-of-the-money.
    Failure modes: Most OTM options expire worthless, so repeated buying of cheap OTM options bleeds capital; conversely, OTM sellers face gap risk — a sudden large move can convert small premium income into large losses. Skew means OTM equity puts are often relatively expensive.
    Misconceptions: 'Cheap' OTM options are not necessarily good value: their price already embeds the market's implied probability of the required move, and the probability-weighted expected payoff is typically less than the premium after costs.
    Example: With stock at $100, the $110 call is OTM and might trade at $0.40; unless the stock exceeds $110 at expiration it expires worthless and the buyer loses the $40 per contract.
    Intrinsic value = 0; premium = time value only.
    Aliases: OTM option

    Deep in-the-money option reviewed #

    A deep in-the-money option is an option whose strike is far enough inside the money that its time value is negligible and its price moves almost one-for-one with the underlying (delta near +1 for calls, -1 for puts). 'Deep' is an informal descriptor with no fixed regulatory threshold.
    Intuition: A deep ITM option behaves almost like a leveraged surrogate for the stock itself, with most of its value being intrinsic.
    Mechanics: Premium approximates intrinsic value plus minimal time value; gamma, vega, and theta are near zero. Deep ITM American calls on dividend stocks and deep ITM puts are the prime early-exercise candidates because remaining time value can fall below the value of capturing the dividend or strike interest.
    Failure modes: Deep ITM strikes are often illiquid with wide bid-ask spreads, so entry/exit costs can exceed the residual time value. Deep ITM European options can trade below intrinsic value due to discounting.
    Misconceptions: Deep ITM calls are not identical to owning stock: they receive no dividends, and holders must still act (sell or exercise) before expiration; brokers may also auto-exercise them, creating an unintended stock position.
    Example: With stock at $150, the $100 call is deep ITM and might trade at $50.30 — $50 intrinsic value plus $0.30 time value, with delta near 1.0.
    Aliases: deep ITM option

    Deep out-of-the-money option reviewed #

    A deep out-of-the-money option is an option whose strike is far from the current underlying price, so it has no intrinsic value, a very small delta, and a premium close to zero that consists only of time value. 'Deep' is informal; no fixed threshold defines it.
    Intuition: A deep OTM option is a cheap tail-risk ticket: it almost always expires worthless but can multiply in value during extreme moves.
    Mechanics: Price is dominated by the implied probability of a large move (fat tails and skew make these options systematically pricier than lognormal models suggest). Delta-based margin systems treat, e.g., ten deep OTM options with delta 0.20 as two futures-equivalent contracts.
    Failure modes: Black-Scholes-style models underprice deep OTM options when return distributions have fat tails — a key lesson of the 1987 crash. Deep OTM quotes are wide and stale, so mark-to-model and end-of-day pricing can be unreliable.
    Misconceptions: A near-zero price does not mean 'free optionality': the tiny premium reflects a tiny probability-weighted payoff, and repeatedly selling deep OTM options concentrates exposure to rare, severe losses (picking up pennies in front of a steamroller).
    Example: With the S&P 500 at 5,000, a 30-day 4,000-strike put is deep OTM and may trade for a few cents, yet it is exactly the instrument that pays off in a crash.
    Aliases: deep OTM option

    Option chain reviewed #

    An option chain is a quotation display listing all available option series for a given underlying — typically every strike and expiration for both calls and puts — together with bids, asks, last prices, volume, open interest, and often implied volatility and Greeks.
    Intuition: The option chain is the menu of every option available on a stock or index, laid out so you can compare strikes and expirations at a glance.
    Mechanics: Chains are organized by expiration date and strike, with calls and puts shown side by side. Traders read chains to select strikes for strategies, gauge liquidity via volume/open interest and bid-ask width, and compute implied volatilities across the surface.
    Failure modes: Displayed quotes can be stale or wide on illiquid series, and last-trade prices may be hours old; using last price instead of current bid/ask misstates fair value and strategy P&L.
    Misconceptions: High volume on a chain line is not proof of 'smart money' direction — it cannot distinguish opening from closing or buying from selling without trade-level data.
    Example: Pulling up the SPY option chain for the monthly expiry shows strikes from 400 to 600 in $5 steps, with the at-the-money calls quoted at $3.10 bid / $3.20 ask and open interest of 85,000 contracts.
    Aliases: option matrix

    Option series reviewed #

    An option series is the set of option contracts of the same type (all calls or all puts) on the same underlying instrument that share the same expiration date and the same strike (exercise) price — i.e., a single line of an option chain, such as 'XYZ January 100 calls'.
    Intuition: If the option chain is the menu, a series is one specific dish: one underlying, one expiry, one strike, one side (call or put).
    Mechanics: All contracts within a series are fungible: any long contract in the series can be offset by selling any contract in the same series. Exchanges list new series as the underlying price moves and as new expiration cycles approach; open interest and volume are reported per series.
    Failure modes: Off-the-run series (far dated, deep ITM/OTM) can have zero open interest and no active market maker quotes, making them practically untradable at fair prices.
    Misconceptions: A 'series' is not a spread or a sequence of trades — it is simply the identifier for all contracts sharing underlying, type, strike, and expiration.
    Example: The 'AAPL Dec 2026 200 call' series groups every AAPL December 2026 call contract struck at $200; buying and later selling that same series closes the position.
    Aliases: series of options

    Option class reviewed #

    An option class is the set of all option contracts of the same type (calls or puts, but not both) covering the same underlying instrument — for example, all listed calls on XYZ stock across every strike and expiration.
    Intuition: A class is the whole family of calls (or puts) on one underlying; series are the individual family members.
    Mechanics: Classes are the unit at which exchanges organize listing, and they aggregate series across strikes and expirations. Position limits, exercise limits, and many spread definitions (e.g., spreads use options 'of the same class') are expressed per class.
    Failure modes: Confusing class with series leads to order-entry errors: a spread requires different series within the same class, while offsetting a position requires the identical series.
    Misconceptions: Option 'class' has nothing to do with share classes of stock (Class A/B shares); it groups option contracts by type and underlying.
    Example: All listed SPY puts — every strike and every expiration — together form the SPY put class; the Dec 480 put is one series within it.
    Aliases: class of options

    Option writer reviewed #

    An option writer is the party that originates (sells to open) an option contract, receiving the premium and in return accepting the obligation to sell the underlying (call) or buy it (put) if the holder exercises. The CFTC glossary also lists 'option grantor' as a synonym.
    Intuition: The writer is like an insurance company: it collects the premium up front and must pay out if the insured event (exercise) happens.
    Mechanics: Writers post margin because their obligations can exceed the premium received. When a holder exercises, the clearing organization assigns the obligation to a writer of that series; writers can close by buying back the same series (offsetting).
    Failure modes: Writing naked options exposes the seller to losses many times the premium (unlimited for naked calls); even covered writers face assignment at inconvenient times, e.g., short ITM calls before ex-dividend dates.
    Misconceptions: Writing an option is not the same as shorting the underlying, and 'most options expire worthless' does not make writing free money — the tail losses on the minority that finish ITM are what the premium compensates.
    Example: A trader writes one XYZ $50 put and collects $1.50 ($150). If XYZ falls to $45 and the holder exercises, the writer is assigned and must buy 100 shares at $50 — a $350 loss net of premium.
    Aliases: option seller, option grantor, option maker

    Covered call reviewed #

    A covered call is a position long the underlying asset and short a call option on it; the long asset 'covers' the writer's obligation to deliver if the call is exercised. It converts some upside potential into immediate premium income.
    Intuition: You already own the stock and rent out the upside above the strike: you keep the premium, but if the stock rallies past the strike your shares get called away.
    Mechanics: For each 100 shares held, the investor sells one call and collects the premium. By put-call parity, a covered call has the same payoff shape as a cash-secured short put at the same strike. Upside is capped at strike + premium; downside is the full stock decline less the premium.
    Failure modes: The premium cushions only small declines — a large drop in the stock produces nearly the full stock loss, so it is not downside protection. Repeatedly capping winners while keeping losers can degrade long-run returns (opportunity cost in strong bull markets).
    Misconceptions: A covered call is not a 'safe income' or low-risk strategy: its payoff is identical to a naked cash-secured short put, which regulators treat as a higher-risk position. Selling a call against stock does not lock in a gain.
    Example: Own 100 shares of XYZ bought at $48; sell the $50 call for $1.50. If XYZ finishes at $55, shares are called away at $50 for a total profit of ($2.00 + $1.50) x 100 = $350; if XYZ falls to $40, the loss is ($8.00 - $1.50) x 100 = $650.
    Profit at expiration = (S_T - S_0) + premium - max(S_T - K, 0), per share, where S_0 is the stock purchase price and K the call strike. Max profit = (K - S_0) + premium.
    Aliases: buy-write

    Naked call reviewed #

    A naked (uncovered) call is a short call option position where the writer does not hold the underlying asset or an offsetting position to cover the obligation; the CFTC defines a naked option as the sale of a call or put without holding an equal and opposite position in the underlying instrument.
    Intuition: You have sold someone the right to buy stock from you at a fixed price without owning the stock — if it soars, you must buy it at the market price to deliver, and there is no ceiling on that price.
    Mechanics: The writer collects the premium and posts substantial margin. If assigned, the writer must buy the underlying in the open market and deliver it at the strike. Because the underlying price is unbounded, potential loss is theoretically unlimited; brokers restrict naked call writing to high approval levels.
    Failure modes: Gap moves and short squeezes can produce losses far exceeding posted margin before the writer can react; buy-ins after assignment can compound losses. This is the classic structure behind blow-ups from unlimited-risk premium selling.
    Misconceptions: High win rate does not imply positive expectancy: naked call sellers win the premium often but occasionally lose many multiples of all premiums collected. 'The odds are against exercise' is not a risk control.
    Example: Write one XYZ $60 call for $2. If XYZ gaps to $90 on takeover news, the writer is assigned and loses ($30 - $2) x 100 = $2,800 on a $200 premium.
    Profit at expiration = premium - max(S_T - K, 0), per share; maximum gain = premium; maximum loss is unbounded.
    Aliases: uncovered call

    Cash-secured put reviewed #

    A cash-secured put is a short put option position backed by enough cash in the account to buy the underlying at the strike price if assigned. The writer collects the premium and accepts the obligation to purchase the shares at the strike.
    Intuition: You get paid a premium to place a limit-buy order: if the stock drops to the strike you buy it (at an effective discount of the premium), and if it doesn't you keep the premium.
    Mechanics: Sell one put and set aside K x 100 in cash as security. By put-call parity, its payoff equals a covered call at the same strike. Maximum gain is the premium; maximum loss is (K - premium) x 100 if the stock goes to zero. The CBOE S&P 500 PutWrite Index (PUT) benchmarks this strategy systematically.
    Failure modes: The 'discount entry' framing fails when the stock falls far below the strike — the writer owns shares well above market. Assignment is likeliest exactly when the stock is collapsing, concentrating losses in bad states.
    Misconceptions: It is not lower-risk than a covered call — put-call parity makes the two economically identical at the same strike. The cash security covers the purchase obligation, not the market loss on the resulting stock position.
    Example: Sell one XYZ $45 put for $1.20, securing $4,500 in cash. If XYZ finishes at $47, keep the $120 premium; if assigned at $40, the effective cost basis is $43.80 and the position is already down $380.
    Profit at expiration = premium - max(K - S_T, 0), per share; breakeven = K - premium.
    Aliases: cash-covered put

    Protective put reviewed #

    A protective put is a position long the underlying asset plus a long put option on it, which sets a floor on the position's value: losses below the put strike are offset by gains in the put. It is the options-market analogue of buying insurance on a holding.
    Intuition: You keep all the upside of owning the stock but pay a premium so that, no matter how far it falls, you can always sell at the strike price.
    Mechanics: Hold (or buy) 100 shares and buy one put, typically at or below the current price. Maximum loss is limited to (S_0 - K) + premium per share; upside remains open, reduced by the premium. By put-call parity, long stock + long put equals a long call plus cash (a fiduciary call).
    Failure modes: Chronic put buying is expensive — downside implied volatility (skew) makes protective puts costly, so persistent hedging can erode returns more than the occasional crash would. Hedging only works to expiration; gaps after expiry are unprotected.
    Misconceptions: A protective put does not eliminate losses — it caps them at a level that already embeds the premium cost. It is also not 'free insurance' financed by anything; the cost is paid explicitly in premium.
    Example: Own 100 shares of XYZ at $50 and buy the $48 put for $1. If XYZ collapses to $30, the total loss is capped at ($2 + $1) x 100 = $300 instead of $2,000.
    Profit at expiration = max(S_T, K) - S_0 - premium, per share; maximum loss = (S_0 - K) + premium.

    Married put reviewed #

    A married put is a protective put in which the underlying shares and the put option are purchased simultaneously as a single package, rather than the put being added to a pre-existing stock holding. The put guarantees a minimum sale price for the shares until expiration.
    Intuition: It is buying a stock and its insurance policy on the same ticket — you know your maximum possible loss on the combined position from day one.
    Mechanics: Buy 100 shares and one put at the same time. The payoff is identical to a protective put; the distinction is transactional (simultaneous purchase, which also matters for tax-lot identification in some jurisdictions). By put-call parity the position is equivalent to a long call plus discounted strike cash.
    Failure modes: Same as protective puts: the recurring premium drag compounds over time, and protection ends at expiration. Choosing strikes too far OTM leaves a large deductible; too far ITM makes the 'insurance' cost prohibitive.
    Misconceptions: A married put is not a distinct payoff from a protective put — many texts use the terms interchangeably; the only real difference is the timing of the stock and put purchases. It does not make a falling-stock position safe, only loss-limited.
    Example: Buy 100 shares of XYZ at $50 and simultaneously buy the $50 put for $2.50. Worst case at expiration is a $250 loss; if XYZ rises to $60 the net gain is ($10 - $2.50) x 100 = $750.
    Maximum loss = (S_0 - K) + premium, per share; profit = max(S_T, K) - S_0 - premium.

    Collar reviewed #

    A collar is a position long the underlying asset, long an out-of-the-money put (the floor), and short an out-of-the-money call (the cap) on the same underlying and expiration; the call premium finances part or all of the put. It brackets the position's value between the two strikes.
    Intuition: You give up the big upside above the call strike to pay for protection against the big downside below the put strike — outcome is 'collared' into a band.
    Mechanics: Hold 100 shares, buy a put at K1 below the price, sell a call at K2 above it. A 'zero-cost collar' chooses strikes so the call premium equals the put premium. Value at expiration is bounded between K1 and K2 (plus net premium effects).
    Failure modes: The short cap creates regret and assignment risk in strong rallies, and the floor still leaves the band between strikes exposed. Zero-cost construction often forces an uncomfortably tight cap or a distant floor.
    Misconceptions: A collar is not a free hedge: the put is paid for by surrendered upside, which is a real economic cost. Nor does a zero-cost collar lock in current value — it only bounds the range of outcomes.
    Example: Own XYZ at $100; buy the $90 put for $2 and sell the $110 call for $2 (zero cost). At expiration the position is worth between $90 and $110 per share no matter where XYZ trades.
    Value at expiration per share = min(max(S_T, K1), K2) plus/minus net premium; K1 = put strike (floor), K2 = call strike (cap).

    Bull call spread reviewed #

    A bull call spread is a vertical spread consisting of a long call at a lower strike and a short call at a higher strike, same underlying and expiration, entered for a net debit. It profits from a moderate rise in the underlying, with both maximum gain and maximum loss fixed at entry.
    Intuition: You buy upside participation but sell away the part above the higher strike to reduce the cost — a cheaper, capped way to be bullish.
    Mechanics: Net debit = premium paid - premium received. Maximum loss is the debit (below the lower strike); maximum gain is (strike width - debit) at or above the higher strike. The CFTC describes the call bull spread as buying the lower-strike (higher-delta) call and selling the higher-strike call.
    Failure modes: The capped upside badly lags outright call or stock ownership in large rallies; early assignment on the short ITM leg (especially before ex-dividend) can disrupt the spread and create an unintended stock position.
    Misconceptions: It is not 'free leverage': the reduced cost comes from selling the tail upside, and the probability-weighted expectancy still reflects the market's implied distribution. Limited risk does not mean likely profit.
    Example: Buy the XYZ $100 call for $4, sell the $105 call for $1.50: debit $2.50. Above $105 the spread is worth $5, a $2.50 gain; below $100 the $250 debit is lost.
    Max loss = net debit; max gain = (K2 - K1) - net debit; breakeven = K1 + net debit, where K1 < K2 are the call strikes.

    Bear call spread reviewed #

    A bear call spread is a vertical spread consisting of a short call at a lower strike and a long call at a higher strike, same underlying and expiration, entered for a net credit. It profits if the underlying stays below the short strike, with fixed maximum gain (the credit) and maximum loss.
    Intuition: You sell a call to bet the stock won't rise past your strike, and buy a higher call as a stop-loss so a rally can't hurt you beyond a known amount.
    Mechanics: Net credit received at entry. Maximum gain = credit if the underlying finishes at or below the short strike; maximum loss = (strike width - credit) above the long strike. The CFTC describes the call bear spread as purchasing the higher-strike call and selling the lower-strike call.
    Failure modes: Frequent small credits are punctuated by losses several times larger; assignment of the short leg near or after expiration can leave a residual stock position (pin risk). Selling calls into strong uptrends repeatedly loses.
    Misconceptions: A high probability of keeping the credit is not the same as positive expectancy — the loss when wrong is a multiple of the credit. Credit received is not 'income'; it is payment for accepting bounded but real downside.
    Example: Sell the XYZ $100 call for $4, buy the $105 call for $1.50: credit $2.50. If XYZ stays at or below $100, keep $250; above $105, lose $250.
    Max gain = net credit; max loss = (K2 - K1) - net credit; breakeven = K1 + net credit, where K1 < K2 are the call strikes.

    Bull put spread reviewed #

    A bull put spread is a vertical spread consisting of a short put at a higher strike and a long put at a lower strike, same underlying and expiration, entered for a net credit. It profits if the underlying stays above the short strike, with fixed maximum gain (credit) and maximum loss.
    Intuition: You sell a put betting the stock stays up, and buy a cheaper lower put as insurance so a sell-off can't cost more than a known amount.
    Mechanics: Net credit at entry; maximum gain = credit at or above the short strike; maximum loss = (strike width - credit) at or below the long strike. It is the credit-spread counterpart of the bull call spread, with nearly identical payoff at equivalent strikes.
    Failure modes: Gap-down moves can blow through both strikes and realize maximum loss with no chance to adjust; short-leg assignment before ex-dividend or at expiration can leave unwanted long stock.

    Example: Sell the XYZ $100 put for $3, buy the $95 put for $1: credit $2. If XYZ stays at or above $100, keep $200; at or below $95, lose $300.

    Call debit spread reviewed #

    A call debit spread is a vertical spread consisting of a long call at a lower strike and a short call at a higher strike, same underlying and same expiration, entered for a net debit because the lower-strike call costs more than the higher-strike call. It is synonymous with the bull call spread: a moderately bullish position with maximum gain and maximum loss both fixed at entry.
    Intuition: You buy upside participation up to the higher strike and sell away everything above it to cut the cost — a cheaper, capped way to be bullish.
    Mechanics: Net debit = premium paid minus premium received. Below the lower strike both calls expire worthless and the debit is lost; above the higher strike the spread is worth the strike width. The CFTC describes the call bull spread as buying the lower-strike (higher-delta) call and selling the higher-strike call, same class and expiration.
    Failure modes: The capped upside lags outright calls badly in large rallies; early assignment on the short ITM leg (especially before an ex-dividend date) can leave an unintended stock position and disrupt the spread.
    Misconceptions: It is not 'free leverage': the lower cost comes from selling the tail upside, and limited risk does not mean likely profit — the debit can still be lost in full.
    Example: Buy the XYZ $100 call for $4.00, sell the $105 call for $1.50: net debit $2.50. Above $105 the spread is worth $5 (gain $2.50); at or below $100 the $250 debit per spread is lost.
    Max loss = net debit; max gain = (K2 - K1) - net debit; breakeven = K1 + net debit, where K1 < K2 are the call strikes.

    Call credit spread reviewed #

It is synonymous with the bear call spread: a moderately bearish-to-neutral position whose maximum gain is the credit received and whose maximum loss is the strike width minus the credit.
