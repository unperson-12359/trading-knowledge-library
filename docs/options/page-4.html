    Volatility term structure #

    The volatility term structure is the pattern of at-the-money implied volatility across option expiration dates for the same underlying — typically upward sloping in calm markets (contango) and inverted (near-dated vols above far-dated) around known events or in stressed markets.
    Intuition: It is the market's calendar of anxiety: comparing implied vol by expiry shows when traders expect turbulence — before an earnings date or election, near-dated vols pop above the back months.
    Mechanics: ATM implied volatilities are extracted per expiry and plotted against time to maturity; known events (earnings, Fed meetings, harvest reports) create localized humps, after which vol typically falls ('vol crush'). The term structure's slope drives calendar-spread economics and the pricing of variance at different horizons, and it mean-reverts because long-dated vols anchor toward long-run averages.
    Failure modes: Comparing raw implied vols across expiries without adjusting for time misleads: total variance grows with horizon, so a 'flat' term structure still implies changing per-period volatility. Event humps also make naive contango/backwardation reads ambiguous around catalysts.
    Misconceptions: An inverted term structure is not necessarily a trading signal to sell front-month vol — the elevation usually prices a specific scheduled event whose realized move may justify it. The term structure describes implied, not expected realized, volatility.
    Example: With XYZ earnings in 10 days, the 2-week ATM implied vol trades at 45% while 3-month sits at 25%: an inverted term structure concentrated around the event, which typically collapses once the report is out.
    Aliases: vol term structure, IV term structure

    Volatility surface #

    The volatility surface is the three-dimensional representation of implied volatility as a function of both strike (or moneyness/delta) and time to expiration for options on one underlying — combining the volatility smile/skew across strikes with the term structure across expiries into a single object used for pricing, quoting, and risk management.
    Intuition: It is the complete topographic map of option prices translated into volatility units: every strike-and-expiry pair sits at its own implied-vol altitude, and traders read the terrain for relative cheapness.
    Mechanics: Practitioners build the surface by inverting a pricing model (Black-Scholes or Black-76) at every listed strike and expiry, then interpolating with arbitrage-constrained schemes (e.g., SVI fits). Surfaces are quoted in strike terms (absolute) or delta/moneyness terms (relative), and their evolution is described by heuristics such as sticky-strike versus sticky-delta. Off-model derivatives are priced by interpolation on the surface.
    Failure modes: The surface is a snapshot, not a dynamics model: hedging strategies need assumptions about how the surface moves with spot (sticky-strike vs sticky-delta), and the wrong assumption systematically misprices hedge P&L. Sparse or wide quotes at the wings make surface construction fragile exactly where tail exposures live.
    Misconceptions: The surface is not a forecast of future volatility at each point — it is a quoting convention for today's prices. Treating each point as an independent expectation ignores that no-arbitrage tightly constrains how the surface can bend.
    Example: An SPX surface might show 30-day vol rising from 14% ATM to 22% at the 10-delta put, while the 1-year tenor shows a milder 16%-to-19% slope — one object capturing both skew and term structure.
    Aliases: implied volatility surface, IV surface, vol surface

    Forward volatility #

    Forward volatility is the implied volatility for a future time interval backed out of two points on the volatility term structure: the volatility that, compounded with the near-leg volatility, reproduces the far-leg's total implied variance. It expresses the market's current pricing of volatility over a forward window rather than from today.
    Intuition: If 3-month options imply 20% vol and 6-month options imply 25%, the extra variance in the back three months implies a specific 'forward' vol for that window — the market's price for turbulence starting three months from now.
    Mechanics: Because total implied variance grows approximately linearly in time, forward variance between T1 and T2 is the difference of the two legs' total variances scaled by the interval: sigma_fwd = sqrt[(T2*sigma2^2 - T1*sigma1^2)/(T2 - T1)]. Forward vols are used to isolate event windows, compare calendar spreads fairly, and price forward-start options.
    Failure modes: The square-root-of-difference formula amplifies quote noise: small errors in either leg can produce wildly unstable or even imaginary forward vols when the term structure inverts sharply. It also assumes variance additivity, which event-dominant windows violate in interpretation.
    Misconceptions: Forward implied volatility is not a forecast of future implied or realized volatility — like a forward interest rate, it is a break-even price consistent with today's term structure, mixing expectations with risk premia.
    Example: With 50-day implied vol at 12% and 80-day at 17%, the forward vol for the 30-day window between them is sqrt[(80*0.17^2 - 50*0.12^2)/30] ≈ 23% — the market pricing a volatile event in that window.
    sigma_fwd = sqrt((T2*sigma_2^2 - T1*sigma_1^2)/(T2 - T1)), where sigma_1, sigma_2 are implied volatilities to T1 < T2.
    Aliases: forward implied volatility, forward vol

    Variance risk premium #

    The variance risk premium is the empirical tendency for option-implied (risk-neutral) variance to exceed subsequently realized variance — equivalently, the average loss borne by buyers of variance swaps and the average gain collected by sellers. Documented in equity-index markets by Carr and Wu (2009), it represents the compensation investors demand for bearing variance risk.
    Intuition: Volatility insurance is expensive on average: the volatility implied by option prices usually turns out higher than the volatility that actually materializes, and that persistent gap is the premium paid to whoever sells the insurance.
    Mechanics: Measured as implied variance (e.g., VIX^2 or a variance-swap strike) minus realized variance over the matching horizon. Carr and Wu show the excess returns of shorting variance swaps cannot be explained by standard factor models, implying variance is priced as its own risk factor; the premium is time-varying and spikes in crises, when realized variance can exceed implied many-fold.
    Failure modes: The premium is an average, not a constant: in crises realized variance dwarfs implied and short-variance books suffer losses that erase years of premium. Measurement is also sensitive to the realized-variance estimator, sampling frequency, and the implied benchmark used.
    Misconceptions: A persistent variance premium does not make selling volatility a free lunch — it is compensation for negative-skew, crisis-concentrated losses, and its existence says nothing about whether any given short-vol trade is attractive today.
    Example: If VIX averages 19% while the S&P 500's subsequent 30-day realized volatility averages 16%, the roughly 3-vol-point (in variance terms, 361 - 256 = 105 vol-squared points) gap is the variance risk premium earned by systematic option sellers on average.
    VRP(T) = E^Q[Var_T] - E^P[Var_T], empirically approximated by VIX^2 (or variance-swap strike) minus realized variance over the period.

    Volatility risk premium #

    The volatility risk premium is the tendency of option-implied volatility to exceed subsequently realized volatility, making net sellers of options profitable on average and net buyers unprofitable on average. It is the volatility-units counterpart of the variance risk premium and the empirical basis for systematic option-selling strategies.
    Intuition: Options usually cost more than the movement they end up delivering: implied volatility overstates realized volatility most of the time, and that persistent overpricing is the wage paid to option sellers.
    Mechanics: Measured per expiry as implied volatility at trade date minus realized volatility over the option's life (IV - RV). The premium is concentrated in equity index options, varies through time, and turns sharply negative in crashes when realized volatility explodes past implied. It is related to but distinct from the variance risk premium, which is measured in variance (squared) units and is the cleaner theoretical object.
    Failure modes: The premium's existence does not survive naive harvesting: short-volatility strategies realize their losses in concentrated crashes (negative skew), and after costs, margin drag, and gap risk the achievable premium is far smaller than the raw IV-RV gap suggests. Backtests of 'free' premium routinely omit these frictions.
    Misconceptions: Implied exceeding realized most of the time is not a market inefficiency you can simply collect — it is a risk price, and the episodes when realized exceeds implied are exactly when leveraged short-vol books blow up. It also does not imply option buyers are irrational; they are buying insurance.
    Example: If one-month SPX options are sold at 18% implied volatility and the index subsequently realizes 14%, the seller captured a 4-vol-point premium; in October 2008 realized volatility reached multiples of implied, reversing months of premium in weeks.
    VRP per trade ~ IV(entry) - RV(over option life); aggregated as average implied minus average realized volatility.
    Aliases: IV-RV spread, implied minus realized volatility premium

    Implied move #

    The implied move is the price range the options market currently prices for an underlying over a given horizon, conventionally estimated from the at-the-money straddle price (or a fraction of it) for the expiration covering that horizon. It is the dollar translation of implied volatility, widely used to gauge event expectations such as earnings.
    Intuition: The cost of buying both the ATM call and put is, roughly, what the market charges for the expected round trip — so the straddle price reads directly as 'how big a move is priced in.'
    Mechanics: Common practitioner conventions: implied move ~ ATM straddle price, or ~0.85 x straddle to approximate a one-standard-deviation (68%) range, or S x IV x sqrt(T/365) from ATM implied volatility. The implied move is a pricing statement, not a forecast: it embeds risk premia and supply/demand for event protection, and it typically exceeds the average realized event move.
    Failure modes: Treating the implied move as a confidence bound fails repeatedly: realized moves beyond the implied range are common around events, and the straddle-based estimate itself shifts with skew, term structure, and bid-ask width. Post-event volatility crush punishes traders who bought the move at its priced peak.
    Misconceptions: The implied move is not the market's prediction of the actual move — it is the break-even for a straddle buyer, so the stock must move more than the implied move for that buyer to profit. It is also silent about direction.
    Example: With XYZ at $100 before earnings, the weekly $100 straddle costs $6.00: the implied move is about $6 (breakevens $94 and $106), and an 85% rule would quote a ~$5.10 one-sigma expected range.
    Implied move ~ Price(ATM call) + Price(ATM put); 1-sd version ~ 0.85 x straddle, or S x IV x sqrt(T).
    Aliases: implied range, options-implied move

    Expected move #

    The expected move is the estimated price range within which an underlying is expected to trade by a given expiration with approximately one-standard-deviation (~68%) probability, derived from option prices — typically computed as about 85% of the at-the-money straddle price or directly from ATM implied volatility scaled by the square root of time.
    Intuition: It is the options market's plus-or-minus error bar on the stock: a $100 stock with a $5 expected move is priced to stay roughly between $95 and $105 two-thirds of the time.
    Mechanics: Vendor conventions vary: Barchart computes the expected move as 0.85 x the weighted average of the two calls and two puts nearest the money; brokerage platforms often display S x IV x sqrt(T/365). The expected move is used to place strikes for credit spreads and condors outside the priced range and to contextualize event risk.
    Failure modes: The 68% interpretation assumes a distribution the market does not actually price: fat tails make out-of-range outcomes far more frequent than one-sigma intuition suggests, and event windows concentrate the move into minutes, so the range is routinely breached around catalysts.
    Misconceptions: The expected move is not a target or support/resistance level with predictive force — it is a restatement of option prices. Selling strikes 'just outside the expected move' is selling roughly one-sigma insurance, with the tail risk that implies, not a statistical edge.
    Example: XYZ at $200 with 30-day IV of 24%: the one-month expected move is about 200 x 0.24 x sqrt(30/365) ≈ $13.75, so the platform displays an expected range of roughly $186 to $214.
    Expected move ~ 0.85 x ATM straddle, or S x IV x sqrt(T/365) for a 1-standard-deviation range over T days.
    Aliases: expected range, 1-sigma expected move

    Gamma exposure #

    Gamma exposure (GEX) is the aggregate gamma held by options market makers (dealers) across strikes and expirations, typically expressed in dollars of underlying per one-point or one-percent move. It estimates how much dealers must buy or sell in the underlying to re-hedge as the price moves, and its sign distinguishes volatility-dampening (long gamma) from volatility-amplifying (short gamma) regimes.
    Intuition: Every option a dealer sells must be hedged with stock or futures; add up those hedges across the whole market and you get an estimate of the mechanical buying and selling that price moves themselves will trigger.
    Mechanics: Dealers delta-hedge their option books, so their required hedge flow per unit move equals their net gamma: long gamma means selling into rallies and buying dips (stabilizing); short gamma means the reverse (destabilizing). Practitioners estimate GEX from open interest by strike with assumptions about dealer positioning; Cboe's own analysis of SPX 0DTE flow found customer activity balanced enough that net market-maker gamma was typically small relative to futures liquidity.
    Failure modes: GEX is an estimate built on unverifiable assumptions about who is long or short each series — Cboe notes outside observers cannot see the customer/dealer split, and published estimates for the same day have ranged from record short to +$50bn long. Treating GEX levels as precise magnets or guaranteed support/resistance is unsupported.
    Misconceptions: High option volume does not mean high dealer gamma — if customer buying and selling are balanced, net dealer exposure is near zero regardless of notional traded. Nor does a 'gamma flip level' mechanically force price behavior; it summarizes positioning, not causation.
    Example: If dealers are net long $2bn of SPX gamma per 1% move, a 1% rally obliges them to sell roughly $2bn of futures to stay hedged — flow that leans against the move; Cboe measured typical 0DTE net gamma at only 0.04%-0.17% of daily S&P futures liquidity.
    GEX per strike ~ gamma x open interest x contract multiplier x spot (per unit move); summed across strikes for total dealer gamma.
    Aliases: GEX, net gamma exposure

    Dealer gamma #

    Dealer gamma is the net gamma position carried by options market makers as a consequence of filling customer order flow: when customers net-buy options, dealers end up short gamma and must hedge by trading with the market's direction; when customers net-sell, dealers are long gamma and hedge against the move. It describes positioning, not a tradable instrument.
    Intuition: Dealers try to stay direction-neutral, so whatever convexity customers buy, dealers are short — and that forces them to buy high and sell low mechanically, which can amplify trends, or the reverse, which dampens them.
    Mechanics: A dealer short a call is short delta and short gamma: as the underlying rises they must buy more of it to re-hedge, and as it falls they sell — same-direction flow. Aggregate dealer gamma therefore determines whether hedging stabilizes or destabilizes: Cboe's 0DTE analysis quantifies net market-maker gamma per strike and found it generally small because customer buy/sell flow is balanced.
    Failure modes: Dealer-gamma narratives are chronically over-extrapolated from unobservable positioning; even Cboe's transaction-level data show typical net exposures are a fraction of a percent of futures liquidity, so blaming dealer hedging for everyday moves usually misattributes cause. Positioning can also flip intraday as prices cross high-gamma strikes.
    Misconceptions: Dealers do not hold views — they hold inventory to hedge, so 'dealers are short the market' confuses short gamma with directional shorting. Nor is dealer hedging automatically destabilizing: the sign of their net gamma, not its existence, determines the effect.
    Example: Ahead of a large OPEX, if customers are net long SPX calls, dealers are short those calls and short gamma; a rally then forces them to buy futures into strength, mechanically adding to the move until positions roll off at expiration.
    Aliases: market-maker gamma, dealer gamma positioning

    Max pain #

    Max pain is the strike price at which the aggregate expiration value of outstanding options (open interest) would be smallest — the underlying price at which the greatest total value of calls and puts would expire worthless, inflicting maximum loss on option holders in aggregate. 'Max pain theory' is the hypothesis that prices tend to gravitate toward this level into expiration.
    Intuition: Compute, for every possible expiration price, the total dollar loss of all option holders; the price that maximizes that collective pain is the max pain strike.
    Mechanics: For each candidate strike, multiply the intrinsic value of calls and puts by their open interest at every listed strike and sum the dollar totals; the candidate minimizing that aggregate intrinsic payout is the max pain price. The number moves as open interest changes and is published by analytics vendors as a sentiment reference.
    Failure modes: The theory is controversial and not consistently supported: the underlying's price is driven by far more than option open interest, and any apparent pinning can reflect ordinary hedging flows rather than manipulation. Treating a computed max pain level as a price target is unsupported by evidence.
    Misconceptions: Max pain does not imply a conspiracy of writers steering price — most option exposure is held by hedged market makers and institutions, for whom expiration value is not a simple loss. The metric also ignores that much open interest closes or rolls before expiration.
    Example: If XYZ open interest is concentrated in $100 calls and $95 puts, and computing total holder losses across candidate prices finds the minimum payout at $98, then $98 is quoted as that expiration's max pain price.
    Max pain = argmin over candidate prices S of [sum over strikes K of (OI_call(K)*max(S-K,0) + OI_put(K)*max(K-S,0))].
    Aliases: max pain price, maximum pain strike
