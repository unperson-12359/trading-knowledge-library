    Put credit spread #

    A put credit spread is a vertical spread consisting of a short put at a higher strike and a long put at a lower strike, same underlying and same expiration, entered for a net credit. It is synonymous with the bull put spread: a moderately bullish-to-neutral position whose maximum gain is the credit and whose maximum loss is the strike width minus the credit.
    Intuition: You sell a put betting the stock stays above your strike, and buy a cheaper lower-strike put as insurance so a sell-off cannot cost more than a known amount.
    Mechanics: The net credit is kept in full if the underlying finishes at or above the short strike; the long put caps losses below the lower strike. By put-call parity its payoff closely mirrors the call debit spread at equivalent strikes.
    Failure modes: Gap-down moves can blow through both strikes and realize maximum loss with no chance to adjust; short-leg assignment near expiration or before ex-dividend can leave unwanted long stock.
    Misconceptions: Choosing far-OTM strikes for a high win rate does not create an edge: the credit shrinks toward fair compensation for the residual tail risk, and one maximum loss erases many wins.
    Example: Sell the XYZ $100 put for $3.00, buy the $95 put for $1.00: net credit $2.00. If XYZ stays at or above $100, keep $200; at or below $95, lose $300.
    Max gain = net credit; max loss = (K2 - K1) - net credit; breakeven = K2 - net credit, where K1 < K2 are the put strikes.
    Aliases: bull put spread, short put spread, put vertical credit spread

    Long straddle #

    A long straddle is the simultaneous purchase of a call and a put on the same underlying, at the same strike price (typically at-the-money) and the same expiration date. The position profits if the underlying moves far enough in either direction to overcome the combined premium paid.
    Intuition: You are buying a big move without picking a direction: if the stock swings hard either way one leg pays off, and if it goes nowhere both legs decay.
    Mechanics: Cost = call premium + put premium; breakevens at expiration are K +/- total premium. The position is long volatility and long gamma, with maximum loss limited to the premium paid if the underlying pins the strike at expiration.
    Failure modes: The market prices straddles to the expected move, so the buyer loses if realized volatility undershoots implied volatility — the classic post-earnings 'IV crush' loss even when the stock moves. Time decay is steepest for ATM straddles near expiration.
    Misconceptions: A straddle does not profit from any move, only from moves larger than the premium implies; buying a straddle before a known event means paying an event-inflated premium that typically collapses when the news is out.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, buy the $100 call for $3.00 and the $100 put for $2.50 (total $5.50). XYZ must finish above $105.50 or below $94.50 at expiration to profit; a finish at $100 loses the full $550.
    Payoff at expiration = |S_T - K| - (C + P), per share, where C and P are the call and put premiums; breakevens = K + (C + P) and K - (C + P).
    Aliases: straddle purchase, bottom straddle

    Short straddle #

    A short straddle is the simultaneous sale of a call and a put on the same underlying, at the same strike price and the same expiration date, collecting both premiums. It profits most if the underlying finishes exactly at the strike and exposes the seller to unlimited loss above the strike and large loss below it.
    Intuition: You are selling a big move: you keep both premiums if the stock goes nowhere, but a large swing in either direction costs you point-for-point past the breakevens.
    Mechanics: Credit = call premium + put premium; breakevens at expiration are K +/- total credit. The position is short volatility and short gamma, with maximum gain equal to the credit and theoretically unlimited risk on the call side. Short straddle losses were a component of Nick Leeson's Barings Bank collapse.
    Failure modes: Gap moves produce losses far exceeding the credit before the seller can react; both legs are naked, so assignment risk, pin risk near expiration, and unlimited upside loss all apply simultaneously.
    Misconceptions: A high win rate does not make short straddles safe: the occasional large loss dwarfs the accumulated credits. Selling both legs does not 'hedge' the position — the call and put cannot offset each other because only one side can finish in-the-money.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, sell the $100 call for $3.00 and the $100 put for $2.50 (credit $5.50). If XYZ finishes at $100, keep $550; if XYZ gaps to $115, lose ($15 - $5.50) x 100 = $950.
    Payoff at expiration = (C + P) - |S_T - K|, per share; max gain = C + P; breakevens = K +/- (C + P).
    Aliases: straddle write, top straddle

    Long strangle #

    A long strangle is the simultaneous purchase of an out-of-the-money put (lower strike) and an out-of-the-money call (higher strike) on the same underlying with the same expiration date. It is cheaper than a long straddle but requires a larger move in either direction to profit.
    Intuition: You buy a cheaper ticket on a big move: the strikes are further apart so you pay less, but the stock has to travel further before either leg has value.
    Mechanics: Cost = OTM call premium + OTM put premium; breakevens at expiration are call strike + total premium and put strike - total premium. Maximum loss is the combined premium if the underlying finishes between the strikes.
    Failure modes: Because both legs are OTM, the most likely outcome is a total loss of premium; moves that are large but arrive late still lose to time decay, and event-driven buyers pay inflated implied volatility that collapses after the event.
    Misconceptions: Cheaper than a straddle does not mean better value: the lower premium reflects a lower probability-weighted payoff, not a bargain.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, buy the $105 call for $1.50 and the $95 put for $1.20 (total $2.70). XYZ must finish above $107.70 or below $92.30 to profit; anywhere between $95 and $105 loses the full $270.
    Payoff at expiration = max(S_T - K_c, 0) + max(K_p - S_T, 0) - (C + P), where K_c > K_p are the call and put strikes; breakevens = K_c + (C + P) and K_p - (C + P).
    Aliases: bottom vertical combination

    Short strangle #

    A short strangle is the simultaneous sale of an out-of-the-money put (lower strike) and an out-of-the-money call (higher strike) on the same underlying with the same expiration date, collecting both premiums. It profits if the underlying stays between the strikes and carries unlimited loss above the call strike and large loss below the put strike.
    Intuition: You sell insurance on both tails at once: you win small credits repeatedly as long as the stock stays in a range, but a breakout in either direction hurts.
    Mechanics: Credit = call premium + put premium; breakevens at expiration are call strike + credit and put strike - credit. The position is short volatility with undefined risk on both sides; brokers require margin for the naked exposure.
    Failure modes: Short strangles win often but lose severely in gaps and trends; both legs are naked, so early assignment, dividend-driven call assignment, and expiration pin risk all apply. A stock pinned between the strikes near expiry can still whip through a strike in the final hour.
    Misconceptions: A high probability of profit is not a margin of safety: the expected value after costs is what matters, and the rare full-range breakout can erase years of credits.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, sell the $105 call for $1.50 and the $95 put for $1.20 (credit $2.70). If XYZ finishes between $95 and $105, keep $270; if XYZ falls to $85, lose ($10 - $2.70) x 100 = $730.
    Payoff at expiration = (C + P) - max(S_T - K_c, 0) - max(K_p - S_T, 0), where K_c > K_p; max gain = C + P.
    Aliases: top vertical combination
    Related:

    Iron condor #

    An iron condor is a four-leg, same-expiration strategy combining an out-of-the-money bull put spread (long lower put, short higher put) and an out-of-the-money bear call spread (short lower call, long higher call), all at four different strikes. The standard ('short') iron condor is entered for a net credit and profits maximally when the underlying finishes between the two short strikes.
    Intuition: It is a short strangle with the tails cut off: you sell options on both sides of the market but buy further-out options as wings, so your worst case is fixed.
    Mechanics: Net credit = total premium sold minus premium bought; maximum gain is the credit between the short strikes; maximum loss is the wider wing width minus the credit, realized beyond either long strike. 'Iron' indicates the structure mixes calls and puts, unlike an all-call or all-put condor.
    Failure modes: Losses are a multiple of the credit, so a few full-wing losses erase many wins; with four legs, bid-ask slippage and commissions are material, and expiration pin risk on a short strike can leave an unwanted stock position over the weekend.
    Misconceptions: Defined risk does not mean low risk of ruin: selling far-OTM wings for a 'high probability' condor still concentrates tail exposure. The credit is not income; it is the price of bearing the bounded loss.
    Example: With XYZ at $100: buy $90 put, sell $95 put, sell $105 call, buy $110 call for a $1.80 net credit. Max gain $180 if XYZ finishes between $95 and $105; max loss $320 beyond either wing.
    Max gain = net credit; max loss = wing width - net credit; breakevens = short put strike - credit and short call strike + credit.
    Aliases: short iron condor
    Related:

    Reverse iron condor #

    A reverse iron condor (long iron condor) is a four-leg, same-expiration strategy combining a bear put spread (long higher put, short lower put) and a bull call spread (long lower call, short higher call) — the mirror image of the standard iron condor, entered for a net debit. It profits when the underlying moves beyond either of the long inner strikes, with both maximum gain and loss fixed.
    Intuition: It is a long strangle with the far tails sold off: you pay a smaller debit for a big-move bet, accepting a capped payoff instead of unlimited upside.
    Mechanics: Net debit = premium paid minus premium received; maximum loss is the debit if the underlying finishes between the long inner strikes; maximum gain is the wing width minus the debit beyond either outer (short) strike. It is long volatility and long gamma between the inner strikes.
    Failure modes: The most likely outcome is losing the full debit, since the underlying must travel past the inner strikes before any payoff accrues; post-event implied-volatility collapses erode the position even when the directional view is right.
    Misconceptions: It is not 'an iron condor with unlimited profit' — the outer short strikes cap the payoff, which is precisely what makes it a debit trade cheaper than a strangle.
    Example: With XYZ at $100: sell $90 put, buy $95 put, buy $105 call, sell $110 call for a $2.00 net debit. Max loss $200 between $95 and $105; max gain $300 at or beyond $90 or $110.
    Max loss = net debit; max gain = wing width - net debit; breakevens = long put strike - debit and long call strike + debit.
    Aliases: long iron condor

    Iron butterfly #

    An iron butterfly is a four-leg, same-expiration strategy combining a short straddle at the middle strike (short call and short put at the same strike) with a long out-of-the-money strangle as wings (long lower-strike put, long higher-strike call). Entered for a net credit, it reaches maximum profit when the underlying finishes exactly at the middle strike.
    Intuition: It is a short straddle with wings: you sell the at-the-money call and put for a large credit, then buy cheap far-out options to cap the damage if the stock runs.
    Mechanics: Net credit = premiums sold minus premiums bought; maximum gain is the credit at the middle strike; maximum loss is the wing width minus the credit, realized beyond either long strike. It can be decomposed into a bull put spread and a bear call spread sharing the same short strike.
    Failure modes: Maximum profit requires the underlying to pin the body strike exactly, a low-probability outcome; the large credit accompanies steep short gamma near expiration, and assignment of an ITM short leg can leave a residual stock position.
    Misconceptions: The large credit is not an edge over the iron condor — it reflects the narrower, lower-probability profit zone; on a risk-adjusted basis neither structure has an inherent advantage.
    Example: With XYZ at $100: sell the $100 call and $100 put, buy the $90 put and $110 call for a $5.00 net credit. Max gain $500 at exactly $100; max loss $500 at or beyond $90 or $110; breakevens at $95 and $105.
    Max gain = net credit; max loss = (K_wing - K_body) - net credit; breakevens = K_body +/- net credit.
    Aliases: ironfly, short iron butterfly
    Related:

    Long call butterfly #

    A long call butterfly is a three-strike, same-expiration spread built from calls only: long one call at a lower strike, short two calls at a middle strike, and long one call at a higher strike, with the middle strike equidistant from the wings. Entered for a small net debit, it reaches maximum value when the underlying finishes exactly at the middle strike.
    Intuition: You are buying a target: a cheap bet that the stock will land near the middle strike at expiration, with the wings defining your worst case.
    Mechanics: Net debit = (lower call + upper call) - 2 x middle call; the position is worth zero outside the wings and peaks at the wing width at the body strike. It is a combination of a bull call spread and a bear call spread sharing the short middle strike, and it is short volatility between the wings.
    Failure modes: The profit zone is narrow, so the small debit is lost in full most of the time; with three strikes and four contracts, transaction costs consume a large share of the theoretical edge, and short-gamma whipsaw near the body strike close to expiration is severe.
    Misconceptions: A butterfly's high reward-to-risk ratio is not free money — the maximum payoff is attained only at one exact price, so probability-weighted value, not the peak, determines fairness.
    Example: Buy the $95 call, sell two $100 calls, buy the $105 call for a $1.00 net debit. Max value $5.00 at $100 (gain $4.00); outside $95-$105 the $100 debit is lost; breakevens at $96 and $104.
    Value at expiration = max(0, min(S_T - K1, K3 - S_T)) when K2 - K1 = K3 - K2; max value = K2 - K1 at S_T = K2; max loss = net debit.
    Aliases: call butterfly spread
    Related:

    Long put butterfly #

    A long put butterfly is a three-strike, same-expiration spread built from puts only: long one put at a higher strike, short two puts at a middle strike, and long one put at a lower strike, with the middle strike equidistant from the wings. Entered for a small net debit, its expiration payoff matches the long call butterfly at the same strikes, peaking when the underlying finishes at the middle strike.
    Intuition: The same target bet as the call butterfly, assembled from puts instead — by put-call parity the two structures are economically equivalent at identical strikes.
    Mechanics: Net debit = (higher put + lower put) - 2 x middle put; the position is worth zero outside the wings and peaks at the wing width at the body strike. It decomposes into a bear put spread plus a bull put spread sharing the short middle strike.
    Failure modes: Identical structural weaknesses to the call butterfly: a narrow profit zone, heavy relative transaction costs, and short-gamma whipsaw risk around the body strike near expiration; put-side skew can make the put version priced differently in practice.
    Misconceptions: The call and put butterflies are not different bets — put-call parity makes their expiration payoffs identical at the same strikes, so the choice between them is about pricing and liquidity, not payoff shape.
    Example: Buy the $105 put, sell two $100 puts, buy the $95 put for a $1.00 net debit. Max value $5.00 at $100; outside $95-$105 the debit is lost; breakevens at $96 and $104.
    Value at expiration = max(0, min(K3 - S_T, S_T - K1)) when K2 - K1 = K3 - K2; max value = K3 - K2 at S_T = K2; max loss = net debit.
    Aliases: put butterfly spread

    Broken-wing butterfly #

    A broken-wing butterfly is a butterfly variant in which the distances between the middle strike and the two wing strikes are unequal — typically the far wing is placed further away (or a strike is skipped), skewing the payoff so that risk is reduced or eliminated on one side at the cost of added exposure on the other.
    Intuition: It is a butterfly bent out of symmetry: you give up the balanced payoff to create a trade that can profit, or at least not lose, on the side you fear less.
    Mechanics: Construction remains 1 long - 2 short - 1 long in the same option type and expiration, but the wing widths differ (e.g., $5 on one side, $10 on the other). The asymmetric strikes shift the peak and can turn the position into a net-credit trade with no loss on one side and an embedded vertical-spread loss on the other.
    Failure modes: The 'no risk on one side' property depends on entering for at least zero cost and breaks down after fees and slippage; the widened wing side carries a larger maximum loss than a symmetric butterfly, which traders underestimating the asymmetry can miss.
    Misconceptions: It is not a defect-free butterfly — the broken wing trades one risk profile for another; calling it a 'free' directional bet ignores the embedded debit/credit spread exposure on the wide side.
    Example: Buy the $95 call, sell two $100 calls, buy the $110 call (broken 5/10 wings) for a $0.20 credit. If XYZ finishes at or below $95, keep the $20 credit; the peak value is $5.20 at $100, and beyond $110 the embedded $5-wide spread loses.
    Value at expiration (call version, strikes K1 < K2 < K3, K2 - K1 != K3 - K2) = max(S_T - K1, 0) - 2 x max(S_T - K2, 0) + max(S_T - K3, 0) - net premium.
    Aliases: broken fly, skip-strike butterfly

    Calendar spread #

    A calendar spread is the simultaneous sale of an option at a given strike with a nearer expiration and purchase of the same type of option at the same strike with a later expiration, on the same underlying, entered for a net debit. The long calendar profits if the underlying is near the strike when the short leg expires, exploiting the faster time decay of the near-dated option.
    Intuition: You sell the fast-decaying front month and buy the slow-decaying back month at the same strike, harvesting the difference in decay if the stock stays put.
    Mechanics: Both legs share the strike; only expirations differ (a 'horizontal' spread). Maximum loss is the net debit; the maximum gain occurs with the underlying at the strike at the first expiration, when the short leg expires worthless while the long leg retains time value. The position is net long vega and net long theta.
    Failure modes: A large move in either direction before the first expiration hurts both legs' relative value and can approach the full debit as a loss; a spike in near-term implied volatility (e.g., into earnings) inflates the short leg against the trader. Assignment of the short ITM leg leaves stock-plus-long-option exposure.
    Misconceptions: A calendar is not a riskless theta harvest — it is a bet on the underlying staying near the strike and on the volatility term structure; losses to the full debit are possible well before the long leg's expiry.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, sell the 30-day $100 call for $2.50 and buy the 60-day $100 call for $4.00: net debit $1.50. If XYZ is at $100 in 30 days, the short call expires worthless while the long call retains most of its value.
    Value at first expiration ~ V_long(T2 - T1 remaining) - net debit; exact value requires a model since the long leg still has time value.
    Aliases: time spread, horizontal spread

    Diagonal spread #

    A diagonal spread is a spread between two calls or two puts on the same underlying that differ in both strike price and expiration date — typically long a longer-dated option at one strike and short a shorter-dated option at a different strike. It combines the directional character of a vertical spread with the time-decay differential of a calendar spread.
    Intuition: It is a calendar spread tilted with a directional view: the different strikes add a bullish or bearish lean to the front-month decay harvest.
    Mechanics: Legs differ in strike and expiry, so the payoff depends on both the underlying's level and the term structure of volatility. A common structure, the diagonal call spread (long a longer-dated ITM call, short a near-dated OTM call), behaves like a leveraged covered call; maximum loss is limited to the net debit for the debit variants.
    Failure modes: The dual dependence on price and time makes outcomes hard to bound intuitively: big early moves, term-structure shifts, or early assignment of the short leg (especially before ex-dividend dates) can each undermine the position. Rolling the short leg repeatedly converts defined plans into path-dependent outcomes.
    Misconceptions: A diagonal is not simply a cheaper covered call — unlike stock, the long option decays and can expire worthless, so the 'poor man's covered call' framing hides the long leg's own theta and vega risks.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, buy the 90-day $90 call for $12 and sell the 30-day $105 call for $1.50: net debit $10.50. If XYZ drifts to $104 by the short leg's expiry, the short call decays while the long call keeps most of its value.
    Aliases: diagonal time spread
    Related:

    Ratio spread #

    A ratio spread is a vertical-style spread in which more options are sold than bought at different strikes, same type and expiration — for example, long one call at a lower strike and short two calls at a higher strike. The extra short option(s) leave part of the position uncovered, giving the spread undefined or large risk beyond the short strikes.
    Intuition: You finance a directional option purchase by selling two further-out options instead of one — cheap or free to enter, but the second short option is naked and can hurt badly in a big move.
    Mechanics: Common ratios are 1x2 or 2x3. A 1x2 call ratio spread profits most if the underlying pins the short strike at expiration; beyond the upper breakeven the uncovered short call produces open-ended losses. Often established for a small debit, credit, or zero cost.
    Failure modes: The naked extra short leg converts a 'cheap' spread into an unlimited-risk position on the call side (or strike-sized risk on the put side); gap moves past the short strike realize losses that dwarf the initial outlay, and margin requirements reflect this.
    Misconceptions: Zero-cost entry does not mean zero risk — the unpaid-for short option is exactly where the risk hides. Ratio spreads are not hedged spreads; they are spreads plus a naked option.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, buy one $100 call for $4.00 and sell two $105 calls for $2.00 each (zero cost). Max gain $5.00 at $105; above $110 the uncovered short call loses point-for-point without limit.
    1x2 call ratio payoff = max(S_T - K1, 0) - 2 x max(S_T - K2, 0) - net premium; upper breakeven = 2*K2 - K1 +/- net premium adjustment.
    Aliases: ratio vertical spread

    Backspread #

    A backspread is a vertical-style spread in which more options are bought than sold at different strikes, same type and expiration — for example, short one call at a lower strike and long two calls at a higher strike. It is the converse of the ratio spread: the CFTC glossary describes it as a delta-neutral ratio spread in which more options are bought than sold, benefiting from an increase in volatility.
    Intuition: You sell one near option to finance two further-out options, creating a position that loses a little in a quiet market but pays off disproportionately in a large directional move.
    Mechanics: Common structure is 1 short x 2 long, often entered near zero net cost. A call backspread has unlimited upside gain beyond the long strikes and a bounded maximum loss when the underlying pins the long strike at expiration; put backspreads mirror this to the downside. The position is net long vega and long gamma.
    Failure modes: The maximum loss sits exactly at the long strike — the price level traders instinctively anchor to — and slow grind moves lose to time decay; gains require a fast, large move, since a late move arrives after the short leg has already hurt.
    Misconceptions: A backspread is not 'unlimited profit for free': the zero or credit entry embeds a real loss zone between the strikes, and the position loses in the most common (quiet) scenarios.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, sell one $100 call for $4.00 and buy two $105 calls for $2.00 each (zero cost). Max loss $500 if XYZ finishes at $105; above $110 the two long calls outrun the short and profit grows without bound.
    1x2 call backspread payoff = 2 x max(S_T - K2, 0) - max(S_T - K1, 0) + net credit; max loss at S_T = K2 equals (K2 - K1) - net credit.
    Aliases: reverse ratio spread

    Jade lizard #

    A jade lizard is a retail-coined, three-leg strategy with one expiration: short an out-of-the-money put and short an out-of-the-money call spread (short a call, long a further OTM call), structured so the total credit collected exceeds the width of the call spread. That sizing condition eliminates upside risk in theory, leaving the naked short put as the only source of loss.
    Intuition: It is a short strangle with the upside tail capped: you collect premium on both sides, and if the call-spread credit is wide enough, even a runaway rally cannot lose money — only a sell-off can.
    Mechanics: Construction: sell 1 OTM put at K1, sell 1 OTM call at K2, buy 1 OTM call at K3 (K1 < K2 < K3), same expiry, with credit > (K3 - K2). Maximum gain is the credit if the underlying finishes between K1 and K2; above K3 the call-spread loss is fully offset by the credit; below K1 the short put loses point-for-point down to zero on the stock.
    Failure modes: The naked put retains full downside exposure to a collapse of the underlying (loss up to the strike times 100 per contract); assignment risk on the short put is real in falling markets, and the 'no upside risk' property can be voided by fees, slippage, or legging in at worse prices.
    Misconceptions: 'No upside risk' does not mean low risk — the strategy is simply a bullish-skewed short-volatility trade whose entire risk is concentrated on the downside, where equity crashes live.
    Example: With XYZ at $48: sell the $45 put for $0.90, sell the $52 call for $1.50, buy the $53 call for $1.10 — net credit $1.30 > $1.00 spread width. Max gain $130 between $45 and $52; downside breakeven $43.70.
    Downside breakeven = K1 - total credit; upside loss = max(0, (K3 - K2) - total credit), which is zero by construction.

    Big lizard #

    A big lizard is a retail-coined, three-leg strategy with one expiration: short an at-the-money straddle (short call and short put at the same strike) plus long a further out-of-the-money call as an upside wing, sized so the total credit exceeds the width between the straddle strike and the long call. It is the straddle-based analogue of the jade lizard, leaving the naked short put as the only source of loss.
    Intuition: It is a short straddle with the upside tail capped: you collect a large ATM premium, and if the credit exceeds the call wing's width, a rally cannot lose in theory — only a sell-off can.
    Mechanics: Construction: sell 1 ATM put and 1 ATM call at strike K, buy 1 OTM call at K2 (K2 > K), same expiry, with total credit > (K2 - K). Maximum gain is the credit if the underlying pins K at expiration; above K2 the call-spread loss is offset by the credit; below K the short put loses point-for-point down to zero on the stock.
    Failure modes: The naked put side carries full downside exposure in a crash; because the body is a short ATM straddle, the position bleeds badly through even moderate sell-offs, and assignment/pin risk on the ATM strikes near expiration is acute.
    Misconceptions: 'No upside risk' is a construction property, not a safety feature — the strategy concentrates short-volatility exposure exactly where equity losses cluster, and the large credit is compensation for that.
    Example: With XYZ at $100: sell the $100 put for $3.00, sell the $100 call for $3.50, buy the $105 call for $2.00 — net credit $4.50 < $5.00 width, so upside is not fully covered; collecting $5.50 instead would remove upside loss. Downside breakeven is $95.50 at a $4.50 credit.
    Downside breakeven = K - total credit; upside loss = max(0, (K2 - K) - total credit), which is zero by construction.

    Risk reversal #

    A risk reversal is an options position that is long an out-of-the-money call and short an out-of-the-money put (bullish form), or the reverse (bearish form), on the same underlying with the same expiration; the put premium finances the call. In volatility markets the same term denotes a skew quote: the implied volatility of a given-delta call minus that of the matching put.
    Intuition: You swap away the downside tail to pay for the upside tail — a synthetic-like way to get leveraged directional exposure for little or no upfront premium.
    Mechanics: The bullish risk reversal approximates a long forward: it gains as the underlying rises above the call strike and loses below the put strike, where the short put is naked. Dealers quote the 25-delta risk reversal as RR25 = IV(25d call) - IV(25d put), a standard market measure of skew; in equity markets it is usually negative (puts bid over calls).
    Failure modes: The financing short put is naked: a sharp decline produces losses that dwarf the zero-cost entry, and skew means the put sold is often the relatively expensive leg, so the 'free' upside is paid for by selling crash insurance. Early assignment on the short put adds path risk.
    Misconceptions: A risk reversal is not a directional view with no cost — the cost is the accepted downside tail. Nor is a steep risk-reversal quote proof of informed positioning; it is a relative-pricing (skew) measure, not a forecast.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, buy the $105 call for $1.50 and sell the $95 put for $1.50 (zero cost). Above $105 the position gains point-for-point; below $95 it loses point-for-point as if long stock from $95.
    RR(25-delta) = sigma_call(25d) - sigma_put(25d); position payoff at expiration = max(S_T - K_c, 0) - max(K_p - S_T, 0) - net premium.

    Synthetic long stock #

    A synthetic long stock position is a long call and a short put at the same strike and expiration on the same underlying; by put-call parity its payoff replicates owning the underlying outright, gaining or losing point-for-point with the stock from the strike.
    Intuition: You get the stock's payoff without buying the stock: above the strike you exercise into shares, below it the short put forces the purchase — either way you end up long at the strike.
    Mechanics: At expiration the combined value equals S_T - K (plus any net premium adjustment), matching a forward purchase at K. The position requires no stock borrow and typically little capital upfront, but the short put carries full downside margin and assignment risk; unlike stock, the synthetic pays no dividends and confers no voting rights.
    Failure modes: The short put leg is naked and margined, so a sharp decline generates losses and margin calls like leveraged stock ownership; early assignment on the ITM short put (e.g., deep ITM or rate-driven) collapses the synthetic into actual stock before expiration.
    Misconceptions: A synthetic long is not free leverage: the position carries the full economic exposure of the stock minus dividends, and the apparent cheapness reflects deferred payment of the strike (financing), not a discount.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, buy the $100 call for $4.50 and sell the $100 put for $4.00 (net debit $0.50). If XYZ finishes at $110, the position is worth $10 for a $9.50 gain — matching 100 shares bought at $100 plus a small financing difference.
    Value at expiration = max(S_T - K, 0) - max(K - S_T, 0) = S_T - K; initial cost C - P approximates S_0 - PV(K) by put-call parity.

    Synthetic short stock #

    A synthetic short stock position is a short call and a long put at the same strike and expiration on the same underlying; by put-call parity its payoff replicates being short the underlying, losing or gaining point-for-point as the stock rises or falls from the strike.
    Intuition: You get the payoff of a short sale without borrowing shares: below the strike the put pays, above it the short call obliges you to sell — either way you are effectively short from the strike.
    Mechanics: At expiration the combined value equals K - S_T (plus any net premium adjustment), matching a forward sale at K. The structure avoids stock-borrow costs and hard-to-borrow constraints, but the short call is naked with theoretically unlimited risk, and no borrow means no rebate considerations but also margin on the call.
    Failure modes: The naked short call has unlimited loss in a rally or short squeeze — worse than a stock short in that it cannot be 'covered' by holding shares; assignment on the ITM short call (especially before ex-dividend dates) can force delivery and create borrow needs the structure was meant to avoid.
    Misconceptions: Avoiding the borrow does not avoid short-seller economics: the options market prices in borrow difficulty through put-call parity (expensive puts / cheap calls), so hard-to-borrow names simply shift the cost into the option prices.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, sell the $100 call for $4.50 and buy the $100 put for $4.00 (net credit $0.50). If XYZ finishes at $85, the position gains $15, replicating a short sale from $100; above $100 losses are unlimited.
    Value at expiration = max(K - S_T, 0) - max(S_T - K, 0) = K - S_T; initial credit P - C approximates PV(K) - S_0 by put-call parity.

    Conversion #

    A conversion is an arbitrage position consisting of long the underlying, long a put, and short a call at the same strike and expiration — long stock plus a synthetic short stock via options, per the CFTC glossary definition. The legs offset into a near-riskless position whose value at expiration equals the strike, so it is traded when the combined price deviates from put-call parity fair value.
    Intuition: You hold the stock and simultaneously hold its exact mirror image built from options; the two cancel, and any small pricing mismatch between them is the arbitrage profit.
    Mechanics: Long stock + long put + short call (same K, same expiry) has a constant expiration value of K, independent of the underlying's path. The position is established when the synthetic short (short call + long put) is rich relative to actual stock; profit is the locked-in deviation, financed at prevailing rates until expiration.
    Failure modes: The 'riskless' label ignores execution: legging risk, borrow/assignment on the short call before ex-dividend dates (which strips the stock and breaks the hedge), and financing-cost changes can exceed the thin arbitrage margin. Pin risk at expiration adds uncertainty about residual positions.
    Misconceptions: Conversions are not free money available to retail: in liquid markets the parity deviation is usually smaller than transaction costs, and early-assignment events turn a flat position into a directional one.
    Example: Buy 100 XYZ shares at $100, buy the $100 put for $4.00, sell the $100 call for $4.60: net cost $99.40 for a position that will be worth exactly $100 at expiration, locking in $0.60 before carry costs.
    Expiration value = S_T + max(K - S_T, 0) - max(S_T - K, 0) = K; fair setup cost approximates PV(K) by put-call parity.

    Reversal conversion #

    A reversal (reverse conversion) is an arbitrage position consisting of short the underlying, short a put, and long a call at the same strike and expiration — short stock plus a synthetic long stock via options. It is the mirror image of the conversion, traded when the synthetic long is cheap relative to actual stock under put-call parity.
    Intuition: You are short the stock and long its exact option-built replica; the two cancel into a fixed payoff, monetizing any small mispricing between the stock and its synthetic.
    Mechanics: Short stock + short put + long call (same K, same expiry) has a constant expiration obligation/value of -K (the position is effectively short the strike). It is established when call-put prices imply a synthetic long cheaper than the actual stock; the position earns the deviation but requires borrowing the shares.
    Failure modes: Stock-borrow recall or buy-ins break the hedge and can force unwinds at a loss; assignment on the short put and changes in borrow fees or rates can each consume the thin margin. These are market-maker trades with razor-thin edges.
    Misconceptions: A reversal is not a directional short: the option legs neutralize the stock exposure, so P&L comes only from pricing deviation and carry, not from the stock falling.
    Example: Short 100 XYZ shares at $100, sell the $100 put for $4.00, buy the $100 call for $3.40: net proceeds $100.60 against a fixed $100 obligation at expiration, locking in $0.60 before borrow and financing costs.
    Expiration value = -S_T - max(K - S_T, 0) + max(S_T - K, 0) = -K; fair proceeds approximate PV(K) by put-call parity.

    Box spread #

    A box spread is a four-leg, same-expiration position combining a bull call spread and a bear put spread at the same two strikes — equivalently, long call + short put at the lower strike and short call + long put at the upper strike. Constructed with European-style options it has a constant expiration payoff equal to the strike difference, making it a synthetic zero-coupon loan.
    Intuition: A box is options-market lending: you pay a fixed price today for a guaranteed fixed payoff at expiration, so the only real variable is the implied interest rate.
    Mechanics: Because the payoff is fixed at (K2 - K1), the fair price is the present value of that amount; paying less is effectively borrowing, paying more is effectively lending at the implied rate. With American-style index or equity options, early exercise of an ITM leg can break the box and convert it into directional exposure — the source of notorious catastrophic retail losses.
    Failure modes: Early assignment on American-style legs destroys the fixed payoff and can produce losses vastly larger than the intended interest-like economics (the infamous 1R0NYMAN case, roughly a 2,000% loss on margin). Execution across four legs also invites slippage that erases the thin rate edge.
    Misconceptions: A box spread is not 'free money' or riskless yield: with American options it embeds assignment tail risk, and the implied rate is only fair compensation for funding, not an arbitrage gift, once costs are included.
    Example: A 40-50 box (long 40 call, short 50 call, long 50 put, short 40 put) always pays exactly $10 at expiration; paying $9.80 for it today implies earning roughly the risk-free rate to expiry — unless an American-style leg is assigned early.
    Payoff at expiration (long box) = K2 - K1, constant; fair value = (K2 - K1) * e^(-rT) under put-call parity.

    Put-call parity #

    Put-call parity is a no-arbitrage identity linking the prices of a European call and a European put at the same strike and expiration to the underlying's forward price: a long call plus a short put replicates a forward contract, so their price difference must equal the discounted difference between the forward price and the strike.
    Intuition: Whether the stock finishes above or below the strike, one of the two options is exercised and you end up buying at the strike — exactly like a forward — so the two packages must cost the same today.
    Mechanics: Formally C - P = D x (F - K), or in spot terms C - P = S - PV(K) for non-dividend stocks, extended to C - P = S - PV(K) - PV(dividends) when dividends are paid. The identity is model-free (static replication) and underlies synthetic positions, conversions/reversals, and the equivalence of covered calls to cash-secured puts; violations beyond transaction costs imply arbitrage.
    Failure modes: Parity holds exactly only for European options in frictionless markets; American early-exercise rights, dividends, borrow costs, and wide bid-ask spreads create bands within which deviations persist without being exploitable. During market stress, financing and borrow constraints widen these bands dramatically.
    Misconceptions: Parity is not a pricing model that predicts option values — it is a consistency condition among prices that already exist. Nor does it require assumptions about volatility or the underlying's distribution.
    Example: With XYZ at $100, one-year $100-strike European call at $8.00, and the risk-free rate near zero, parity implies the $100 put should trade near $8.00 too; if it traded at $5.00, a conversion would lock in the $3.00 discrepancy.
    C - P = S_0 - K*e^(-rT) - PV(dividends); equivalently C + PV(K) = P + S_0 (fiduciary call = married put).

    Early exercise #

    Early exercise is the exercise of an American-style option before its expiration date. It is rational only in specific circumstances — principally calls on a stock just before an ex-dividend date (to capture the dividend) and deep in-the-money puts (to capture the strike's interest value) — because exercising otherwise destroys the option's remaining time value.
    Intuition: Exercising early is like tearing up an insurance policy to claim it today: you get the intrinsic value but throw away the remaining time value, so it pays only when something specific (a dividend, interest on the strike) exceeds that sacrifice.
    Mechanics: An American call on a non-dividend stock should never be exercised early (Merton's result): selling the option always yields at least as much. Before an ex-dividend date, exercising an ITM call is optimal when the dividend exceeds the remaining time value; deep ITM puts can be optimal to exercise early because the strike cash earns interest immediately. Writers face early assignment randomly through the OCC's allocation process.
    Failure modes: Retail holders routinely forfeit time value by exercising when selling would pay more; writers who ignore dividend calendars get assigned at the worst times and find their 'covered' position stripped of stock the day before ex-dividend.
    Misconceptions: Early exercise is not generally 'taking profit early' — it usually leaves money on the table versus selling the option. The blanket rule 'never exercise early' is also wrong: dividends and deep ITM puts create legitimate exceptions.
    Example: XYZ trades at $55 and goes ex-dividend tomorrow for $1.00; the $50 call trades at $5.10 ($5.00 intrinsic + $0.10 time value). Since the $1.00 dividend exceeds the $0.10 time value, exercising today to hold the stock over the ex-date is rational.
    Rule of thumb: early-exercise an ITM call before ex-date only if dividend > time value of the call; never early-exercise a call on a non-dividend-paying stock.
    Aliases: premature exercise
