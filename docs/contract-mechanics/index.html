    Open interest #

    The total number of futures or option contracts, long or short, in a delivery month or market that have been entered into and not yet liquidated by an offsetting transaction, exercised, or fulfilled by delivery.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Open interest counts how many contracts are still 'alive' at the end of the day — positions traders are still holding, not how many changed hands (that is volume).
    Mechanics: When a new buyer and a new seller create a contract, open interest rises by one; when an existing long and an existing short offset each other, it falls by one; when one side is new and the other is closing, it is unchanged. Exchanges and clearing organizations compute it daily per contract month and it feeds reports such as the CFTC Commitments of Traders breakdown.
    Failure modes: Open interest says nothing about the direction of positioning (longs vs shorts balance to zero by construction), so reading it as a bullish or bearish signal on its own misleads. It also collapses mechanically during contract rollover, which can be mistaken for loss of interest.
    Misconceptions: A common error is confusing open interest with trading volume: volume counts every transaction, while open interest counts only outstanding positions. Another is believing high open interest implies the market must move in a particular direction.
    Example: If crude oil futures show 500,000 contracts of open interest and a new buyer and new seller trade 1,000 contracts with each other, open interest rises to 501,000; if instead the seller was closing an old short, open interest stays at 500,000.
    OI_t = OI_{t-1} + (contracts opened by new buyer-new seller pairs) - (contracts closed by offsetting pairs), counted as one side of each open contract.
    Aliases: OI, open contracts, open commitments
    Related:

    Underlying asset #

    The cash commodity, security, futures contract, index, rate, or other reference item on which a derivative contract is based and from which its value is derived; for options, it is what must be delivered or accepted if the option is exercised.
    Intuition: A derivative is a bet or a hedge on something else — that 'something else' is the underlying. The contract never stands alone; its price moves because the underlying's price moves.
    Mechanics: The derivative's payoff is defined contractually as a function of the underlying's price (e.g., a call's payoff at expiry is max(S - K, 0), where S is the underlying price). The contract specification names the underlying, its grade or composition, and how its price is observed for settlement.
    Failure modes: When the underlying is illiquid, hard to observe, or itself manipulated (e.g., a thin index or a squeezed cash market), the derivative's price and settlement can be distorted even if the contract terms are sound.
    Misconceptions: Buying a derivative is not the same as owning the underlying: a futures or option position usually confers no dividends, voting rights, or ownership, and cash-settled contracts never deliver the asset at all.
    Example: For an E-mini S&P 500 futures contract the underlying is the S&P 500 stock index; for a CME corn futures contract the underlying is a specified grade of corn deliverable at approved facilities.
    Aliases: underlying, underlying instrument, underlying commodity

    Contract multiplier #

    The fixed dollar amount per unit of quoted price (or per index point) set by the exchange in the contract specification, which converts a quoted price into the total monetary value of one contract; for equity options the standardized multiplier is typically 100 shares per contract.
    Intuition: Quotes come in points, but money moves in dollars — the multiplier is the exchange-fixed exchange rate between the two.
    Mechanics: Contract dollar value = quoted price x contract multiplier (for unit-quoted contracts, price x contract size plays the same role). A one-point price move changes the position value by exactly one multiplier unit, which is also how tick value is derived (tick size x multiplier).
    Failure modes: Using the wrong multiplier (e.g., confusing standard, mini, or micro variants of the same product) scales P&L, margin, and hedge ratios incorrectly — a 10x or 50x error in position sizing.
    Misconceptions: The multiplier is not chosen by the trader and is not universal: it is fixed per contract by the exchange and differs across products (e.g., $50 per point for E-mini S&P 500 vs $5 for the Micro E-mini).
    Example: A U.S. listed equity call quoted at $2.50 represents $250 per contract because standardized equity options use a 100-share multiplier; likewise an index future quoted at 5,000 with a $50 multiplier controls $250,000 of notional.
    Contract value = P x M, where P = quoted futures price (or index level) and M = contract multiplier ($ per point); equity option premium in dollars = quoted premium x 100 (shares per contract).
    Aliases: point multiplier, point value, dollar multiplier

    Contract size #

    The actual quantity of the underlying asset represented by one futures or derivative contract, as standardized by the exchange in the contract specification.
    Intuition: One 'contract' is a fixed bundle — contract size tells you how big the bundle is, so one futures contract on crude oil always means the same number of barrels.
    Mechanics: The exchange sets the contract size (e.g., 1,000 barrels for WTI crude futures) so that every contract is fungible. Position exposure equals number of contracts x contract size, and delivery obligations are stated in multiples of the contract size.
    Failure modes: Hedgers whose actual exposure is not a multiple of the contract size are left with partial (basis) exposure; mini/micro variants exist partly because the standard size is too coarse for smaller exposures.
    Misconceptions: Contract size is sometimes confused with margin: the full value controlled by a contract is price x contract size, while margin is only the small good-faith deposit posted against it.
    Example: One CME WTI crude oil futures contract represents 1,000 barrels, so a trader long 3 contracts controls 3,000 barrels of exposure; if price rises $1 per barrel, the position gains $3,000.
    Exposure (units of underlying) = N x CS, where N = number of contracts and CS = contract size; dollar notional = N x CS x price for unit-quoted contracts.
    Aliases: contract unit, trading unit
    Related:

    Tick size #

    The smallest increment by which the price of a given contract is permitted to move up or down, as fixed by the rules of the exchange.
    Intuition: Prices on an exchange ladder are not continuous — they move on rungs, and the tick size is the spacing between rungs.
    Mechanics: All bids, offers, and trades must be quoted in whole multiples of the tick size. Exchanges set it per contract (and sometimes per spread type or venue), balancing price granularity against order-book depth; a tick that is too large widens effective spreads, one too small fragments liquidity.
    Failure modes: A tick size that is large relative to price makes the minimum spread economically significant and can queue transactions; in fast markets, limit orders resting at a tick level may be skipped as prices jump multiple ticks.
    Misconceptions: Tick size is not a measure of volatility or of how far price 'usually' moves — it is purely the minimum quoting increment, and price can move many ticks in one trade.
    Example: If an equity index future has a tick size of 0.25 index points, an order at 5,000.10 is rejected as invalid, and the nearest valid quotes are 5,000.00 and 5,000.25.
    Aliases: minimum price fluctuation, minimum tick, minimum price increment

    Tick value #

    The monetary profit or loss on one contract resulting from a one-tick (minimum price fluctuation) move in the contract's price.
    Intuition: Tick size tells you the smallest price step; tick value tells you how many dollars that step is worth in your account.
    Mechanics: Tick value = tick size x contract size (for unit-quoted contracts) or tick size x contract multiplier (for point-quoted contracts). It is the atomic unit of P&L: total P&L = number of ticks moved x tick value x number of contracts.
    Failure modes: Assuming a single tick value across contract variants (standard vs micro) or across currencies misstates risk; for contracts quoted in foreign currencies the tick value also varies with the FX rate.
    Misconceptions: Traders sometimes quote risk in ticks without converting to dollars, which hides that identical tick counts imply very different dollar risks across products.
    Example: With WTI crude futures at a tick size of $0.01 per barrel and a contract size of 1,000 barrels, a 12-tick favorable move on one contract earns 12 x $10 = $120.
    Tick value = tick size x contract size (e.g., $0.01/barrel x 1,000 barrels = $10 per contract for WTI crude oil futures).
    Aliases: minimum price fluctuation value, dollar value of a tick

    Strike price #

    The price, specified in the option contract, at which the underlying security, futures contract, or commodity will be bought (call) or sold (put) if the option is exercised.
    Intuition: The strike is the deal price locked in at contract creation: it is the yardstick that decides whether exercising the option is worth anything.
    Mechanics: Exchanges list standardized series of strikes around the current underlying price. The strike fixes intrinsic value (max(S-K,0) for a call, max(K-S,0) for a put) and moneyness: calls are in-the-money when the underlying trades above the strike, puts when it trades below.
    Failure modes: Comparing options across different strikes as if the premium alone measures cheapness ignores that the strike determines both payoff and probability of finishing in-the-money.
    Misconceptions: The strike is not a price target or forecast — it is a contractual term chosen from listed series, and an at-the-money option is not 'fairly priced' by definition.
    Example: A holder of an OICX July 60 call can buy the underlying stock at $60 per share until expiration; if the stock trades at $68, the call has $8 of intrinsic value.
    Call intrinsic value = max(S - K, 0); put intrinsic value = max(K - S, 0), where S = underlying price and K = strike price.
    Aliases: exercise price, striking price, strike
    Related:

    Expiration date #

    The date on which an option contract automatically expires and ceases to exist; it is the last day on which the option may be exercised (and for American-style options, the end of the exercise window).
    Intuition: An option is a wasting asset with a printed shelf life: after the expiration date the contract is gone, worth whatever final settlement paid — usually zero for out-of-the-money options.
    Mechanics: At expiration, in-the-money options are typically exercised 'by exception' above small thresholds (OCC uses $0.01 for equity options) unless contrary instructions are given, while out-of-the-money options expire worthless. Time value decays toward zero as expiration approaches, and for standard monthly U.S. equity options expiration falls on the third Friday of the expiration month.
    Failure modes: Traders who treat expiration day like any other trading day face pin risk (underlying closing near the strike), after-hours price moves that flip moneyness after the close, and automatic-exercise outcomes they did not intend.
    Misconceptions: Expiration date is not always the last trading day: for many European-style index options the last trading day is the Thursday before expiration Friday, and the exercise style is set in each product's specifications.
    Example: A standard U.S. equity option expiring in July ceases to exist after the third Friday of July; a holder who does not sell or exercise it by then keeps only any automatic-exercise value.
    Aliases: expiry, expiration, option expiration

    Maturity date #

    The specified date on which the principal (face value) of a debt instrument such as a bond is scheduled to be repaid to the investor; for futures, 'maturity' refers to the period within which the contract can be settled by delivery.
    Intuition: The maturity date is the loan's end date: it is when the borrower's promise to hand back the principal comes due.
    Mechanics: A bond pays coupons on a regular schedule until maturity, when the final coupon plus the face value are paid. Time to maturity shortens as time passes, and together with the coupon rate it drives interest-rate sensitivity: longer maturities generally carry greater interest rate risk.
    Failure modes: Selling before maturity exposes the holder to whatever price the market dictates, so 'getting your principal back' holds only if the bond is held to maturity and the issuer does not default; callable bonds can be retired before the stated maturity.
    Misconceptions: Maturity date is not the same as duration: a coupon bond's duration is always shorter than its maturity (only a zero-coupon bond's duration equals its maturity), because some cash arrives earlier as coupons.
    Example: A 10-year Treasury note issued in 2026 pays interest every six months and repays its face value on its 2036 maturity date; if sold in 2030, the investor receives the then-market price instead of par.
    Aliases: maturity, due date

    Option premium #

    The price the buyer of an option pays (and the writer receives) for the rights granted by the contract; it is the market price of the option itself, composed of intrinsic value plus time value.
    Intuition: The premium is what you pay for the right-but-not-the-obligation: it is the cost of insurance or of a leveraged bet, paid up front and never refundable.
    Mechanics: Premiums are determined by supply and demand in the options market and are driven mainly by the underlying price, strike, time to expiration, implied volatility, and secondarily by dividends and interest rates. Quoted per share (or per unit), the dollar premium equals the quote times the contract multiplier (typically 100 for U.S. equity options).
    Failure modes: Treating a low premium as 'cheap' ignores implied volatility and moneyness: a $0.10 far out-of-the-money option can be overpriced relative to its probability-weighted payoff.
    Misconceptions: The option writer does not 'return' the premium at expiration — the writer keeps it regardless of outcome; and for the buyer, the premium is a sunk cost that must be subtracted from any exercise or resale proceeds to compute net profit.
    Example: Buying one equity call quoted at $2.50 costs $250 (2.50 x 100 shares); if the option is later sold for $4.00, the net profit before commissions is $150.
    Premium = intrinsic value + time value; dollar premium per contract = quoted premium x contract multiplier.
    Aliases: premium, option price

    Intrinsic value #

    The value an option would have if exercised immediately: the amount by which the underlying price exceeds the strike for a call, or by which the strike exceeds the underlying price for a put; only in-the-money options have intrinsic value.
    Intuition: Intrinsic value is the cash you could capture right now by exercising and trading out of the underlying — everything above that in the premium is hope, not substance.
    Mechanics: For a call with underlying price S and strike K, intrinsic value = max(S - K, 0); for a put, max(K - S, 0). It sets a lower bound on an American-style option's premium, since selling the option for less than intrinsic value would invite immediate-exercise arbitrage.
    Failure modes: For deep-in-the-money or hard-to-borrow underlyings, quoted premiums can briefly deviate from intrinsic value when the underlying's price is stale or early exercise is optimal, so 'intrinsic value' computed from a stale quote misleads.
    Misconceptions: Intrinsic value is not the same as profit: an in-the-money option still loses money for its buyer if the premium paid exceeds the intrinsic value realized.
    Example: With the underlying stock at $32.80, a $32.50-strike call has $0.30 of intrinsic value; if its premium is $1.10, the remaining $0.80 is time value.
    Call: IV = max(S - K, 0); Put: IV = max(K - S, 0), where S = current underlying price and K = strike price.
    Aliases: in-the-money amount, exercise value
    Related:

    Extrinsic value #

    The portion of an option's premium in excess of its intrinsic value, reflecting the market's payment for the possibility that the option gains value before expiration; in standard usage it is synonymous with time value, though it embeds volatility, carry, and dividend effects as well as time.
    Intuition: Extrinsic value is the 'maybe' premium: the extra amount buyers pay over immediate-exercise value for the chance the underlying moves favorably before expiry.
    Mechanics: Extrinsic value = premium - intrinsic value. It is largest for at-the-money options with long time to expiration and high implied volatility, and it erodes toward zero as expiration approaches (time decay) or as the option moves deep in- or out-of-the-money.
    Failure modes: Because extrinsic value decays nonlinearly (fastest near expiration and near the money), hedged strategies built on a constant-decay assumption misestimate carry; volatility collapses can erase extrinsic value even when the underlying is unchanged.
    Misconceptions: Extrinsic value is often equated purely with time; in fact implied volatility, interest rates, and dividends also inflate or deflate it, which is why the term is preferred when discussing non-time influences.
    Example: A $32.50-strike call trading at $1.10 with the stock at $32.80 carries $0.80 of extrinsic value ($1.10 premium minus $0.30 intrinsic), which an early exerciser forfeits.
    Extrinsic value = option premium - intrinsic value.
    Aliases: time value (broad sense)

    Time value #

    That portion of an option's premium that exceeds its intrinsic value; it reflects the probability that the option will move (further) into the money before expiration, so the longer the time remaining, the greater the time value, all else equal.
    Intuition: Time value is the price of possibility: with more days left, there is more chance for the underlying to move in your favor, and the market charges for that chance.
    Mechanics: Time value = premium - intrinsic value. It decays as expiration approaches (theta decay), with decay accelerating in the final weeks and being most pronounced for at-the-money options; higher implied volatility raises it, and it reaches zero at expiration.
    Failure modes: Long-option positions lose time value every day even when the underlying is flat, so a correct directional view can still lose money if the move comes too slowly; decay is nonlinear, so linear extrapolation of daily decay underestimates late-life erosion.
    Misconceptions: Time value does not measure how much time is left in a literal sense — two options with identical time to expiry can have very different time values because volatility and moneyness differ.
    Example: A two-week $32.50 call on a $32.80 stock priced at $1.10 has $0.80 of time value; at expiration, if the stock is still $32.80, the premium collapses to roughly its $0.30 intrinsic value.
    Time value = option premium - intrinsic value.
    Aliases: extrinsic value, time premium

    American-style option #

    An option that the holder may exercise at any time on or before the expiration date; all standardized U.S. exchange-traded equity options use American-style exercise.
    Intuition: An American option is a standing offer: any day before expiry, the holder can invoke the strike and take or deliver the underlying.
    Mechanics: Early exercise is a right, not an obligation, and exercising early forfeits any remaining time value, so holders usually sell rather than exercise; early exercise of a call becomes rational mainly to capture an impending dividend, and of a deep in-the-money put to receive strike proceeds sooner. Because the writer can be assigned at any time, short American options carry early-assignment risk.
    Failure modes: Writers who ignore dividend dates or deep-in-the-money scenarios can be assigned early at inopportune times, e.g., losing stock plus dividend entitlements or facing unexpected margin and stock-delivery obligations.
    Misconceptions: The label has nothing to do with geography or where the option trades — it names the exercise style; and early exercise is rarely optimal for the holder despite being always available, because it discards time value.
    Example: A holder of an American-style OICX July 60 call can instruct their broker to buy 100 shares at $60 on any trading day up to the July expiration, whereas a writer of that call can be assigned on any such day.
    Aliases: American option, American-style exercise
    Related:

    European-style option #

    An option that may be exercised only on its expiration date; most (but not all) U.S. index options use European-style exercise.
    Intuition: A European option is a single-day right: you can trade the contract any time, but you can invoke the strike only on expiration day itself.
    Mechanics: The holder can always close the position by selling the option in the market before expiration; only exercise is restricted to the expiry date (and the last trading day is typically the day before expiration for index options). With no early-exercise possibility, European options are simpler to price (e.g., Black-Scholes applies directly) and writers face no early-assignment risk.
    Failure modes: Holders needing to monetize before expiry must sell into the market, so wide bid-ask spreads or halted trading can trap value; confusing a European index option's last trading day with its expiration date can leave a position held unintentionally through settlement.
    Misconceptions: European-style does not mean the position is locked until expiry — closing trades are always possible; nor does the name imply the option trades in Europe.
    Example: A European-style index call can only be exercised at expiration, and its last trading day is usually the Thursday before expiration Friday; a holder wanting out on Tuesday simply sells the option on the exchange.
    Aliases: European option, European-style exercise
    Related:

    Bermudan option #

    An exotic option that can be exercised only on a specified set of predetermined dates during its life, an exercise style intermediate between American (any time) and European (at expiry only).
    Intuition: A Bermudan option opens the exercise window on a schedule — say, the first business day of each month — rather than every day (American) or just once at the end (European).
    Mechanics: The contract lists the permissible exercise dates in advance. Its value sits between that of the equivalent European and American options, and pricing typically requires numerical methods (trees or simulation) that check optimal exercise at each permitted date.
    Failure modes: Missing an exercise window is irreversible until the next scheduled date, so holders carry gap risk between windows; pricing models that ignore the discrete exercise schedule misvalue the option relative to both limiting styles.
    Misconceptions: Bermudan options are not a blend or average of American and European contracts — they are a distinct exercise schedule, and the name (like the other styles) has no geographic meaning.
    Example: A Bermudan swaption might allow the holder to enter the underlying interest rate swap only on the swap's quarterly coupon dates during a five-year window.
    Aliases: Bermuda option, mid-Atlantic option, quasi-American option
    Related:

    Option exercise #

    The act by which the holder of an option invokes the contractual right to buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying at the strike price, converting the option position into a transaction or position in the underlying.
    Intuition: Exercising is cashing in the ticket: instead of selling the option, you use it to force the trade at the strike.
    Mechanics: The holder gives irrevocable exercise instructions to their broker, which routes them to the clearing organization (OCC for U.S. securities options) before exercise cut-off times; settlement then proceeds as a purchase or sale of the underlying (physical) or a cash payment. At expiration, OCC's 'exercise by exception' automatically exercises options in-the-money by at least $0.01 unless contrary instructions are submitted. Exercising early forfeits remaining time value, so holders often prefer selling the option.
    Failure modes: Exercise requires the cash or stock to complete the underlying transaction; holders without it may be restricted by their broker. Failing to submit explicit instructions around thresholds or after-hours price moves can produce unintended exercises or lapses of in-the-money value.
    Misconceptions: Exercise is not the usual way to realize an option's value — most long options are closed by sale (historically roughly 70% of contracts are closed out and only a small share are exercised), because selling preserves time value that exercise destroys.
    Example: Exercising an OICX 60 call means buying 100 shares at $60 each; if the stock is $68 and the call trades at $8.50, selling the call realizes $8.50 while exercise-plus-sale realizes only the $8 intrinsic value.
    Aliases: exercise, exercising an option

    Option assignment #

    The designation by the clearing organization of an option writer who must fulfill the contract — selling the underlying at the strike (assigned call) or buying it at the strike (assigned put) — when a holder exercises.
    Intuition: Assignment is the other side of exercise: when a holder invokes the ticket, a writer is tapped on the shoulder and must honor the deal.
    Mechanics: When exercise notices reach the clearing organization, OCC assigns them randomly to clearing members carrying short positions in that series; members in turn allocate assignments to their customers (by random selection or first-in-first-out). Writers of American-style options can be assigned any trading day, with likelihood rising for in-the-money options near ex-dividend dates (calls) or deep in-the-money (puts).
    Failure modes: Writers can be assigned overnight and discover the resulting stock position only the next morning, leaving them exposed to opening gaps; assignment near ex-dividend dates can also force delivery of stock plus dividend obligations.
    Misconceptions: Assignment is not directed at a specific writer by the exercising holder — it is allocated by the clearing organization, so no short writer can predict or prevent being selected; also, closing the short (buy to close) eliminates assignment risk only if done before assignment.
    Example: An investor short an OICX 60 call wakes to find the call was exercised: they must sell 100 shares at $60 regardless of the current $68 market price, an immediate $8-per-share adverse fill versus the market.

    Physical settlement #

    A provision in a futures or other derivative contract requiring delivery of the actual underlying commodity or asset (or a delivery instrument such as a warehouse receipt) to satisfy the contract at maturity.
    Intuition: With physical settlement, holding the contract to the end means real goods or securities change hands — not just a check for the price difference.
    Mechanics: After the last trading day, remaining shorts tender delivery notices through the clearing organization, and remaining longs must accept delivery and pay the invoice price (e.g., for bond futures, invoice price = contract size x futures settlement price x conversion factor + accrued interest). Delivery occurs at exchange-approved facilities during the delivery period.
    Failure modes: Traders who fail to close longs before first notice day can be obligated to take (and pay for) full physical delivery, including storage, transport, and financing; squeezes on deliverable supply can distort prices near expiry.
    Misconceptions: Most futures positions never reach delivery — they are offset beforehand — but physical settlement is not merely theoretical: the deliverability of the contract is what ties the futures price to the cash market at expiry (convergence).
    Example: A refiner holding CME WTI crude futures through expiry receives delivery notices for 1,000-barrel lots at the Cushing, Oklahoma delivery point and pays the invoice price per contract.
    Aliases: physical delivery, delivery settlement

    Cash settlement #

    A method of settling futures, options, and other derivatives whereby the seller pays the buyer the cash value of the underlying, or a cash amount based on the level of an index or price, according to a procedure specified in the contract, instead of delivering the asset itself.
    Intuition: Cash settlement settles the score in money only: at expiry the contract simply pays out the difference, so no barrels, shares, or bonds ever move.
    Mechanics: At maturity, positions are valued against the final settlement price computed by the exchange's specified procedure (e.g., a special opening quotation of an index), and the clearing organization credits or debits the difference. Cash settlement is standard where the underlying is not practically deliverable, such as equity indexes.
    Failure modes: The settlement procedure itself becomes the point of fragility: if the reference price is derived from a short window or a manipulable market, the final settlement value can be distorted, and hedges calibrated to continuous prices can slip.
    Misconceptions: Cash-settled does not mean 'synthetic' or less connected to the underlying — arbitrage against the cash market still enforces convergence to the settlement value; nor does it mean daily P&L differs, since all futures are marked to market daily regardless of final settlement style.
    Example: At expiration, an E-mini S&P 500 futures position is settled in cash against the special opening quotation of the S&P 500 index; no shares of the 500 companies are delivered.

    Final settlement price #

    The price at which a cash-settled futures (or option) contract is settled at maturity, determined pursuant to a procedure specified by the exchange.
    Intuition: The final settlement price is the official last mark of a contract's life — the single number that decides the terminal cash flows for everyone still holding it.
    Mechanics: The exchange's contract rules define exactly how it is computed: common procedures include a special opening quotation based on constituent opening prices (equity index futures) or a volume-weighted average over a defined window. The clearing organization settles all remaining open positions against this price, and it need not equal the last traded price of the contract.
    Failure modes: Because the final settlement price follows its own procedure, it can deviate sharply from the contract's last traded price (e.g., an opening-rotation quotation after an overnight event), producing unexpected P&L for positions marked against the close.
    Misconceptions: It is not simply the closing price of the futures contract on the last trading day; each product's rulebook defines a distinct calculation, and the two can differ materially.
    Example: Quarterly S&P 500 index options settle against the special opening quotation (SOQ) of the index on expiration morning, so a trader holding through expiry is exposed to opening prices of the 500 constituents, not the prior close.
    Aliases: final settlement value, expiry settlement price

    Daily settlement price #

    The official daily price at which the clearing organization clears all trades and settles all accounts between clearing members for each contract month; it is the benchmark used for marking positions to market, computing margin calls, and invoicing deliveries.
    Intuition: The daily settlement price is the exchange's official 'score' for the day: everyone's futures P&L and margin requirements are recomputed against it every session.
    Mechanics: Exchanges derive it from trading activity near the close (e.g., a volume-weighted average over a designated closing period, with exchange discretion in illiquid conditions). All open positions are marked to this price daily: gains are credited and losses debited (variation margin), and for physically delivered contracts it feeds the delivery invoice price.
    Failure modes: In illiquid or disorderly closes the settlement price may differ from the last trade, so P&L and margin calls can move against a trader even when their executed prices looked favorable; it is also a manipulation target ('banging the close').
    Misconceptions: The settlement price is not necessarily the last traded price or the simple closing quote — exchanges use defined closing-period procedures; and daily price limits are measured against the prior day's settlement, not the last trade.
    Example: If yesterday's settle on a corn future was 450 cents and today's settle is 460, a long one-contract position is credited 10 cents x 5,000 bushels = $500 of variation margin overnight.

    Delivery month #

    The specified month within which a futures contract matures and can be settled by delivery, or within which the delivery period begins, as defined in the contract specification.
    Intuition: The delivery month is the contract's calendar label: 'December corn' means the corn future whose obligations fall due in December.
    Mechanics: Exchanges list a cycle of delivery months per product (e.g., March, May, July, September, December for many grains). Each month trades as a distinct contract with its own price and open interest; the nearest is the spot/front month, later ones are back months, and prices across months embed carrying costs (contango) or discounts (backwardation).
    Failure modes: Liquidity migrates from the front month to the next month well before expiry, so trading the wrong month late in its life means wide spreads and delivery risk; confusing the delivery month with the last trading day (which usually precedes it) is a common operational error.
    Misconceptions: The delivery month is not a single date but a window: the exact first/last trading and notice days are set per product and can fall in the prior month.
    Example: A trader long 'ZCZ25' holds December 2025 corn futures; as December approaches, volume and open interest shift to the March 2026 contract, prompting most traders to roll rather than risk delivery.

    First notice day #

    The first day on which notices of intent to deliver the actual commodity against open short futures positions can be issued (received by longs); the date varies by commodity and exchange.
    Intuition: First notice day is when holding a long futures position stops being purely financial: from then on, a long can be handed a delivery notice and be obliged to pay for and take the goods.
    Mechanics: In physically delivered markets, shorts may tender delivery notices from first notice day through the delivery period; the clearing organization assigns them to longs, who must pay the invoice price. First notice day often falls before the delivery month and before the last trading day, so speculators typically exit or roll long positions beforehand.
    Failure modes: Longs who overlook first notice day can be assigned delivery against their will, requiring full contract-value funding and triggering liquidation by brokers who do not support delivery; liquidity also thins as commercials dominate the final sessions.
    Misconceptions: First notice day is not the same as the last trading day, and it is not uniform across markets — each contract's calendar sets its own, sometimes weeks before trading ends.
    Example: For many CBOT grain contracts, first notice day is the last business day preceding the delivery month, so a speculator long December corn would normally exit by late November to avoid delivery assignment.

    Last trading day #

    The final day on which a futures or options contract may be traded on the exchange; after the close of the last trading day, remaining open positions proceed to settlement or delivery.
    Intuition: The last trading day is the deadline for closing a position on your own terms — miss it, and the contract's settlement machinery decides the outcome for you.
    Mechanics: Each product's specification fixes the last trading day: for standard U.S. equity options it is the expiration Friday (third Friday of the month), while for many European-style index options it is the Thursday before expiration Friday; futures contracts typically stop trading a set number of days before or within the delivery month, after which delivery or final cash settlement occurs.
    Failure modes: Positions held past the last trading day lose the ability to be closed competitively and are exposed to the exchange's settlement procedure, which can produce prices far from recent trades; low liquidity on the last day also degrades exit quality.
    Misconceptions: The last trading day is not always the same as the expiration date — for many index options trading stops the day before expiration — and it differs by product, so assuming the equity-option convention generalizes is unsafe.
    Example: A trader long a European-style index call who wants out must sell by the Thursday before expiration Friday; after that close, only the expiration-day settlement value determines the payoff.
    Aliases: LTD, final trading day
