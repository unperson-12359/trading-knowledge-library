Trading concept

An exchange rate between two currencies that does not directly involve the home reference currency — in market practice, a pair not involving the US dollar (e.g. EUR/GBP, GBP/JPY). Because most currencies are quoted primarily against the dollar, cross rates are typically derived by multiplying or dividing the two currencies' dollar rates.

Intuition

A cross rate is the exchange rate between two 'non-anchor' currencies, usually computed through their rates against the dollar.

Mechanics

The derived (implicit) cross rate is obtained as S(a/b) = S(a/$) x S($/b) using the quoted dollar pairs. Dealers quote liquid crosses directly, but the bid-ask of the implicit cross disciplines market makers: if a directly quoted cross diverges from the implicit value, triangular arbitrage becomes possible. The dollar's vehicle-currency role (on one side of 88% of all FX trades in 2022) is why crosses route through it.

Formula
S(a/b) = S(a/$) x S($/b); for quotes sharing the same base, divide: EUR/GBP = EUR/USD / GBP/USD.

Failure modes

Derived crosses inherit the spreads and stale prices of both dollar legs, so in fast or illiquid markets the implicit cross can be wrong; thin crosses have wide spreads and limited depth compared with dollar pairs.

Misconceptions

Cross rates are not independent prices: arbitrage keeps them aligned with the constituent dollar pairs, so a 'mispriced cross' is usually just a fleeting inconsistency, not a distinct market view.

Example

With EUR/USD at 1.0850 and USD/JPY at 150.00, the implicit EUR/JPY cross is 1.0850 x 150.00 = 162.75.
