Risk-neutral valuation

Trading concept

Risk-neutral valuation is the pricing principle that an asset's fair value equals the discounted expectation of its future payoff computed under the risk-neutral (equivalent martingale) measure — the probability measure in which all assets earn the risk-free rate. In a complete, arbitrage-free market this measure is unique, and every derivative has a unique arbitrage-free price under it.

Intuition

Instead of arguing about how much extra return investors demand for risk, you re-weight the odds so everyone looks risk-neutral, take the average payoff, and discount — the answer is the same either way, but much easier to compute.

Mechanics

By the fundamental theorem of asset pricing, no-arbitrage is equivalent to the existence of a risk-neutral measure Q under which discounted prices are martingales; the price of a payoff H_T is E^Q[H_T] discounted. This is why option pricing formulas contain the risk-free rate instead of the stock's expected return, and it underlies Black-Scholes, trees, and Monte Carlo methods alike.

Formula
V_0 = e^(-rT) * E^Q[payoff at T], where Q is the risk-neutral measure under which the underlying's expected return equals r.

Failure modes

In incomplete markets (jumps, stochastic volatility, untradeable risks) infinitely many risk-neutral measures exist, so 'the' price is not pinned down without additional economic choice. The method also silently assumes tradeability and financing at the risk-free rate, which fails in stressed funding markets.

Misconceptions

Risk-neutral probabilities are not the market's real-world forecasts — they are pricing weights that embed risk premia, so reading them as literal crash odds overstates tail probabilities. Risk neutrality is a computational device, not a claim that investors are indifferent to risk.

Example

A stock can go to $120 or $80 in one year, r = 5%; the risk-neutral up probability is (105 - 80)/(120 - 80) = 0.625, so a call paying max(S - 100, 0) is worth (0.625 x $20)/1.05 = $11.90 regardless of the true up probability.
