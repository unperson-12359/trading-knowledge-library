Cup and handle

Trading concept

A bullish continuation pattern popularized by William O'Neil in 'How to Make Money in Stocks' (1988), consisting of a U-shaped consolidation (the cup) followed by a smaller downward-drifting pullback (the handle) on the right side, completed by a breakout above the handle's resistance. The cup resembles a rounding bottom within an existing uptrend. It is a technical-analysis hypothesis, not a proven predictive law.

Intuition

After a strong rally the stock digests its gains in a wide bowl; the small final dip (the handle) shakes out the last weak holders before the uptrend resumes through the old high.

Mechanics

Require a prior uptrend a few months old; the cup should be U-shaped (a sharp V is disqualifying) and ideally retrace no more than one-third to one-half of the prior advance. The handle forms in the upper half of the cup, retracing up to roughly one-third of the cup's advance, over one to several weeks. The trigger is a breakout above handle resistance with a substantial volume increase; the target projects the cup's depth from the breakout point.

Formula
Price target = B + (R - C), where B = breakout price above handle resistance, R = right peak of the cup, and C = low of the cup.

Failure modes

Handles that drift too deep (retracing more than half the cup) or form in the cup's lower half signal weak demand and high failure rates; low-volume breakouts frequently fail. In bear markets the breakout often becomes a bull trap at the old high.

Misconceptions

That any V-shaped dip followed by a pullback qualifies — O'Neil's specification demands a rounded U cup, a shallow handle in the upper half, and volume expansion on the breakout. Also that the pattern works without a prior uptrend; it is a continuation pattern by construction.

Example

A stock runs from $10 to $32.69, rounds out a three-month cup with a 42% retracement, forms a six-week handle, then gaps above handle resistance at $32 on surging volume; the measured target is about $41.
