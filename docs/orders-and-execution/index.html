    Market order #

    An instruction to buy or sell immediately at the best prices available when the order reaches the market, without a specified execution-price limit.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is an instruction controlling how an attempt to buy or sell may interact with available liquidity.
    Mechanics: The order consumes eligible opposite-side quotes in priority order until filled or liquidity is exhausted, so a large order may execute at several prices.
    Failure modes: Fast prices, thin depth, venue trigger conventions, and partial fills can produce execution or non-execution different from the displayed quote.
    Misconceptions: An order's trigger or limit constrains its behavior; it does not guarantee both execution and a particular price.
    Example: A 1,000-share buy finds 400 shares at $10.00 and 600 at $10.05, giving a $10.03 average fill.
    Related:
    • Limit order
    • Slippage
    • Market impact
    Source: SEC Office of Investor Education, Trading Basics — Market, limit, stop, and stop-limit orders, pp. 1-2
    Source: SEC Investor.gov - Types of Orders — Market orders

    Limit order #

    An instruction to buy only at a stated price or lower, or sell only at a stated price or higher.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is an instruction controlling how an attempt to buy or sell may interact with available liquidity.
    Mechanics: If immediately marketable it executes against eligible prices within the limit; otherwise it rests subject to venue priority until filled, canceled, or expired.
    Failure modes: Fast prices, thin depth, venue trigger conventions, and partial fills can produce execution or non-execution different from the displayed quote.
    Misconceptions: An order's trigger or limit constrains its behavior; it does not guarantee both execution and a particular price.
    Example: A buy limit at $10 can fill at $9.98 or $10.00 but cannot fill at $10.01; it may never fill.

    Stop order #

    An instruction that activates when a specified stop condition is met and then becomes a market order.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is an instruction controlling how an attempt to buy or sell may interact with available liquidity.
    Mechanics: The broker or venue monitors the applicable trade or quote trigger; after activation, the released market order executes at available prices rather than the stop price.
    Failure modes: Fast prices, thin depth, venue trigger conventions, and partial fills can produce execution or non-execution different from the displayed quote.
    Misconceptions: An order's trigger or limit constrains its behavior; it does not guarantee both execution and a particular price.
    Example: A sell stop at $20 triggers during a gap and the resulting market order first fills at $18.75.

    Stop-limit order #

    An instruction that becomes a limit order after its stop condition is met, combining a trigger price with an execution-price boundary.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is an instruction controlling how an attempt to buy or sell may interact with available liquidity.
    Mechanics: On trigger, activate the stated buy or sell limit; it may execute at the limit or better, rest unfilled, or expire according to its time in force.
    Failure modes: Fast prices, thin depth, venue trigger conventions, and partial fills can produce execution or non-execution different from the displayed quote.
    Misconceptions: An order's trigger or limit constrains its behavior; it does not guarantee both execution and a particular price.
    Example: A sell stop-limit with stop $20 and limit $19.50 triggers at $20 but does not fill if the market gaps directly to $19.

    Market impact #

    The change in execution prices or subsequent market prices attributable to the strategy's own order interacting with finite liquidity.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is an instruction controlling how an attempt to buy or sell may interact with available liquidity.
    Mechanics: Compare execution and post-trade prices with a pre-trade benchmark while controlling for market movement; model temporary and persistent components as functions of size, liquidity, volatility, and time.
    Failure modes: Fast prices, thin depth, venue trigger conventions, and partial fills can produce execution or non-execution different from the displayed quote.
    Misconceptions: An order's trigger or limit constrains its behavior; it does not guarantee both execution and a particular price.
    Example: A buy equal to 15% of interval volume lifts several ask levels and fills 30 basis points above the arrival midpoint.

    Slippage #

    The difference between an intended or reference price and the actual execution price, signed so adverse execution is a cost.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is an instruction controlling how an attempt to buy or sell may interact with available liquidity.
    Mechanics: Choose a decision, arrival, or quote benchmark; compare each fill with it; weight by executed quantity; and separate spread, delay, and impact where possible.
    Failure modes: Fast prices, thin depth, venue trigger conventions, and partial fills can produce execution or non-execution different from the displayed quote.
    Misconceptions: An order's trigger or limit constrains its behavior; it does not guarantee both execution and a particular price.
    Example: A buy decided at $100 that fills at $100.12 has $0.12, or 12 basis points, of adverse slippage.
    Buy slippage = fill price - reference price; sell slippage = reference price - fill price
    Related:

    Trailing stop order #

    A stop order (or stop-limit order) whose stop price is not fixed but is set as a specified dollar amount or percentage away from the market price. As the price moves in the trader's favor the stop price trails it by that amount; if the price moves adversely the stop price stays fixed, and it triggers a market (or limit) order once reached.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is a stop-loss that ratchets: it follows the market when the market moves your way and never moves back, so it locks in progressively more of a favorable move without capping further gains.
    Mechanics: The broker or venue recomputes the trigger as the extreme favorable price plus/minus the trailing amount; for a sell trailing stop the trigger equals the highest price since submission minus the trail amount. Triggering conventions differ by venue: some trigger on last-sale prices, others on quotation prices. Once triggered, a plain trailing stop becomes a market order, a trailing stop-limit becomes a limit order.
    Failure modes: In a gap or fast market the triggered market order can fill far away from the trailing stop price, so the 'locked-in' level is not guaranteed. Short-term noise can also trigger the stop prematurely, whipsawing the trader out of a position.
    Misconceptions: A trailing stop does not guarantee an exit at the trailing stop price - it is a trigger, not an execution price. It also does not protect against overnight gaps, since it can only trigger while the market is trading.
    Example: You buy XYZ at $20 and place a sell trailing stop $1 below the market. XYZ peaks at $24 and falls; the trigger ratchets up to $23 and stays there, and the shares are sold (at or near $23) when the price trades down to $23.
    Sell trailing stop trigger = max(market price since order placement) - trailing amount (fixed $ or %). Buy trailing stop trigger = min(market price since order placement) + trailing amount.
    Aliases: trailing stop-loss order, trailing stop
    Related:
    • Stop order
    • Stop-limit order
    • Conditional order
    Source: SEC Investor Bulletin: Understanding Order Types — Stop, Stop-Limit, and Trailing Stop Orders

    Market-if-touched order #

    A conditional order to buy below (or sell above) the current market price that becomes a market order once the specified trigger ('touch') price trades. It is the mirror image of a stop order: a buy MIT is placed below the market and a sell MIT above it.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It lets you say 'if the price falls to X, buy me in immediately at whatever is available' - a way to enter on a pullback with certainty of execution rather than certainty of price.
    Mechanics: The order rests off-book at the broker or venue until the trigger price is touched (trade or quote, depending on venue convention); it is then released as a market order and fills at the best available prices. On CME Globex the analogous triggered market behavior is a market order released upon trigger.
    Failure modes: Because it becomes a market order, a touch during a thin or fast market can fill at a price far worse than the trigger price. A one-tick touch can also trigger entry immediately before the move fails.
    Misconceptions: Traders often confuse MIT orders with stop orders; the placement is inverted (buy MIT below the market vs. buy stop above it). Nor does the touch price guarantee the fill price.
    Example: With stock at $50, you place a buy MIT at $48. When a trade prints at $48, the order converts to a market order and fills at the best offer, perhaps $48.02.
    Aliases: MIT order, touch order
    Related:
    • Stop order
    • Limit-if-touched order
    • Market order
    • Conditional order
    Source: IBKR Guides: Order Types (Trader Workstation) — Market if Touched / Trailing Market if Touched

    Limit-if-touched order #

    A conditional order that becomes a limit order at a specified limit price once a trigger ('touch') price is reached. It combines the trigger logic of a market-if-touched order with the price protection of a limit order.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is a standing instruction: 'if the market trades down to my trigger, work a limit order at my chosen price' - you get price control, at the cost of possibly not being filled.
    Mechanics: The order is held by the broker or venue until the touch price trades; it is then submitted as a limit order at the preset limit price (for a sell LIT the limit is typically at or below the trigger, for a buy LIT at or above it). If the market moves away through the limit, the order rests unfilled.
    Failure modes: If the market gaps through both the trigger and the limit price, the order triggers but never fills, leaving the intended entry or exit unexecuted exactly when it was most wanted.
    Misconceptions: A LIT is not assured of execution once triggered - the limit price can prevent the fill. It is also not identical to a stop-limit order, whose trigger placement convention is the opposite (buy above / sell below the market).
    Example: Stock trades at $50; you place a buy LIT with trigger $48 and limit $48.10. When $48 trades, a limit order to buy at $48.10 goes live and fills only at $48.10 or better.
    Aliases: LIT order
    Related:
    • Market-if-touched order
    • Stop-limit order
    • Limit order
    • Conditional order
    Source: IBKR Guides: Order Types (Trader Workstation) — Limit if Touched / Trailing Limit if Touched

    Day order #

    An order with a time-in-force of one trading day: if it is not executed by the end of the trading session for which it was entered, it expires automatically. It is the default duration for orders when no other time frame is specified.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A day order is 'live for today only' - you do not have to remember to cancel it tonight, because the market does it for you.
    Mechanics: The order works in the order book during the session; at the close any unexecuted balance is canceled and does not carry into after-hours or the next trading day. In FIX and exchange protocols this is TimeInForce = Day (value 0), and absence of a time-in-force field generally implies Day.
    Failure modes: A resting day order placed early can be forgotten intraday and fill later at a price that no longer reflects the trader's intent after news or market moves. It also silently lapses, so a trader who wanted multi-day exposure must re-enter it.
    Misconceptions: A day order is not canceled at the instant the regular session closes in all markets - the exact expiry follows each venue's session definition, and 'day' on a 23-hour futures market spans that market's full trading day.
    Example: You place a limit order to buy 100 shares at $10 on Monday morning; if the price never dips to $10 by the close, the order expires Monday evening and is gone Tuesday.
    Aliases: day limit order
    Related:
    • Good-till-canceled order
    • Good-till-date order
    • Immediate-or-cancel order
    • Limit order
    Source: SEC Investor Bulletin: Understanding Order Types — Timing Restrictions and Trading Instructions - Day
    Source: FIX 4.2 Execution Report (MsgType 8), FIX Dictionary (Onix Solutions) — TimeInForce <59> - absence indicates Day order

    Good-till-canceled order #

    An order that remains active until it is fully executed or explicitly canceled, persisting across trading days. In practice brokers and venues cap how long a GTC order can rest (commonly 30-90 days), so 'till canceled' is bounded by the firm's maximum order age.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: A GTC order is a standing instruction - set it once and it keeps working day after day until you pull it or it fills.
    Mechanics: The unexecuted balance is carried forward each day by the broker/venue ('GT renewal' in FIX terms) and re-exposed to the market each session until filled, canceled, or aged out. Venues may adjust resting GTC orders for corporate actions such as stock splits.
    Failure modes: Forgotten GTC orders can fill weeks later into stale information - e.g., a GTC bid fills after bad news breaks but before the trader re-evaluates. Split or dividend adjustments can also leave the order at an unintended effective price.
    Misconceptions: 'Good till canceled' does not mean good forever: brokers impose maximum durations, and exchanges may purge resting orders at their own discretion or on corporate actions.
    Example: You leave a GTC limit order to sell 500 shares at $25; three weeks later the stock rallies to $25 and the order executes without you having re-entered it.
    Aliases: GTC order, good-till-cancelled order
    Related:
    • Day order
    • Good-till-date order
    • Limit order
    Source: SEC Investor Bulletin: Understanding Order Types — Timing Restrictions and Trading Instructions - Good-Til-Canceled (GTC)
    Source: FIX 4.2 Execution Report (MsgType 8), FIX Dictionary (Onix Solutions) — Handling of GT orders (GTC/GTD renewal and expiry)

    Good-till-date order #

    An order that remains active until it is executed, canceled, or a trader-specified expiry date (or date-time) is reached, whichever comes first. It generalizes the day order and the GTC order by making the expiration explicit.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It is a standing order with a calendar deadline: 'keep trying until Friday, then stop.'
    Mechanics: The broker or venue carries the open balance across sessions; when the specified ExpireDate or ExpireTime is reached, any remaining quantity is expired (canceled) and reported back - in FIX with OrdStatus/ExecType = Expired. Each venue enforces its own maximum allowed lifetime.
    Failure modes: As with GTC orders, a resting GTD order can fill long after submission when market conditions have changed. Time-zone and session-boundary conventions for the expiry date differ across venues, so the order may die earlier or later than assumed.
    Misconceptions: GTD does not expire at the start of the named date in all markets - some venues treat the order as valid through the end of that date's session, others at a specified time.
    Example: Ahead of an earnings date you place a GTD limit buy at $40 expiring next Friday; it works all week, and any unfilled balance is canceled automatically when Friday's session ends.
    Aliases: GTD order, good-till-time order (GTT)
    Related:
    • Good-till-canceled order
    • Day order
    • Limit order
    Source: FIX 4.2 Execution Report (MsgType 8), FIX Dictionary (Onix Solutions) — ExpireDate <432>/ExpireTime <126> and GT order expiry handling
    Source: Cboe Titanium U.S. Equities BOE Specification — Time in Force semantics (terminology aligned with FIX)

    Immediate-or-cancel order #

    An order that must be executed immediately, in whole or in part, against whatever liquidity is available at the moment it arrives; any unfilled remainder is canceled rather than posted. It sacrifices completion for zero resting exposure.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: An IOC is 'take what you can right now and walk away' - it never sits in the book waiting to be picked off.
    Mechanics: The venue matches the order against the top of the book (and successive levels, up to any limit price), fills what is immediately available, and cancels the rest. Reg NMS defines an automated quotation in part by its ability to immediately execute IOC orders up to displayed size and immediately cancel the unexecuted portion.
    Failure modes: In thin books most of the order can come back unfilled - an IOC 'fill' may be only a small fraction of the intended size. Sweeping multiple price levels with an IOC also pays the spread and impact at each level it consumes.
    Misconceptions: IOC does not mean all-or-nothing - partial fills are explicitly allowed (that is what distinguishes it from fill-or-kill). Nor does an IOC guarantee any execution at all.
    Example: You send an IOC limit order to buy 1,000 shares at $50.00; only 300 are offered at or below $50.00, so you get 300 shares immediately and the remaining 700 are canceled.
    Aliases: IOC order, fill-and-kill (FAK, futures equivalent)
    Related:

    Fill-or-kill order #

    An order that must be executed immediately and in its entirety; if the full size cannot be filled at once at acceptable prices, the entire order is canceled with no partial execution.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: FOK is 'all of it, right now, or nothing' - used when a partial position is worthless or unwanted.
    Mechanics: On arrival the venue checks whether the full order quantity is immediately executable within the limit price (or at the market); if yes it executes completely, otherwise the whole order is killed and nothing rests on the book.
    Failure modes: For sizes larger than displayed top-of-book liquidity, FOK orders are killed almost every time, so repeated FOK attempts can signal intent without ever transacting. In fast markets the available size can vanish between order construction and arrival.
    Misconceptions: FOK is frequently confused with all-or-none: an AON order demands full size but may wait in the market for it, while FOK also demands immediacy. FOK does not guarantee a fill - it guarantees no partial fill.
    Example: You submit a FOK order to buy 5,000 shares at $10. Only 4,200 are immediately available at $10 or better, so the entire order is canceled and no shares change hands.
    Aliases: FOK order
    Related:
    • Immediate-or-cancel order
    • All-or-none order
    • Market order
    Source: SEC Investor Bulletin: Understanding Order Types — Timing Restrictions and Trading Instructions - Fill-Or-Kill (FOK)

    All-or-none order #

    An order that may only be executed in its full specified quantity or not at all, but which - unlike a fill-or-kill order - may remain active until the full size can be executed or the order is canceled.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: AON is 'I want the whole block or nothing, and I am willing to wait' - it trades immediacy for a no-partial-fill guarantee.
    Mechanics: The order is marked with a minimum-quantity-equal-to-size condition; it rests (or is worked by the broker) until a single execution or combination can satisfy the entire quantity. Some venues do not display AON interest, and Reg NMS execution-quality statistics exclude orders to be executed only at full size from 'covered orders'.
    Failure modes: AON orders can sit unfilled for long periods because counterparties rarely show the full size at one price; the all-or-nothing constraint also removes the order from normal price-time priority on many venues, lowering fill probability.
    Misconceptions: AON is not the same as FOK (no immediacy requirement), and 'all or none' does not prevent the order from being picked off in pieces over time on venues that allow it to rest - it prevents partial *executions*, not partial interest, only where venue rules enforce it.
    Example: You place an AON order to sell 20,000 shares at $12; it remains working until a buyer (or matched crossing interest) can take the full 20,000 shares at $12 or better, otherwise it expires unfilled.
    Aliases: AON order
    Related:
    • Fill-or-kill order
    • Minimum quantity order
    • Iceberg order
    Source: SEC Investor Bulletin: Understanding Order Types — Timing Restrictions and Trading Instructions - All-Or-None (AON)
    Source: Regulation NMS, 17 CFR 242.600 (Cornell LII) — (b)(27) Covered order - exclusion of full-size-only orders

    Post-only order #

    A limit order with an instruction that it may only add liquidity to the order book: if it would execute immediately against a resting order (i.e., would 'take' liquidity), it is instead canceled or repriced one tick away from the market. It guarantees the trader acts as a maker, avoiding taker fees and often earning liquidity rebates.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Post-only is 'join the queue or don't trade at all' - you will never cross the spread, so you control fees and avoid paying for immediacy.
    Mechanics: The venue checks the order's limit price against the opposite side of the book on arrival; a marketable post-only order is rejected/canceled or slid to a non-marketable price depending on venue rules. It then rests at its limit, earning maker treatment if executed against. On derivatives platforms (e.g., Bybit) it is expressed as timeInForce = PostOnly.
    Failure modes: In a fast-moving market the order may be canceled or slid repeatedly and never rest at an executable price, so the trader misses the move entirely. Repricing-by-venue variants can also post at a price different from the one intended.
    Misconceptions: Post-only does not guarantee a fill or a rebate - it only guarantees that any execution that occurs will be as liquidity provider. Cancel-on-marketability and reprice-on-marketability are different venue behaviors, not one universal rule.
    Example: With the book at $10.00 x $10.02, you submit a post-only buy at $10.02; because it would lift the offer immediately, the venue cancels it (or posts it at $10.01), ensuring you never pay the taker fee.
    Aliases: post-only limit order, maker-only order
    Related:

    Reduce-only order #

    An order instruction, standard on derivatives and crypto exchanges, that permits the order to only reduce an existing open position: any portion that would increase or reverse the position is rejected or canceled. It is used to guarantee that stop-loss or take-profit orders cannot accidentally open a new position.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Reduce-only is a safety latch: 'this order may shrink my position but must never grow it' - so a late-triggering exit order can't flip you long when you meant to be flat.
    Mechanics: The venue checks the order against the current position before executing; if execution would increase the position (or none exists), the order is rejected or its quantity is clamped/canceled. On Bybit, for example, reduceOnly = true means the position can only reduce in size if the order is triggered, and reduce-only orders submitted with no open position are refused.
    Failure modes: If the position was already closed by another order, the reduce-only exit order is canceled, which is intended - but traders relying on it as a stop can be left with a residual position if the reduce-only quantity was clamped below the full size. Venue-specific priority rules may cancel distant reduce-only orders under margin stress.
    Misconceptions: Reduce-only is not itself an order type like limit or market; it is a modifier on another order. It also does not guarantee an exit at a particular price - only the direction of position change.
    Example: You are long 2 BTC perpetuals and place a reduce-only sell limit for 2 BTC; if the position is closed manually first, the reduce-only order is canceled instead of opening a 2 BTC short.
    Aliases: reduce only, RO order
    Related:
    • Close-only order
    • Stop order
    • Limit order
    • Conditional order
    Source: Bybit API Documentation: Place Order (POST /v5/order/create) — reduceOnly parameter - 'true means your position can only reduce in size if this order is triggered'

    Close-only order #

    An order instruction restricting the order to closing (fully exiting or reducing) an open position, so that execution can never establish or add to a position. On derivatives platforms it appears both as a standalone close-position function and as a 'close on trigger' flag on conditional orders.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Close-only means the order's only job is to get you out - it cannot turn into a new bet in the opposite direction.
    Mechanics: The platform ties the order to the open position: quantity is bounded by the position size, and if the position no longer exists when the order triggers or arrives, the order is rejected or canceled. Close-on-trigger variants attach this behavior to stop/take-profit orders so a late trigger cannot open a reverse position.
    Failure modes: A close-only order submitted after the position has already been closed is simply canceled, which can leave a hedge or paired leg unexpectedly live. During liquidation or margin stress the venue may prioritize, resize, or cancel closing orders in ways the trader did not anticipate.
    Misconceptions: Close-only is often treated as identical to reduce-only; in practice close-only usually targets full position exit (and is rejected with no position), while reduce-only strictly bounds any execution to position-reducing size. Neither guarantees execution price.
    Example: Short 5 ETH contracts, you place a close-only buy stop; when the trigger fires it buys back at most the 5 contracts needed to flatten, and if you already covered manually, the order is refused rather than opening a long.
    Aliases: close on trigger, closing-only order
    Related:
    • Reduce-only order
    • Stop order
    • One-cancels-the-other order
    Source: Bybit API Documentation: Place Order (POST /v5/order/create) — Position-closing order parameters (reduceOnly / close-on-trigger family)

    Maker-or-cancel order #

    A limit order instruction requiring the order to either post to the book as a liquidity-adding ('maker') order or be canceled immediately; if it would execute on arrival and remove liquidity, it is killed rather than executed or repriced. It is the cancel-on-marketability variant of post-only behavior.
    Intuition: Maker-or-cancel is a hard line: 'if I can't be the maker, don't trade at all' - no sliding, no accidental taker fill.
    Mechanics: On arrival the venue tests marketability; a maker-or-cancel order that would cross or lock the opposite side is canceled back to the sender, while a non-marketable one rests at its limit price and earns maker fees/rebates if later executed against. Related Cboe instructions implement post-only/book-only handling with cancel behavior for would-be removers.
    Failure modes: The order can be canceled repeatedly in a rising (for buys) or falling (for sells) market, so the strategy never establishes the intended position. Traders also confuse it with reprice variants and are surprised when nothing rests on the book.
    Misconceptions: It is not a guarantee of earning a rebate - resting maker orders may simply never be hit. And unlike some post-only implementations, maker-or-cancel never reprices; it cancels.
    Example: The market is $10.00 x $10.01; you send a maker-or-cancel buy at $10.01 - it would take the offer, so the exchange cancels it; resent at $10.00 it posts and waits for a seller to hit it.
    Aliases: MOC (maker-or-cancel), post-only (cancel variant)

    Pegged order #

    An order whose limit price is not static but automatically tracks ('pegs to') a reference price - typically the national best bid, the national best offer, or their midpoint - plus or minus an optional offset, re-pricing itself as the reference moves.
    Intuition: A pegged order floats with the market: instead of canceling and re-entering a limit order every time the quote moves, the venue re-prices it for you.
    Mechanics: The exchange computes the order's working price from the reference quote (same-side for primary peg, opposite-side for market peg, NBBO midpoint for midpoint peg) and updates it automatically as the quote changes; a cap limit price bounds how far it can follow. In exchange protocols this is OrdType = Pegged with an ExecInst selecting the peg type and a PegDifference offset (FIX tag 211).
    Failure modes: Pegged orders re-price into deteriorating quotes automatically, so they can 'chase' a market that is stepping away and fill at progressively worse relative prices. They are also vulnerable to quote flicker and to execution just before adverse moves (adverse selection on stale references).
    Misconceptions: A peg is not a fixed limit - the executed price moves with the reference, so the fill price is not known in advance (only the cap is). Nor does pegging guarantee queue priority at each new price level.
    Example: You place a midpoint-pegged buy with cap $50.00; as the NBBO moves from 49.90x49.94 to 49.96x50.00, your working price steps from $49.92 to $49.98 automatically.
    Working price = reference price + offset (buy: min(result, cap); sell: max(result, cap)), where reference is NBB/NBO (primary peg), NBO/NBB (market peg), or NBBO midpoint (midpoint peg).
    Aliases: peg order, relative order

    Midpoint peg order #

    A pegged order whose working price tracks the midpoint of the national best bid and offer, so executions occur halfway between the displayed bid and ask. It is typically non-displayed and is used to capture half the spread relative to crossing it.
    Intuition: It is a standing offer to trade 'at the halfway mark': both sides split the spread instead of one side paying it.
    Mechanics: The venue continuously re-prices the order to the NBBO midpoint as quotes move; the order rests non-displayed and executes only when contra-side interest (often a market or marketable order) is willing to trade at the midpoint. Exchange protocols denote it as a midpoint peg instruction (e.g., Cboe ExecInst M).
    Failure modes: When the NBBO is locked or crossed the midpoint is unstable, and some venues suspend midpoint matching in those states. Midpoint orders are also adversely selected: they tend to fill just before the quote moves against the resting side.
    Misconceptions: A midpoint peg does not guarantee a better net price than a market order once missed fills and adverse selection are counted; and the 'midpoint' is the NBBO midpoint, not the midpoint of any single venue's book.
    Example: NBBO is $20.00 x $20.04; your midpoint-pegged sell rests at $20.02 (non-displayed) and executes when a buy order sweeps through, saving $0.02 versus hitting the bid.
    Working price = (national best bid + national best offer) / 2.
    Aliases: midpoint peg, MPL order (NYSE)

    Primary peg order #

    A pegged order whose working price tracks the same side of the market: a buy primary peg follows the national best bid, a sell primary peg follows the national best offer, usually plus or minus an offset. It keeps the order at the top of its side's queue without crossing the spread.
    Intuition: It is 'stay on the bid (or offer) automatically' - you remain competitively posted as a liquidity provider while the market moves.
    Mechanics: The venue re-prices the order as the same-side NBBO quote moves (Cboe ExecInst R: peg Buy to NBB, Sell to NBO), with an optional offset to be more or less aggressive, subject to a cap limit. The order adds liquidity and, being displayed or non-displayed per instructions, earns maker treatment on execution.
    Failure modes: Because it only ever posts passively, a primary peg can go unfilled for an entire move; and when it does fill, it is often because a more aggressive trader expects the quote to move through it (adverse selection).
    Misconceptions: Primary peg is not a market order with a floating cap - it never takes liquidity. It also does not follow the trader's own venue quote but the national same-side best quote.
    Example: With NBBO at $30.00 x $30.02, your primary-pegged buy sits at $30.00; when the bid steps to $30.01, your order re-prices to $30.01 automatically.
    Buy working price = national best bid + offset; sell working price = national best offer - offset (bounded by the cap limit price).
    Aliases: primary peg, pegged-to-primary, relative order (passive)

    Market peg order #

    A pegged order whose working price tracks the opposite side of the market: a buy market peg follows the national best offer, a sell market peg follows the national best bid, typically with an offset to stay slightly less aggressive. It behaves like a continuously adjusted marketable limit order.
    Intuition: It is 'keep me at the ask so I can buy (or at the bid so I can sell) as the market moves' - aggressive but price-bounded.
    Mechanics: The venue re-prices the order off the contra-side NBBO quote (Cboe ExecInst P: peg Buy to NBO, Sell to NBB) with an optional offset, executing immediately if marketable and re-pegging as the quote moves, bounded by a cap limit price.
    Failure modes: It removes liquidity and pays the spread repeatedly as the quote steps away, so total cost can approach that of chasing with market orders. A one-tick-wide or flickering quote can also produce a string of small fills at worsening prices.
    Misconceptions: Market peg is the opposite reference of primary peg - traders routinely invert the two. It does not guarantee full execution: once the quote moves beyond the cap, the remainder stops following.
    Example: NBBO is $15.00 x $15.02; your market-pegged buy (offset $0.00) works at $15.02 and takes the offer; when the offer moves to $15.03, the order follows, up to your cap.
    Buy working price = national best offer - offset; sell working price = national best bid + offset (bounded by the cap limit price).
    Aliases: market peg, pegged to market, opposite-side peg

    Discretionary order #

    A limit order displayed (or ranked) at one price but carrying a hidden 'discretionary' range up to a more aggressive price at which it is willing to execute. The visible price quotes passively while the discretionary amount lets it step up (or down) to trade with incoming marketable interest.
    Intuition: It is a limit order with a secret reserve of price flexibility: 'show 10.00, but I'm really willing to pay up to 10.02 if someone comes to me.'
    Mechanics: The order rests at its ranked price; when contra-side interest arrives within the discretionary range, the venue executes at prices up to the discretion limit (in FIX carried by DiscretionInst tag 388 and DiscretionOffset tag 389). Exchange variants (e.g., midpoint discretionary orders) rank at a passive price with discretion to the NBBO midpoint, sometimes with protections that disable discretion when the quote appears unstable.
    Failure modes: Exercising discretion means trading with aggressive, possibly informed flow, so discretionary executions suffer adverse selection - fills cluster just before adverse moves. The hidden range also makes realized execution prices harder to predict and benchmark.
    Misconceptions: Discretion is not a wider displayed quote - other participants see only the ranked price. And it is broker/venue machinery, not the human 'discretionary trading' style.
    Example: You rest a buy at $25.00 with $0.03 of discretion; when a seller sweeps at $25.02, your order executes at prices between $25.00 and $25.03 even though you were only visibly bidding $25.00.
    Aliases: discretionary peg order, order with discretion

    Reserve order #

    An order that displays only a fraction of its total size to the market, holding the remainder 'in reserve' and automatically refreshing the displayed quantity from the reserve as executions occur. It is functionally the same construct as an iceberg order.
    Intuition: It is an order with a hidden back-room supply: the market sees a small clip, but each time the clip is taken, another quietly replaces it.
    Mechanics: The trader specifies total quantity and a display (refresh) quantity; the venue exposes only the display amount in the book, and when it is filled, a new clip of the same size is drawn from the reserve, typically receiving new time priority. In exchange protocols the reserve is set by a MaxFloor/display-quantity field.
    Failure modes: Refreshed clips usually go to the back of the time-priority queue at the price level, so the reserve fills more slowly than an equivalent fully displayed order. Sophisticated participants also detect the refresh pattern and infer the hidden size, reviving the very information leakage the order was meant to avoid.
    Misconceptions: A reserve order does not hide the order's existence - the displayed clip is visible and trades are printed. Nor is the reserve guaranteed to fill; it is still a limit order subject to market movement.
    Example: You want to sell 100,000 shares but display only 1,000 at a time; each time the 1,000-share clip is taken, the venue refreshes another 1,000 from the 99,000-share reserve until the full amount is done.
    Aliases: reserve size order, iceberg (displayed-reserve equivalent)
