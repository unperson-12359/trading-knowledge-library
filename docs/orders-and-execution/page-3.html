    Orders and execution

    71 concepts · showing 51–71 of 71

    Positive slippage #

    Slippage in the trader's favor: the actual execution price is better than the expected or benchmark price - a buy filled lower, or a sell filled higher, than the reference. Measured against the NBBO it is what regulators call execution with price improvement.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: You expected to pay $10.00 and got filled at $9.99 - the market slipped in your direction.
    Mechanics: Positive slippage arises when liquidity is available inside the quoted spread (midpoint or hidden interest), when prices move favorably between order decision and execution, or when wholesalers improve on the public quote for retail flow. Reg NMS execution-quality statistics record executions 'with price improvement' relative to the NBBO.
    Failure modes: Averaged positive slippage can mask toxic fills: brokers report improvement relative to NBBO even when the price moved adversely after order receipt. It is also systematically rarer for large informed orders than for small retail ones.
    Misconceptions: Positive slippage is not 'free money' from the broker - it usually reflects midpoint/hidden liquidity or timing luck, and quoted improvement statistics depend on the benchmark chosen. Slippage is not always negative by definition; the sign is empirical.
    Example: Your market buy arrives with the NBO at $20.00 but a hidden midpoint order fills you at $19.98 - two cents per share of positive slippage (price improvement).
    For a buy: positive slippage = benchmark price - execution price ( > 0 means better). For a sell: execution price - benchmark price.
    Aliases: price improvement (fill better than benchmark), favorable slippage

    Negative slippage #

    Slippage against the trader: the actual execution price is worse than the expected or benchmark price - a buy filled higher, or a sell filled lower, than the reference. It is the everyday cost of immediacy, thin liquidity, and adverse price movement during execution.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: You expected $10.00 and paid $10.03 - the three cents is negative slippage, the gap between the price you saw and the price you got.
    Mechanics: Negative slippage arises from spread crossing, walking the book beyond top level (market impact), quote movement between decision and fill (delay), and gaps through stop prices. The SEC's order-types guidance warns that in fast markets a market order's execution price often deviates from the last-traded price or real-time quote.
    Failure modes: It compounds with size and urgency: sweeping multiple levels or trading in halted-reopening and gapping markets produces the worst deviations, and stop orders convert slippage into realized losses by design (trigger then market fill).
    Misconceptions: Negative slippage is not necessarily broker malpractice - most of it is mechanical (spread, depth, volatility). Backtests that assume fills at the signal price systematically ignore it and overstate returns.
    Example: You send a market order to buy 10,000 shares quoted at $30.00; the book is thin and the order sweeps to $30.08, giving an average fill of $30.04 - four cents of negative slippage.
    For a buy: negative slippage = execution price - benchmark price ( > 0 means worse). For a sell: benchmark price - execution price.
    Aliases: adverse slippage, execution shortfall vs. benchmark

    Temporary market impact #

    The component of market impact that reflects the immediate liquidity cost of demanding execution: the price concession needed to find counterparties right now, which dissipates as the order book replenishes after the trade. It scales with execution rate and consumed depth rather than with information.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Push the book and it springs back: the extra cents you pay to get filled instantly mostly fade once you stop trading.
    Mechanics: Each aggressive child order walks available levels; once the order stops, new limit orders refill the book and prices revert toward the pre-trade level over minutes to hours. Optimal-execution models (Almgren-Chriss family) treat temporary impact as an instantaneous cost function of trading speed.
    Failure modes: The reversion assumption fails in one-way markets - what looked temporary becomes permanent when everyone trades the same direction. Estimates calibrated in calm periods also badly understate temporary impact during liquidity withdrawals.
    Misconceptions: Temporary does not mean costless: even if the price reverts afterward, your fills happened at the impacted prices. Nor is the split between temporary and permanent impact observable directly - it is a modeling decomposition.
    Example: A rapid 500,000-share buy pushes the price from $40.00 to $40.15; an hour after the order completes, the quote has settled back to $40.06 - roughly nine cents of the move was temporary impact.
    Common model: temporary impact cost per unit time = eta x (trading rate), i.e., proportional to participation rate; total temporary cost grows with the square of speed for linear price response.
    Aliases: temporary impact, transient impact

    Permanent market impact #

    The component of market impact that persists after trading ends, attributed to the information other participants infer from the order flow: the market re-prices the asset because trading itself signals that someone wants to buy or sell in size. It reflects adverse-selection and information content rather than transient liquidity demand.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: The market learns from your footprint: once participants believe a big informed buyer is active, the price stays higher even after you finish.
    Mechanics: Each trade updates other participants' beliefs about fundamental value or about the existence of a large latent order; quotes re-level and do not revert. In Kyle-style models this is the linear, information-driven response of price to signed order flow (Kyle's lambda).
    Failure modes: Permanent impact is invisible in real time - you only infer it from non-reversion, so execution algorithms can systematically underestimate it for informative flow. It also punishes predictable scheduling: the more detectable the pattern, the larger the inferred information content.
    Misconceptions: Large trades do not have permanent impact only when the trader is informed - the market cannot distinguish intent, so even uninformed size moves expectations. And impact being 'permanent' does not mean the price never moves again for other reasons.
    Example: After a fund accumulates 5% of a small-cap over two weeks, the price settles 8% higher and stays there: the market has priced in the persistent demand it detected.
    Kyle's lambda: price change = lambda x signed order flow, estimated as the regression coefficient of price changes on trade size over a window.
    Aliases: permanent impact, informational impact

    Implementation shortfall #

    The total cost of implementing an investment decision: the difference between the value of a hypothetical 'paper' portfolio traded instantly at the decision price and the value of the real portfolio after actual execution, including all fees. Introduced by Perold (1988), it is the standard total-cost benchmark in transaction cost analysis.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: It answers: 'I decided to buy when the price was $50 - after all delays, partial fills, price moves, and fees, what did that decision actually cost me relative to $50?'
    Mechanics: IS decomposes into delay cost (price move between decision and order submission), execution cost (impact and spread paid on the filled portion), opportunity cost (price move on the unfilled portion), and explicit costs (commissions, fees, taxes). Broker desks typically measure it against the arrival price because they do not observe the client's decision time.
    Failure modes: Everything hinges on identifying the true decision price and time, which are often unrecorded or ambiguous (close vs. arrival conventions differ). Unfilled-order opportunity cost is also counterfactual and easy to mis-measure in trending markets.
    Misconceptions: IS is not the same as slippage against arrival price - slippage is only the execution component; IS also charges delay and opportunity cost. Nor is a small IS always good: it can reflect a tiny completed fraction of a large intent.
    Example: A manager decides to buy 100,000 shares at a $50.00 decision price; 70,000 are filled at $50.20 and the stock closes at $50.60. IS = 70,000x$0.20 (execution) + 30,000x$0.60 (opportunity) + commissions.
    IS = (sum of fill price_i x qty_i - decision price x total intended qty) + explicit costs, for a buy; equivalently delay cost + execution cost + opportunity cost + fees.
    Aliases: IS, paper vs. reality cost

    Explicit transaction cost #

    The directly invoiced, observable costs of trading: commissions, exchange and clearing fees, and taxes or duties. They are known (or contractually set) before the trade and appear as line items rather than being inferred from prices.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Explicit costs are the receipt you can read: commission per share, exchange fee, clearing fee, stamp tax - no estimation required.
    Mechanics: Brokers charge commissions per share/contract or per notional; exchanges charge per-share execution fees (or pay rebates) per their public fee schedules; clearing agencies charge per-contract clearing fees. Rule 10b-10 confirmations must disclose the remuneration the broker received, making these costs visible to the customer.
    Failure modes: Explicit costs are small per trade but compound brutally with turnover, silently consuming strategy edge in high-frequency or high-turnover approaches. Fee schedules with tiers and caps also make the marginal cost volume-dependent and easy to mis-estimate.
    Misconceptions: Zero-commission does not mean zero explicit cost - exchange, clearing, and regulatory fees still apply, and remuneration can be embedded elsewhere (payment for order flow). Explicit costs are also the smaller half of trading cost for large orders; implicit costs usually dominate.
    Example: Buying 1,000 shares: $1.00 commission + $0.30 exchange/regulatory fees + clearing charges - all printed on the confirm, before any slippage is counted.
    Explicit cost = commission + exchange/venue fees + clearing fees + regulatory fees and taxes, summed over the trade.
    Aliases: explicit cost, direct trading cost

    Implicit transaction cost #

    The costs of trading that are not invoiced but must be inferred from prices: the bid-ask spread paid, market impact, delay (price drift while working the order), and opportunity cost of unexecuted size. They emerge from the interaction of the order with the market and usually dominate explicit costs for institutional-size trades.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: Implicit costs never appear on a statement - you discover them by comparing what you paid with what the price would have been had you not traded.
    Mechanics: They are measured against benchmarks: spread cost vs. midpoint, impact vs. pre-trade price, delay vs. decision price, opportunity cost on unfilled quantity. Implementation-shortfall decomposition is the standard framework: implicit components are exactly the delay, execution (impact), and opportunity terms.
    Failure modes: Because they are model- and benchmark-dependent, implicit costs can be understated by convenient benchmark choice (e.g., measuring vs. close instead of decision price). They are also regime-unstable: liquidity that was cheap to access yesterday can vanish in stress.
    Misconceptions: Zero-commission trading is not zero-cost trading - implicit costs remain and are invisible on the confirm. Nor are implicit costs 'just the spread': for size, impact and opportunity cost typically exceed the spread many times over.
    Example: A fund buys 3% of a stock's daily volume with no commission; the average fill is 12 cents above the arrival midpoint - an implicit cost exceeding any explicit fee it would have paid.
    Implicit cost = spread cost + market impact + delay cost + opportunity cost (the non-fee terms of implementation shortfall).
    Aliases: implicit cost, indirect trading cost

    Opportunity cost #

    In transaction cost analysis, the cost of the portion of the intended order that was never executed: the adverse price movement on the unfilled quantity between the decision price and the end of the trading horizon. It charges patience - the money not made (or extra paid) because the order did not complete.
    Open unified concept + skill view →
    Intuition: If you meant to buy 100,000 shares at $50, only got 60,000, and the price ran to $51, the 40,000 you missed cost you a dollar each in foregone upside.
    Mechanics: Computed as the unfilled quantity times the difference between the benchmark price at the end of the horizon (or cancellation) and the decision price. Passive strategies (tight limits, low participation) systematically trade lower market impact for higher opportunity cost.
    Failure modes: It is counterfactual: the 'cost' assumes the unfilled shares would have been worth buying at the horizon price, which is unknowable if the thesis changed mid-way. It also penalizes disciplined limit pricing in fast markets even when the non-fill was the right call.
    Misconceptions: Opportunity cost is not a fee and appears on no statement - but ignoring it makes passive execution look artificially cheap. Conversely, it is not always avoidable by trading faster; faster trading converts it into market impact.
    Example: You work a 50,000-share buy passively at $25.00; 30,000 fill, the stock rallies to $25.50, and you cancel - the 20,000 unfilled shares carry $10,000 of measured opportunity cost.
    Opportunity cost = (Q_intended - Q_filled) x (P_horizon - P_decision), for a buy (sign reversed for a sell).
    Aliases: missed trade cost, non-execution cost

    Delay cost #

    The component of implementation shortfall arising from the lag between the investment decision and the order's actual submission to the market: the price movement in that window, charged on the executed quantity. It measures the cost of hesitation, compliance checks, and order-handling latency before trading begins.
    Intuition: The manager decided at $50; by the time the trader's order hit the market the price was $50.10 - those ten cents are delay cost, before any impact of the trade itself.
    Mechanics: Computed as the executed quantity times the difference between the arrival price (when the order reaches the market) and the decision price. It exists because the portfolio manager's decision and the broker's execution are separated in time and often measured from different reference prices (close vs. arrival).
    Failure modes: Delay cost is only measurable if decision time and price are actually recorded - many firms approximate the decision price with the prior close, contaminating the measure with overnight moves. Overnight decision-to-open delays routinely dominate total shortfall in moving markets.
    Misconceptions: Delay cost is not the broker's fault by default - it accrues before the broker can act, often inside the manager's own workflow. It is also distinct from execution slippage, which is measured from arrival onward.
    Example: A PM decides to sell at a $40.00 close; the desk releases the order next morning with the stock already at $39.60 - the 40-cent drop on the executed shares is delay cost.
    Delay cost = Q_executed x (P_arrival - P_decision), for a buy (sign reversed for a sell).
    Aliases: delay cost (arrival shortfall), hesitation cost

    Price improvement #

    Execution of an order at a better price than the best displayed quote at the time: for a buy, below the national best offer; for a sell, above the national best bid. It is a formal execution-quality metric in Reg NMS reporting and a headline benefit claimed for retail order routing to wholesalers.
    Intuition: The screen said the offer was $20.00 but you got filled at $19.995 - that half cent per share is price improvement.
    Mechanics: Improvement comes from liquidity priced inside the spread: midpoint matches, hidden orders, exchange retail price improvement programs with sub-penny increments, and wholesalers internalizing retail flow at fractional-penny better prices. Reg NMS Rule 600 defines 'executed with price improvement' relative to the NBBO for Rule 605 reporting.
    Failure modes: Reported improvement is benchmark-sensitive: fractions of a cent vs. NBBO can coincide with worse outcomes vs. the midpoint or post-receipt prices, and aggregate statistics can flatter wholesalers. Size is limited - improvement typically applies to small retail orders, not institutional blocks.
    Misconceptions: Price improvement is not a guarantee attached to any order type - it is an opportunistic outcome. And 'improved vs. NBBO' does not necessarily mean best available outcome across all venues.
    Example: A 200-share market buy with the NBBO at $10.00 x $10.02 executes at $10.01 through a wholesaler: one cent per share of price improvement, disclosed in the venue's Rule 605 statistics.
    For a buy: improvement per share = national best offer - execution price; for a sell: execution price - national best bid.
    Aliases: execution with price improvement

    Best execution #

    The regulatory and fiduciary duty of a broker-dealer to seek the most favorable terms reasonably available for a customer's order under the circumstances - weighing price, speed, likelihood of execution and settlement, size, and nature of the order - rather than simply the nominal best price. In the U.S. it is embedded in FINRA Rule 5310 and antifraud principles; in the EU in MiFID II Article 27.
    Intuition: Your broker must work your order as a loyal agent would: not necessarily the single best tick, but the best overall outcome reasonably achievable for that order.
    Mechanics: Firms must establish policies and conduct 'regular and rigorous' reviews of execution quality across venues, considering price improvement opportunities, speed, and fill likelihood; routing decisions driven by payment for order flow must still satisfy the duty. Execution quality is evidenced through Rule 605 statistics and firm-level venue analysis.
    Failure modes: The duty is process-based, not outcome-based, so it can be technically satisfied while routing to mediocre venues; conflicts from payment for order flow and affiliated venues are the persistent weak point. Defining 'best' across speed/price/certainty trade-offs is genuinely judgment-laden.
    Misconceptions: Best execution does not mean the best possible price on every trade - speed and certainty legitimately trade off against price, and the standard is 'reasonably available' terms. Nor is it satisfied by default routing: periodic, documented venue review is required.
    Example: A broker routes a retail market order to a wholesaler offering consistent sub-penny price improvement and fast fills, and documents quarterly reviews comparing those statistics against exchange execution quality - evidencing best execution compliance.
    Aliases: best ex

    Order routing #

    The broker-dealer's decision of where to send a customer order for execution: to a national exchange, an alternative trading system, a market maker/wholesaler, an ECN, or its own inventory. Routing determines the price, speed, and likelihood of execution the customer receives.
    Intuition: The same order can live very different lives depending on which door the broker sends it through - routing is that choice of door.
    Mechanics: Brokers route manually (directed orders) or via automated routers; the choice weighs quoted price and depth, fees/rebates, speed, and any compensation such as payment for order flow. SEC Rules 605 and 606 of Regulation NMS force transparency: market centers publish execution-quality statistics and brokers publish quarterly order-routing reports.
    Failure modes: Routing optimized for the broker's economics (PFOF, exchange rebates) rather than the customer's execution quality is the structural conflict; disclosure mitigates but does not eliminate it. Static routing tables also degrade when venue liquidity shifts intraday.
    Misconceptions: Retail customers can often direct their own orders to specific venues, but few do - and 'commission-free' does not mean routing is free of economic incentives. Routing to the exchange showing the best quote is not automatically best execution either.
    Example: Your broker's router sends your 500-share market buy to a wholesaler that pays for order flow and fills it a tenth of a cent inside the offer, instead of to the exchange posting the NBBO.
    Aliases: routing

    Smart order routing #

    An automated system that splits and routes orders across multiple trading venues - lit exchanges, ECNs, and dark pools - based on real-time quotes, depth, fees, and venue behavior, in order to capture the best available prices and liquidity in fragmented markets.
    Intuition: Where a human picks one venue, the SOR reads every book at once and sends each child order wherever the deal is currently best.
    Mechanics: The SOR maintains a consolidated view of venue order books, then creates child orders - typically aggressive IOCs to sweep displayed liquidity or passive posts where rebates are attractive - and re-routes dynamically when quotes change or child orders are rejected. It proliferated after Regulation NMS (US) and MiFID (EU) fragmented liquidity across venues.
    Failure modes: The consolidated book is built from feeds with latency, so the SOR can route to quotes that no longer exist, generating rejects and partial fills; each extra hop also adds latency. Complexity itself is a risk - misconfigured venue preferences or fee tables produce systematically bad routing.
    Misconceptions: 'Smart' refers to venue selection, not price prediction - an SOR does not know where the market is going. And routing to the best displayed price is not always cheapest once fees, rebates, and information leakage are priced in.
    Example: Your 5,000-share buy is split by the SOR: 1,500 to Exchange A at $20.00, 1,000 to an ECN at $20.00, and the balance posted passively on the venue offering the highest rebate.
    Aliases: SOR, smart order router

    Internalization #

    The practice of a broker-dealer executing a customer's order against its own inventory as principal (or crossing it in-house against other customer orders) instead of routing it to an exchange or external market maker. The executing firm internalizes the trade and captures the spread itself.
    Intuition: Instead of sending your order out to find a counterparty, your broker's own firm is the counterparty.
    Mechanics: Wholesale market makers receive retail order flow (often via payment for order flow arrangements) and fill it from inventory, typically at the NBBO or with fractional price improvement. Reg NMS counts such firms as 'trading centers', and their executions feed Rule 605 quality statistics like exchange executions.
    Failure modes: Internalized flow never reaches the public book, so displayed liquidity understates true trading interest and price formation loses informed-by-retail interaction. The dealer also faces adverse selection on the flow it chooses not to internalize, and conflicts arise when internalization prices merely match rather than improve the NBBO.
    Misconceptions: Internalization is not illegal frontrunning - it is a regulated execution model subject to best execution and trade-through constraints. Nor does it necessarily harm customers: internalizers commonly offer fractional price improvement over the public quote.
    Example: Your 300-share market order never touches an exchange: the wholesaler your broker routes to sells you the shares from its own account at $0.001 better than the public offer.
    Aliases: internalised execution, in-house execution

    Payment for order flow #

    Compensation a broker-dealer receives from a market maker, exchange, or other firm in return for routing customer orders to it for execution. Formally, SEC Rule 10b-10(d)(8) defines it as any monetary payment, service, property, or other benefit resulting in remuneration to the routing broker or dealer in return for order routing.
    Intuition: The wholesaler pays your broker for the right to fill your trades - that payment, not a commission, is how many 'free' brokers get paid.
    Mechanics: Retail brokers route order flow to wholesalers (e.g., Citadel Securities, Virtu) under PFOF arrangements; the wholesaler profits from the spread and internalizes the flow, sharing part of the economics with the broker and often passing fractional price improvement to the customer. Brokers must disclose PFOF on trade confirmations (10b-10) and in quarterly Rule 606 routing reports.
    Failure modes: The conflict of interest is structural: brokers are paid per order routed, creating incentive to route to the highest payer rather than the best executor and to encourage order flow itself. Execution-quality differences of fractions of a cent, scaled across millions of orders, can exceed the commissions saved.
    Misconceptions: PFOF does not mean customers trade at worse-than-displayed prices - orders must still execute at or inside the NBBO, and studies find small net improvement on many retail fills; the debate is about conflicts and aggregate execution quality. It is also banned in several jurisdictions (UK, EU, Canada, Australia), not a universal practice.
    Example: A zero-commission broker routes your 100-share order to a wholesaler that pays it $0.002 per share; you are filled $0.01 inside the offer, the wholesaler keeps the rest of the spread economics.
    Aliases: PFOF

    Maker fee #

    The charge (or, on maker-taker venues, typically a rebate) applied to an execution that adds liquidity - i.e., a resting order that is traded against. In classic U.S. equity maker-taker pricing the 'maker' is paid a rebate rather than charged; on taker-maker venues and most crypto exchanges the maker pays a (usually smaller) fee.
    Intuition: The maker posts and waits; the venue prices that patience differently from aggression - sometimes rewarding it, sometimes charging less for it.
    Mechanics: Exchanges publish per-share/per-contract schedules distinguishing adding from removing liquidity; e.g., Nasdaq pays tiered per-share rebates for displayed liquidity added (around $0.0018-$0.00305 depending on volume tier) while charging for removal. Whether an execution is maker or taker is determined by which side rested in the book first.
    Failure modes: Maker economics distort order placement: traders post sub-optimally priced orders to harvest rebates, and queue formaker status can mean missed fills that cost more than the rebate earned. Fee tiers also make marginal costs volume-dependent and opaque to end clients.
    Misconceptions: 'Maker fee' on U.S. equity exchanges is usually negative - a rebate - while on crypto venues makers genuinely pay a fee; the same word covers both signs. Being a maker is about resting first, not about using a limit order (a marketable limit order is a taker).
    Example: Your resting limit sell is hit by an incoming market order; as the liquidity adder you receive Nasdaq's $0.0020-per-share add rebate instead of paying the $0.0030 removal fee.
    Aliases: liquidity-adding fee, maker charge

    Taker fee #

    The fee charged on an execution that removes liquidity - an order that executes immediately against resting interest. Under maker-taker pricing the taker pays the venue's highest per-share fee, funding the rebates paid to makers.
    Intuition: Crossing the spread for immediacy has a price: the taker fee is the toll for not waiting.
    Mechanics: Any order that executes on arrival - market orders, marketable limits, IOC sweeps - is the liquidity remover and is charged the removal rate (e.g., Nasdaq's $0.0030 per share for shares at or above $1). Taker status, not order type, determines the charge: a limit order that is marketable on arrival takes.
    Failure modes: Aggressive strategies systematically pay the taker rate on every fill, and at scale the fee rivals or exceeds the spread itself - high-frequency takers can see fees consume their entire margin. Volume-tiered pricing also means the realized rate depends on month-end totals, complicating per-trade accounting.
    Misconceptions: A limit order is not exempt from taker fees - marketability at arrival, not order type, decides maker/taker. Nor are taker fees profit for the broker: they are exchange charges, often passed through.
    Example: You send a marketable IOC buy for 10,000 shares; as the remover you pay roughly $30 in taker fees at $0.0030 per share, while the resting sellers collect maker rebates.
    Taker fee = removal rate x shares executed (e.g., $0.0030/share on Nasdaq's standard tier).

    Rebate #

    A payment from an exchange to the party whose resting order added liquidity and was executed against - the 'maker' side of maker-taker pricing. Rebates reward displayed liquidity provision and are funded by the fees charged to liquidity removers.
    Intuition: The exchange pays you to post: your standing bid or offer is inventory the venue wants, and the rebate is the bounty for supplying it.
    Mechanics: Venues publish tiered rebate schedules keyed to the member's monthly volume share; e.g., Nasdaq's add-displayed-liquidity rebates range from about $0.0018 to over $0.003 per share depending on consolidated-volume tier, with separate schedules for midpoint, non-displayed, and retail liquidity. Rebates accrue per execution and are settled monthly.
    Failure modes: Rebate harvesting distorts quoting: strategies post not to trade well but to collect payments, adding flickering liquidity that vanishes under stress. The conflict is acute when brokers keep rebates instead of passing them to clients whose orders earned them.
    Misconceptions: A rebate is not a discount on a fee already paid - it is a cash credit for adding liquidity, and it can make the net cost of a passive fill negative. Rebate rates are also not universal: they depend on venue, tape, tier, and order attributes.
    Example: A market-making firm adds 1.6% of consolidated volume in a month and earns Nasdaq's top-tier $0.00305 per share on every displayed share that is hit - millions of dollars of rebate income.
    Monthly rebate = sum over executions of (rebate rate for the applicable tier x shares executed).
    Aliases: liquidity rebate, maker rebate

    Exchange fee #

    The charges levied by a trading venue on executions and related order activity: per-share or per-contract fees for removing (and on some venues adding) liquidity, routing fees for orders sent to other venues, and auction participation fees. They are published in each exchange's fee schedule and passed through to end clients by brokers.
    Intuition: Every fill on an exchange carries a meter - fractions of a cent per share in equities, visible only on the venue's price list and your broker's fine print.
    Mechanics: Equity exchanges charge per share executed with maker/taker differentiation and volume tiers (e.g., Nasdaq's $0.0030/share removal fee, routing fees of $0.0030-$0.0035 to away venues, and per-share opening/closing cross fees); futures exchanges charge per contract. Reg NMS caps access fees on protected quotations.
    Failure modes: Tiered, monthly-settled schedules make the true marginal fee hard to know at trade time, and routing choices made to minimize venue fees can conflict with execution quality. For active traders, cumulative exchange fees can exceed commissions.
    Misconceptions: Exchange fees are not the same as commissions - they are charged by the venue to the member firm, which may or may not pass them through transparently. 'Fee-free' marketing almost never means venue fees disappear.
    Example: A 20,000-share removal on Nasdaq costs $60 in exchange fees at $0.0030/share; routed to NYSE instead, the same shares would carry a $0.0030 routing fee plus any away-venue charges.
    Venue charge per trade = applicable per-share or per-contract rate x quantity, per the venue's fee schedule and the member's tier.

    Clearing fee #

    The fee charged by a clearing organization (central counterparty) for clearing and settling a trade - standing between buyer and seller, managing margin and default risk, and effecting final settlement. It is charged per contract or per trade to clearing members and passed through to customers.
    Intuition: After the trade, someone has to guarantee both sides perform; the clearing fee pays the clearinghouse for being that guarantor.
    Mechanics: Clearing agencies publish fee schedules filed with regulators; e.g., OCC charges a per-contract clearing fee ($0.025 as of January 2025, previously with a flat $55 cap for very large transactions), set under its capital management policy to cover operating expenses and risk buffers. Futures clearing works similarly at CME and other DCOs on a per-contract basis.
    Failure modes: Clearing fees are small but unavoidable on every cleared trade and scale linearly with contract volume, mattering most to high-turnover derivatives strategies. They also change by regulatory filing on notice, so cost models must track fee schedules.
    Misconceptions: Clearing fees are not exchange fees or broker commissions - they compensate the CCP's guarantee and settlement function, a different entity with its own schedule. They apply even on 'commission-free' trades in cleared products.
    Example: A 100-lot listed options trade incurs a $2.50 OCC clearing fee at $0.025 per contract, on top of any exchange execution fee and broker commission.
    Clearing fee = per-contract rate x number of contracts (e.g., $0.025/contract at OCC).

    Commission #

    The fee a broker charges a customer for executing a trade, historically per trade or per share/contract. Commissions are the visible, contractual component of trading cost and are disclosed on confirmations; many retail equity brokers have moved to zero commission, monetizing order flow instead.
    Intuition: The commission is the broker's sticker price for doing the trade - the one cost line everyone sees before the market takes its invisible share.
    Mechanics: Charged as a flat ticket charge, per share/contract, or as a percentage of principal; Rule 10b-10 requires written disclosure at or before completion of the transaction of the remuneration the broker received from the customer. Zero-commission models substitute payment for order flow and other revenue, shifting rather than eliminating cost.
    Failure modes: Per-trade commissions penalize small and frequent orders disproportionately, distorting strategy design; conversely, zero-commission pricing obscures the real cost structure and encourages overtrading. Per-share schedules with minimums can also exceed the expected value of tiny trades.
    Misconceptions: Zero commission is not zero cost - spread, impact, exchange/clearing fees, and order-flow economics remain. Nor is the commission the biggest cost for size: implicit costs dominate institutional trading.
    Example: A broker charging $0.005 per share bills $5.00 on a 1,000-share order; the confirm discloses this remuneration alongside any fees, separate from whatever the venue charged.
    Total commission = per-unit rate x quantity (or flat ticket charge), per the brokerage agreement.
    Aliases: brokerage commission
