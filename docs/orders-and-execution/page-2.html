    Iceberg order reviewed #

    A large order of which only a small 'tip' is visible in the public order book at any time, with the bulk hidden below the surface and displayed in successive clips as each tip is executed. The purpose is to reduce market impact and information leakage when working a large position.
    Intuition: Like the iceberg, most of the order is underwater - other traders see a small order, not the wall of supply or demand behind it.
    Mechanics: The venue shows the specified peak/display size; once it executes, the next clip is automatically displayed, usually losing time priority on refresh. Some platforms randomize the display size (e.g., IBKR's Dark Ice randomizes +/-50%) to make the pattern harder to detect.
    Failure modes: Detection algorithms watch for repeated refills at one price and can trade ahead of the remaining iceberg, so impact reduction is imperfect. Refresh priority loss also stretches execution time, increasing exposure to adverse market moves (timing risk).
    Misconceptions: Icebergs are not invisible - both the tip and the resulting trade prints are public, and the hidden portion has no standing to trade until displayed. An iceberg also does not reduce the price impact of aggressive orders; it only works for resting liquidity.
    Example: A fund needs to buy 500,000 shares; it posts an iceberg showing 5,000 shares at the bid, and the venue keeps renewing the 5,000-share display until the whole 500,000 is accumulated.
    Aliases: iceberg, hidden-size order
    Related:

    • Hidden order

    Hidden order reviewed #

    A limit order whose full size is never displayed in the public order book; it rests at the venue invisibly and executes only when incoming marketable orders trade at its price. Hidden orders provide no pre-trade transparency but still must execute within Reg NMS price constraints.
    Intuition: A hidden order is a limit order nobody can see - it waits in the dark and only reveals itself by trading.
    Mechanics: The venue accepts the order with a non-displayed/invisible display indicator (e.g., Cboe DisplayIndicator = Invisible); it takes no part in the displayed quote and, at the same price, yields priority to displayed orders. Midpoint pegs and reserve icebergs are common hidden-order forms.
    Failure modes: Hidden orders lose queue priority to displayed interest at the same price and are invisible to liquidity-seeking flow, so fill rates are low precisely when displayed size is thin. They are also systematically adversely selected, filling most often when the market is about to move through their price.
    Misconceptions: Hidden does not mean exempt from trade reporting - executions are printed to the tape like any other trade. Nor does hidden interest get equal standing at a price: displayed orders rank ahead of it.
    Example: You place a hidden buy limit for 10,000 shares at $19.98; the public book shows nothing, but a market sell order sweeping to $19.98 executes against you.
    Aliases: non-displayed order, invisible order
    Related:
    • Iceberg order
    • Reserve order
    • Midpoint peg order
    • Limit order
    Source: Cboe Titanium U.S. Equities BOE Specification — DisplayIndicator = Invisible (non-displayed orders)
    Source: Regulation NMS, 17 CFR 242.600 (Cornell LII) — (b)(73)-(74) Orders providing/removing liquidity

    Minimum quantity order reviewed #

    An order carrying a minimum execution quantity condition: it may execute only if at least the specified minimum number of shares or contracts can fill at once (or per execution), with smaller available quantities skipped or the order canceled.
    Intuition: It says 'don't bother me for scraps' - useful when tiny partial fills cost more in fees or operational overhead than they are worth.
    Mechanics: The venue checks available contra-side size against the MinQty value (FIX tag 110); if the immediately executable quantity meets the minimum, the order trades (often as IOC/FAK), otherwise nothing executes. On CME Globex, MinQty is used with fill-and-kill orders as the minimum quantity for immediate execution.
    Failure modes: The order can miss fills even when aggregate book depth far exceeds its size, because no single price level (or immediate match) meets the minimum. Setting the minimum too high relative to typical displayed size makes execution rare.
    Misconceptions: Minimum quantity is not all-or-none: once the minimum is met, additional partial fills beyond it are allowed. It is also an execution condition, not a display condition - it does not hide size.
    Example: You send a FAK buy for 1,000 contracts with MinQty 400; only 250 are immediately available, so nothing executes and the order is killed rather than leaving a 250-contract stub.
    Aliases: min-quantity order, MinQty order
    Related:

    • Fill-or-kill order

    Conditional order reviewed #

    An order that is submitted to the market (or canceled) only when one or more user-defined conditions are met - typically a price, time, or event condition on the same or another instrument. Stop, trailing-stop, and if-touched orders are all specific conditional orders.
    Intuition: It is an 'if this, then trade' instruction held by the broker: nothing exists in the market until the condition fires.
    Mechanics: The broker or venue monitors the condition (e.g., last price of a defined contract reaching a level) and transmits the attached market or limit order when it evaluates true. Because the order is held off-book until then, trigger evaluation conventions (last trade vs. quote) and trigger latency are venue-specific.
    Failure modes: The held order is invisible to the market, so it has no queue priority when triggered and can miss fast moves entirely. Platform outages or a halt in the condition instrument can also prevent the trigger from ever being evaluated.
    Misconceptions: A conditional order does not reserve execution at the condition price - after triggering it is just an ordinary market or limit order. Nor is it necessarily exchange-native; many conditional orders live only in the broker's system.
    Example: You set a conditional order: if the S&P 500 futures fall below 5,000, submit a limit order to buy 100 shares of an index ETF at $450; the buy order only exists once the futures condition fires.
    Aliases: contingent order, trigger order
    Related:
    • Stop order
    • Market-if-touched order
    • One-triggers-the-other order
    • Bracket order
    • Trailing stop order

    One-cancels-the-other order reviewed #

    A linked group of two (or more) orders in which the execution of any one order automatically cancels the other(s). It is typically used to pair a profit-taking limit order with a protective stop order on the same position so that only one exit ever executes.
    Intuition: It is an either/or bracket: 'sell at my target or at my stop - whichever happens first - and kill the other one.'
    Mechanics: The broker links the orders under a group identifier (e.g., an OCA group name); when one member fills, the system cancels the rest of the group. Because the linkage is usually managed by the broker rather than the exchange, cancellation of the sibling is subject to processing latency.
    Failure modes: In fast markets both legs can execute before the cancel message propagates, leaving the trader with an unintended opposite position. If the broker's system fails, the surviving leg may remain live unexpectedly.
    Misconceptions: OCO linkage is not atomic at most venues - 'cancels' is a system action after the fill, not a simultaneous guarantee. It is also not the same as one-triggers-the-other, where one order's fill activates the second rather than canceling it.
    Example: Long 1,000 shares at $50, you place an OCO pair: a sell limit at $55 and a sell stop at $47. If the stop triggers, the $55 limit is canceled automatically; if $55 is hit first, the stop is canceled.
    Aliases: OCO order, one-cancels-all (OCA), order-cancels-order
    Related:

    • One-triggers-the-other order

    • Stop order

    One-triggers-the-other order reviewed #

    A linked pair of orders in which the second (child) order is dormant and is only submitted to the market when the first (parent) order executes. It chains an entry and a contingent follow-on order, such as 'buy if filled, then place my exit'.
    Intuition: It is sequencing built into the order ticket: the second trade arms itself the moment the first one goes through.
    Mechanics: The parent order works normally; on execution the broker's system transmits the child order (market, limit, or stop) with its preset parameters. The child does not exist in the market before the parent fills, and canceling the parent normally cancels the whole chain.
    Failure modes: Between the parent's fill and the child's submission the position is unprotected; a gap in that window (e.g., over a halt or outage) can leave an unhedged position. If the parent partially fills, behavior for the child (fire vs. wait) is platform-specific and often misunderstood.
    Misconceptions: OTO does not guarantee the child fills - once triggered it is an ordinary order subject to market conditions. It is also distinct from OCO, where one execution cancels rather than activates the sibling.
    Example: You place an OTO: buy 500 shares at $20 (parent); when it fills, a sell stop at $19 (child) is submitted automatically to protect the new position.
    Aliases: OTO order, order-triggers-order, order-sends-order (OSO)
    Related:
    • One-cancels-the-other order
    • Bracket order
    • Conditional order
    Source: IBKR Guides: Order Types (Trader Workstation) — Attached/conditional order families (bracket and transmitted-on-fill orders)
    Note: OTO naming and mechanics are broker/platform-specific (IBKR implements the pattern via attached orders and brackets); no tier-1 regulatory definition exists.

    Bracket order reviewed #

    A compound order that surrounds a primary (parent) order with two opposite-side exit orders: a profit-taking limit order on one side and a protective stop order on the other. Once the parent fills, the two exits work as an OCO pair - whichever executes first cancels the other.
    Intuition: It packages a complete trade plan in one ticket: entry, target, and stop, so the position is never left unmanaged.
    Mechanics: A buy parent is bracketed by a high-side sell limit and a low-side sell stop (mirror image for a sell parent). The exit orders are transmitted when the parent fills; execution of either exit cancels its sibling. The whole structure is maintained by the broker's order-management system.
    Failure modes: The stop leg still becomes a market order when triggered, so gap moves can exit far below the intended stop price. Partial parent fills and cancel-replace races can also leave mismatched exit quantities.
    Misconceptions: A bracket does not guarantee bounded loss - the stop leg guarantees an exit attempt, not an exit price. It is also not an exchange-native type on most venues; it is broker-side order logic.
    Example: You buy 200 shares at $100 with a bracket: sell limit at $108 and sell stop at $96. The stock rallies to $108, the limit fills, and the $96 stop is canceled automatically.
    Aliases: bracket, OCO bracket with parent
    Related:

    Source: IBKR Guides: Order Types (Trader Workstation) — Bracket orders

    Scale order reviewed #

    An order that breaks a large total quantity into a series of limit orders placed at progressively more favorable prices (e.g., each buy clip lower than the last), so the position accumulates or distributes gradually across a price range instead of at a single level.
    Intuition: Scaling is dollar-cost-averaging inside the order book: you ladder your bids down (or offers up) and let the market come to you in pieces.
    Mechanics: The platform generates child limit orders at user-defined price intervals and sizes (equal or graduated) and works them simultaneously or sequentially; fills at successive levels produce a volume-weighted average entry between the best and worst level. Scaling tools (e.g., IBKR ScaleTrader) automate clip size and spacing.
    Failure modes: If the price never reaches the deeper levels, only part of the intended position is established (opportunity cost); if it falls through all of them, the trader is fully invested into a continuing decline - the structure concentrates fills exactly when the market is moving against the position.
    Misconceptions: A scale order is not a single order at an average price - it is a bundle of independent limit orders, each of which can fill or not. It also does not reduce market impact the way slicing over time does, because all levels can be visible at once.
    Example: You scale into 10,000 shares with bids of 2,000 shares each at $50.00, $49.50, $49.00, $48.50, $48.00; fills at the first three levels give a 6,000-share position at a $49.50 average.
    Average entry = sum(clip price x clip size) / total filled size.
    Aliases: scale-in/scale-out order, scaled order
    Related:
    • Ladder order
    • Iceberg order
    • TWAP order
    • Limit order
    Source: IBKR Guides: Order Types (Trader Workstation) — Order types list (scaling/laddering order tools)
    Note: Scale orders are broker/platform-implemented order tools (e.g., IBKR ScaleTrader), not regulator-defined; cited to platform documentation.

    Ladder order reviewed #

    An order strategy that places a series of limit orders at successively higher (for sells) or lower (for buys) price levels, forming a 'ladder' of resting interest across a price range. It is in practice a synonym or close variant of the scale order.
    Intuition: Like rungs on a ladder, each order sits one step further from the current price, catching the market at progressively better levels.
    Mechanics: Each rung is an independent limit order with its own size; as price moves through the range, rungs execute in sequence, producing a blended average price. On retail futures/crypto platforms the ladder is often created by one-click bulk placement along the price axis.
    Failure modes: Fully visible ladders reveal the trader's entire price map and can be traded against; and as with scaling, fills concentrate on the wrong side of trends, so the ladder completes exactly when the position is least wanted.
    Misconceptions: A ladder is not an exchange order type - it is a placement pattern executed with ordinary limit orders, and it does not guarantee an average price because upper rungs may never fill.
    Example: Exiting a long, you ladder sell limits at $101, $102, $103, and $104 for a quarter of the position each; a rally to $103 fills three rungs at a $102 average.
    Blended price = sum(rung price x rung size) / total filled size.
    Aliases: laddered order
    Related:
    • Scale order
    • Limit order
    • Iceberg order
    Source: IBKR Guides: Order Types (Trader Workstation) — Order types list (scaling/laddering order tools)
    Note: Ladder orders are a platform/trading-desk placement pattern rather than a regulator- or exchange-defined type; treated as a scale-order variant and cited to platform documentation.

    TWAP order reviewed #

    An algorithmic execution order that slices a large parent order into smaller pieces released at regular time intervals over a specified period, aiming for an average execution price close to the time-weighted average price of the market over that period.
    Intuition: TWAP spreads the trade evenly across the clock - same size every few minutes - so no single moment's price dominates the result.
    Mechanics: The algo divides the schedule into equal time buckets and submits a child order in each (market, or limit subject to user conditions such as marketability or midpoint matching), regardless of volume patterns. Because it ignores volume, TWAP trades through thin periods at the same rate as busy ones.
    Failure modes: The rigid time schedule is predictable and volume-blind: it keeps trading in illiquid stretches (moving the price) and under-participates during heavy volume, so the realized average can deviate from both the market TWAP and VWAP. Incomplete fills at the end time leave residual size.
    Misconceptions: TWAP does not guarantee an average price equal to the market's TWAP - child-order timing, price limits, and missed fills cause deviation. Nor is time-slicing inherently low-impact if the clips are large relative to liquidity in each interval.
    Example: A 120,000-share buy is worked via TWAP over 2 hours: the algo submits roughly 1,000 shares every minute, so the final average price approximates the average of prices across the two hours rather than one moment's quote.
    TWAP over n equal time slices = (1/n) x sum of the price P_i observed in slice i.
    Aliases: TWAP, time-weighted average price order
    Related:

    VWAP order reviewed #

    An algorithmic execution order that slices a large parent order in proportion to expected or realized market volume over a period, aiming for an average execution price at or better than the volume-weighted average price (VWAP) of the market for that period. It is the standard passive benchmark for institutional execution.
    Intuition: VWAP trading hides in the crowd: you trade most when the market trades most, so your footprint tracks everyone else's.
    Mechanics: The algo forecasts the intraday volume profile (typically U-shaped) and schedules child orders so participation matches that profile, adjusting to realized volume as the day progresses. Brokers may also offer 'guaranteed VWAP' execution, where the broker bears the slippage risk for a higher commission.
    Failure modes: Volume-forecast errors skew the schedule (e.g., a surprise midday spike the algo did not anticipate), and because VWAP is a moving target computed from all trades including your own, heavy participation can push the benchmark itself. Chasing a full-day VWAP also means holding price risk until the end of the period.
    Misconceptions: Beating VWAP is not proof of a good trade - it measures execution against the day's volume-weighted average, not against the decision price; a trader can beat VWAP while the stock ran away from the arrival price. VWAP orders also do not guarantee the benchmark price.
    Example: A pension fund must buy 2% of a stock's expected daily volume; the VWAP algo buys in proportion to the volume curve through the session, and the desk is evaluated on the slippage between its average fill price and the day's VWAP.
    VWAP = sum(price_i x volume_i) / sum(volume_i), over all trades i in the period.
    Aliases: VWAP, volume-weighted average price order
    Related:

    POV order reviewed #

    An algorithmic execution order that targets a fixed percentage of the market's traded volume: the algo speeds up when market volume rises and slows when it falls, so the order continuously participates at the specified rate until complete.
    Intuition: POV is 'stay a constant fraction of the flow' - if you ask for 10% of volume, the algo keeps buying (or selling) so that you are roughly one tenth of everything that prints.
    Mechanics: The algo watches real-time volume and submits child orders to keep cumulative participation near the target rate (typically user-set between 1% and 50%), within start/end times and optionally with a 'never take liquidity' constraint that restricts it to passive fills at the cost of completion risk.
    Failure modes: Completion time is unbounded: in a dead market the order may never finish, while a volume surge forces aggressive catch-up trading with elevated impact. A fixed participation rate also becomes predictable and can be gamed by other participants watching the flow.
    Misconceptions: POV does not cap the order's share of daily volume in advance - it tracks volume as it happens, so total execution time and end-time price exposure are unknown when the order is placed. Nor does a low participation rate guarantee low impact.
    Example: You work a 300,000-share sell at a 10% POV target; in an hour when 500,000 shares trade, the algo sells about 50,000, then throttles back when volume dries up.
    Participation rate = cumulative own executed volume / cumulative market volume over the same window; the algo steers this toward the target percentage.
    Aliases: POV, percentage-of-volume order, participation order
    Related:

    Implementation shortfall algorithm reviewed #

    An execution algorithm whose objective is to minimize implementation shortfall - the total cost of executing versus the price at the moment the trading decision was made. It trades off the market impact of trading quickly against the timing (price) risk of trading slowly, front-loading execution when risk dominates.
    Intuition: The IS algo asks: 'every minute I wait, the price can run away; every clip I slam, I move the price' - and picks the schedule that balances those two costs against the decision price.
    Mechanics: Given an urgency/risk-aversion parameter and a participation cap, the algo computes an execution schedule that front-loads size (more at the start, tapering later), typically using hidden order placement and opportunistic liquidity capture; higher urgency trades faster and accepts more impact. Benchmark is the arrival/decision price rather than VWAP.
    Failure modes: Impact and risk models are estimates: in a volatility spike the schedule can be badly miscalibrated, either overpaying impact or under-trading into a runaway market. Model-driven urgency also makes behavior sensitive to parameter choices that are hard to verify ex ante.
    Misconceptions: Minimizing shortfall does not mean beating VWAP or always trading immediately; for patient mandates the optimal IS schedule can be slow. And the decision-price benchmark penalizes delay, which VWAP-style benchmarks do not.
    Example: A manager decides to buy 1 million shares with the stock at $50.00; the IS algo executes 40% in the first hour and tapers off, accepting some impact to avoid the risk that the price drifts to $50.50 before completion.
    IS = execution cost (avg fill price vs. decision price x filled qty) + delay cost (price move before submission) + opportunity cost (price move x unfilled qty); the algo minimizes the expected IS under an impact-vs-risk trade-off.
    Aliases: IS algorithm, arrival-price / IS algo

    Arrival price algorithm reviewed #

    An execution algorithm that aims to achieve, over the life of the order, an average price at or better than the bid/ask midpoint prevailing when the order was submitted (the arrival price). It is the broker-side implementation of the implementation-shortfall objective.
    Intuition: It measures success against 'the price when I walked in the door' and trades faster the more you fear the price drifting away from it.
    Mechanics: The user sets an urgency/risk-aversion level and a maximum percent of average daily volume; higher urgency accelerates the schedule (accepting more market impact), lower urgency stretches it to reduce impact at greater timing risk. Orders are worked largely hidden to limit information leakage, with user-defined start/end times.
    Failure modes: The arrival price is a transient snapshot: in volatile markets it may be unrepresentative within seconds, so performance against it is noisy. High urgency settings recreate the impact of a market order; low settings can leave the order incomplete in a trending market.
    Misconceptions: Arrival price is not the last trade before submission in all conventions - many desks use the quote midpoint. And an arrival-price algo does not promise to beat the arrival price, only to optimize the impact/risk trade-off against it.
    Example: An order to sell 800,000 shares arrives with the quote at $75.00 x $75.04 (arrival price $75.02); set to 'aggressive', the algo completes within two hours at an average of $74.97 - five cents of measured shortfall.
    Benchmark: arrival price = midpoint of best bid and offer at order submission time; slippage = signed difference between average execution price and arrival price.
    Aliases: arrival price algo, strike price algo

    Liquidity-seeking algorithm reviewed #

    A class of execution algorithm that hunts for available liquidity - displayed, reserve, and dark - across multiple venues, pouncing when contra-side interest appears rather than following a fixed time or volume schedule. It prioritizes finding size with minimal footprint over matching a benchmark.
    Intuition: It behaves like a forager: it sniffs across lit books and dark pools, and strikes when it finds someone willing to trade in size.
    Mechanics: The algo pings or sweeps venues with immediate-or-cancel child orders, rests hidden interest in dark pools, and uses randomized sizing/timing (e.g., accumulate/distribute or dark-ice style randomization) to avoid detection; smart order routing infrastructure directs the child orders to whichever venue currently offers liquidity.
    Failure modes: Pinging for liquidity leaks information to venues that see the probes, inviting predatory quoting; and because it trades opportunistically, execution quantity over any window is unpredictable - the order may barely trade in quiet markets.
    Misconceptions: Liquidity-seeking is not a guarantee of size discovery - dark liquidity is finite and shared by competing seekers. It is also a broker/platform product family rather than a single standardized algorithm, so behavior varies widely.
    Example: Tasked with selling 2 million shares without showing size, the algo rests hidden clips in two dark pools, sweeps the lit offer only when it thickens, and accumulates the position over the day with no single large print.
    Aliases: liquidity-seeking algo, liquidity aggregation algo, seeker

    Sweep order reviewed #

    An aggressively priced order that executes immediately through multiple price levels of one or more order books, 'sweeping' all available liquidity up to its limit price. It maximizes speed and executed quantity at the cost of paying successively worse prices down the book.
    Intuition: A sweep says 'take everything offered up to my price, right now' - depth consumed in one action instead of one level at a time.
    Mechanics: The order (typically a marketable limit or IOC) walks the book: it fills at the best price, then the next, until filled or the limit is reached; multi-venue versions route simultaneous child orders to several exchanges. Smart order routers implement sweeps with IOC child orders, re-routing when a target quote vanishes before arrival.
    Failure modes: Sweeping deep into a thin book produces large, immediate market impact and a poor average price; stale-quote races mean some child orders arrive after the liquidity is gone (partial fills). Visible sweeps also signal urgency to the whole market.
    Misconceptions: A sweep is not limited to the national best bid and offer - that is the whole point, and why it differs from a plain market order capped at top of book on protected venues. Nor is a sweep a distinct regulated order type everywhere; it is an execution pattern built from marketable limit/IOC orders.
    Example: With offers of 2,000 at $10.00, 3,000 at $10.02, and 5,000 at $10.05, a sweep buy for 7,000 at $10.05 fills all three levels for a $10.026 average.
    Average sweep price = sum(fill price_i x size_i) / total size, across all price levels consumed.
    Aliases: sweep, book sweep, sweep-to-fill

    Intermarket sweep order reviewed #

    A limit order for an NMS stock that is marked 'intermarket sweep order' and routed to a trading center while, simultaneously, additional orders are routed to execute against the full displayed size of every protected quotation priced better than the ISO's limit. Marking an order ISO exempts the receiving venue from Reg NMS trade-through protection for that execution.
    Intuition: ISO is the regulatory way to say 'I have already cleared every better-priced quote elsewhere - so you may execute mine even if another venue shows a better price.'
    Mechanics: The sender (usually a smart order router) fires IOC limit orders at all protected quotes superior to the ISO price at the same instant the ISO is routed; venues may then execute the ISO without checking those away quotes. Regulation NMS defines the term and Rule 611 provides the trade-through exception conditioned on this simultaneous sweeping.
    Failure modes: The sweep is only as good as its market data: if a protected quote updates between snapshot and routing, the 'simultaneous' clear is incomplete and executions can occur at prices that would otherwise be trade-throughs. ISO usage also shifts responsibility for best price to the sender.
    Misconceptions: An ISO is not a license to trade through quotes at will - the sender must genuinely route against the full displayed size of better-priced protected quotes. It is also a routing designation on a limit order, not a speed guarantee.
    Example: Exchange A shows a protected offer of 500 at $10.00; your router sends a 500-share ISO buy there and simultaneously a 1,000-share ISO buy at $10.01 to Exchange B, allowing B to execute immediately despite A's lower quote.

    Partial fill reviewed #

    An execution of only part of an order's quantity, leaving the remainder open (or canceled, depending on the order's time-in-force). Partial fills occur whenever available contra-side liquidity at acceptable prices is smaller than the order.
    Intuition: You asked for 1,000, the market gave you 300 - the rest is still working unless the order says otherwise.
    Mechanics: Venues report the executed portion with cumulative and remaining quantities; in FIX the order state becomes Partially Filled with CumQty < OrderQty and LeavesQty > 0, and the invariant OrderQty = CumQty + LeavesQty holds while the order is active. IOC orders cancel the remainder immediately; FOK orders never partially fill by construction.
    Failure modes: Partial fills leave sub-scale positions that distort strategy economics (fixed fees spread over fewer shares) and can strand a hedged pair half-executed. In fast markets, repeated small partials also accumulate impact without achieving the intended size.
    Misconceptions: A partially filled order is not done - unless its time-in-force says otherwise, the balance keeps working and can still execute. Nor can an FOK order partial-fill; 'partial' presumes the order type permits it.
    Example: Your limit order to buy 1,000 shares at $25 catches only 400 before the offer lifts; the execution report shows CumQty 400, LeavesQty 600, and the 600-share balance keeps working at $25.
    LeavesQty = OrderQty - CumQty (FIX quantity invariant for an active order).
    Aliases: partial execution

    Full fill reviewed #

    An execution (or series of executions) that completes the entire quantity of an order, leaving nothing open. Once fully filled, the order is terminal - it can no longer be canceled or modified.
    Intuition: The order got every share it asked for; nothing remains in the market.
    Mechanics: The venue reports a final execution bringing cumulative quantity to the full order quantity; in FIX this is OrdStatus = Filled ('order completely filled, no remaining quantity') with LeavesQty = 0. A full fill may result from one execution or the accumulation of many partial fills.
    Failure modes: A full fill at a stale price can itself be the failure - e.g., a resting order that completes only because the market blew through its level (adverse selection in disguise). Confirmed full fills also cannot be unwound except by error-correction procedures.
    Misconceptions: Full fill says nothing about price quality - an order can be completely filled at several progressively worse prices. And 'filled' does not mean settled; clearing and settlement follow execution.
    Example: Your 5,000-share buy at $30 executes in three partials of 2,000, 2,000, and 1,000; the last report shows CumQty 5,000, LeavesQty 0, OrdStatus Filled.
    Full fill condition: CumQty = OrderQty and LeavesQty = 0.
    Aliases: complete fill, filled order

    Order cancellation reviewed #

    The withdrawal of an order's unexecuted balance from the market before it fills, on the trader's instruction or automatically (expiry, disconnect, venue purge). Cancellation applies only to the remaining open quantity - executions already reported stand.
    Intuition: Canceling pulls your resting order off the table; you can only take back what has not yet traded.
    Mechanics: The client sends a cancel request identifying the order (FIX Order Cancel Request; exchanges expose equivalent messages, e.g., Cboe Cancel Order keyed by ClOrdID); the venue acknowledges with a pending state and confirms with a Canceled status showing final CumQty/LeavesQty. A 'pending cancel' acknowledgment explicitly does not mean the order is canceled yet - fills can still arrive in the gap.
    Failure modes: The cancel/fill race: a cancel request that arrives microseconds after an execution is too late, and the trader owns the fill. Mass-cancel storms during volatility can also overwhelm gateways, delaying cancels precisely when they are most wanted.
    Misconceptions: Requesting a cancel is not the same as being canceled - only the venue's confirmation (Canceled status) ends the order. Nor does canceling erase fills: executed quantity remains executed.
    Example: You cancel a resting bid for 800 of an original 1,000 shares (200 already filled); the confirmation reports OrdStatus Canceled with CumQty 200 and LeavesQty 0.
    Aliases: cancel, order cancel request

    Order modification reviewed #

    A change to the terms of a live order - price, quantity, or other parameters - effected as a cancel/replace request that the venue treats as an amendment to the existing order. Most venues re-queue the order (loss of time priority) when price changes or quantity increases, but preserve priority for quantity decreases.
    Intuition: Modifying is editing a live order in place - but the market treats a better price or bigger size as if you just arrived at the back of the line.
    Mechanics: The client sends a cancel/replace (Cboe Modify Order) referencing the order's current ClOrdID; the venue confirms via Pending Replace then Replaced states. Cboe's rules are typical: price, side, quantity, stop price, display quantity, and order type may be adjusted, and time priority is lost unless the change only reduces quantity or adjusts non-price fields.
    Failure modes: Modify requests that cross in flight with executions can yield unexpected positions - exchanges therefore apply quantity deltas to the remaining size rather than blindly replacing it. Losing queue priority on reprice can also mean the order never fills where the original would have.
    Misconceptions: A modification is not guaranteed to keep the order's place in the queue - raising quantity or changing price typically moves it to the back. Nor is a modify request effective on submission; only the venue's Replace confirmation makes it so.
    Example: You improve a resting bid from $24.50 to $24.60; the venue cancels the $24.50 order and replaces it at $24.60 with new time priority behind everyone already bidding $24.60.
    Aliases: order amend, cancel/replace, order replace

    Order rejection reviewed #

    The refusal of an order by the broker, exchange, or counterparty before or after acceptance, so that the order never becomes (or ceases to be) executable. Rejections carry a reason - invalid parameters, risk limits, insufficient margin, closed market, or duplicate identifiers.
    Intuition: A rejection is the market saying 'no' at the door: nothing was executed and nothing rests - you must fix the problem and resubmit.
    Mechanics: The venue returns a reject message with a reason code (in FIX, an Execution Report with ExecType/OrdStatus = Rejected plus an OrdRejReason field; Cboe rejects for duplicates, orders outside trading hours, or stale NBBO conditions). An order can even be rejected after an earlier acknowledgment - status can pass from New to Rejected.
    Failure modes: Silent or mis-parsed rejects leave strategies believing they have live orders when they do not - a classic cause of unhedged exposure in automated systems. Rejection cascades during volatility (risk limits tripping) can also block risk-reducing orders.
    Misconceptions: A rejection is not a partial fill or a failed execution - no quantity traded. And an acknowledgment does not immunize an order from later rejection (e.g., on regulatory or risk grounds).
    Example: You resend an order reusing a live ClOrdID; the exchange rejects it as a duplicate, and your system must generate a fresh identifier before the order can enter the book.

    Order acknowledgment reviewed #

    The venue or broker's confirmation that an order has been received and accepted into the trading system, establishing the order as live with its identifiers and initial state. An acknowledgment is a statement about receipt and acceptance, not about execution.
    Intuition: The ack is the market's receipt: 'we have your order and it is now working' - nothing has traded yet.
    Mechanics: On acceptance the exchange returns an acknowledgment message carrying the assigned order IDs and the order's state (in FIX, an Execution Report with OrdStatus = New; on Cboe BOE, an Order Acknowledgment message). Subsequent cancels, modifies, and fills all reference the acknowledged identifier chain.
    Failure modes: Treating a missing ack as a missing order - when the ack was merely delayed - leads to duplicate submissions and double positions. Conversely, assuming an ack implies a fill is an accounting error.
    Misconceptions: An acknowledgment does not mean the order executed, nor even that it is displayed (hidden and held conditional orders are acked too). It also does not guarantee the order can't subsequently be rejected.
    Example: You submit a limit buy; 200 microseconds later the exchange returns an Order Acknowledgment with OrderID and status New - the order is now in the book, unfilled.

    Execution report reviewed #

    The standard electronic message by which a broker or exchange communicates everything that happens to an order: acknowledgment of receipt, status changes, partial and full fills, cancellations, replacements, and rejections. In the FIX protocol it is the Execution Report message (MsgType = 8).
    Intuition: It is the order's paper trail in real time - every event in an order's life shows up as an execution report.
    Mechanics: Each report carries three state fields - OrdStatus (current state of the order), ExecType (what this message is reporting), and the transaction type - plus cumulative quantity, remaining quantity (LeavesQty), and average price (AvgPx) for the whole order chain. Reports are sent per fill and per state change and do not replace the end-of-day confirm.
    Failure modes: Dropped or reordered reports (sequence gaps, disconnects) leave the buy side with a wrong picture of position and open quantity, which is why session replay and end-of-day reconciliation exist. Execution reports are also point-in-time: relying on them without the final confirm invites booking errors.
    Misconceptions: An execution report is not only a fill notice - it is also how acks, cancels, and rejects are delivered. And per FIX convention it does not replace the end-of-day trade confirmation.
    Example: Your 1,000-share order generates three execution reports: New (ack), Partially Filled 400, and Filled with AvgPx 25.02 - together they fully reconstruct the order's life.
    Aliases: fill report, drop copy, FIX Execution Report

    Average execution price reviewed #

    The quantity-weighted average price at which an order's fills executed: total value traded divided by total quantity filled for that order. It is the price actually achieved across what may be many partial executions at different levels.
    Intuition: If your 1,000 shares came in five pieces at five prices, the average execution price is the single number that correctly prices the whole position.
    Mechanics: The venue accumulates price-times-quantity across all fills of the order chain and reports it as a running field (FIX AvgPx, tag 6) that updates with each partial fill; for multi-day good-till orders it spans all days' executions. Slippage and transaction-cost metrics are computed against this average.
    Failure modes: Averaging hides the distribution: two orders with the same AvgPx can have very different market-impact profiles. Booking systems that mix up per-day and cumulative averages on multi-day orders produce wrong cost basis.
    Misconceptions: Average execution price is not the market VWAP - it is weighted by your own fills only, over your order's lifetime. Nor is it necessarily the price of any single actual print.
    Example: You buy 400 shares at $25.00 and 600 at $25.10; the average execution price is (400x25.00 + 600x25.10)/1,000 = $25.06.
    AvgPx = sum(fill price_i x fill qty_i) / sum(fill qty_i) = CumValue / CumQty.
    Aliases: average fill price, AvgPx
